## Page 4 (Slide 3) — Coulomb's Law (1784)

**Coulomb's Law (1784)**

**Charles-Augustin de Coulomb** (France, 1736–1806)

- Born into a wealthy noble family; attended university and military academy, then served as a military engineer
- After retiring from the army, devoted himself to applied mechanics including elasticity of wires and the law of torsion
- **1784–1791**: Published important results on electricity and magnetism; announced **Coulomb's Law** describing the force between electric charges
- The coulomb (C) was adopted as the SI unit of electric charge
- His work survived the turmoil of the French Revolution (1789–1799)

---

## Page 5 (Slide 4) — Gauss's Law (1813)

**Gauss's Law (1813)**

**Carl Friedrich Gauss** (Germany, 1777–1855)

- Father was a poor bricklayer; recognized as a mathematical prodigy from childhood; the Duke of Brunswick sponsored his education
- Regarded across all eras as one of the greatest mathematicians
- Made enormous contributions to number theory, least squares, real analysis, non-Euclidean geometry, differential geometry, electromagnetism, and optics
- Held a position at the university that was the center of German mathematics and physics
- After his death, his brain was preserved and studied for signs of genius

---

## Page 6 (Slide 5) — Ørsted's Discovery (1820)

**Ørsted's Discovery (1820)**

**Hans Christian Ørsted** (Denmark, 1777–1851)

- Influenced by his father who ran a pharmacy; studied pharmacy at university
- From the early 1800s, when Volta's battery appeared, investigated the effect of electricity on chemistry; held the view that natural phenomena are unified by a single principle
- Returned to Denmark and was appointed professor at age 29
- At age 42, while conducting an experiment at the University of Copenhagen, **first observed that a compass needle deflects when electric current flows through a nearby wire**

---

## Page 7 (Slide 6) — Ampère's Law (1820)

**Ampère's Law (1820)**

**André-Marie Ampère** (France, 1775–1836)

- Born into a merchant family; showed exceptional talent in mathematics, philosophy, and natural sciences
- After his father was executed by the Jacobins following the French Revolution, fell into a prolonged depression
- In 1809 became professor at École Polytechnique; conducted research in electromagnetism for decades
- In 1820 announced **Ampère's Law** — the law describing the magnetic force acting on current-carrying conductors
- The ampere (A) became the SI unit of electric current
- Remembered as one of France's most celebrated scientists

---

## Page 8 (Slide 7) — Biot-Savart Law (1820) — Jean-Baptiste Biot

**Biot-Savart Law (1820)**

**Jean-Baptiste Biot** (France, 1774–1862)

- Studied at École Polytechnique; became mathematics professor at 23, professor at École Polytechnique at 26, and professor of physics at the University of Paris at 29
- In 1804, ascended by balloon to study atmospheric phenomena; in 1806, participated in measuring a meridian arc in Spain
- Conducted extensive research in optics; in 1820, together with Savart, announced the **Biot-Savart Law**

---

## Page 9 (Slide 8) — Biot-Savart Law (1820) — Félix Savart

**Biot-Savart Law (1820)**

**Félix Savart** (France, 1791–1841)

- Born into a family of military engineers; studied medicine and served as a military surgeon in Napoleon's army; also studied physics as a physician
- More talented in theory than experiment; actively conducted research on acoustics
- Jointly with Biot, in 1820 published the **Biot-Savart Law** describing the magnetic field produced by electric current in a wire

---

## Page 10 (Slide 9) — Ohm's Law (1827)

**Ohm's Law (1827)**

**Georg Simon Ohm** (Germany, 1789–1854)

- Inspired by his education-minded father; studied mathematics at university
- Continued research in mathematics and physics while working as a high school teacher
- Influenced by Ørsted's discovery; found that conductors have resistance to current flow → **discovered Ohm's Law**
- Published in Germany but widely rejected by German academia; received the Copley Medal in 1841; finally appointed professor of experimental physics at the University of Munich in 1852
- The ohm (Ω) was adopted as the SI unit of electric resistance

---

## Page 11 (Slide 10) — Faraday's Law (1831)

**Faraday's Law (1831)**

**Michael Faraday** (England, 1791–1867)

- Born in a poor district of London; received no formal education; started work at age 13 as a bookshop assistant
- Attended lectures by chemist Humphry Davy; fascinated by chemistry and the natural sciences; was taken on as Davy's assistant
- Became the greatest scientist of his era; recognized as the leading figure in physics, chemistry, and electrical engineering
- Influenced by Ørsted's discovery; **discovered electromagnetic induction**

## Page 18 (Slide 17) — Electric and Magnetic Phenomena

**Electric and Magnetic Phenomena**

**Belief until the end of the 18th century:**
- Charges create electric fields; only charges experience force in electric fields.
- Magnets create magnetic fields; only magnets experience force in magnetic fields.
- → **Electric and magnetic phenomena have no relation to each other.**

**Ørsted's Discovery in 1820:**
- Moving charges create magnetic fields; moving charges experience force in magnetic fields.
- → **There must be some relationship between electric and magnetic phenomena.**

---

**Faraday's question in 1830:**

|              |                                                                                                                        |

| **Ørsted**   | Moving **charges** create **magnetic fields**, and **moving charges** in magnetic fields experience force.             |
| **Symmetry** | Does moving a **magnet** create an **electric field**, and does a moving magnet in an electric field experience force? |

## Page 20 (Slide 19) — Faraday's Conjecture (continued)

**Faraday's Conjecture**

- **Ørsted**: Moving charges create a magnetic field; moving charges in a magnetic field experience force.
- **Symmetry**: Does a moving magnet create an electric field; does a moving magnet in an electric field experience force?

**Faraday's problem:**
- Confirming that a magnetic field is created → easy using a compass

## Page 26 (Slide 25) — Faraday's Experimental Result

**Faraday's Experimental Result**

- Magnetic field **B** near a closed circuit
- Magnetic flux **Φ_B** through the closed circuit
- Induced EMF **ε** in the closed circuit

Faraday identified the **direct cause** of the induced EMF ε:

→ **When the magnetic flux Φ_B through the closed circuit changes**

---

## Page 27 (Slide 26) — Faraday's Law

**Faraday's Law**

$$\varepsilon = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}$$

- The **rate of change of magnetic flux** through a closed circuit equals the induced EMF
- The minus sign on the RHS means the induced EMF is generated **in the direction that opposes the change**
- The minus sign alone on the RHS is called **Lenz's Law**
- EMF is induced **by any means** that changes the magnetic flux through the closed circuit

---

## Page 28 (Slide 27) — Faraday's Law: Dimensional Check

**Faraday's Law — Dimensional Check**

$$\varepsilon = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} \quad \text{What is the dimension of the RHS?}$$

$$\vec{F}_m = q\vec{v} \times \vec{B}, \qquad \Phi_B = \vec{B} \cdot \vec{S}$$

$$\left[\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}\right] = \left[\frac{BS}{t}\right] = \left[\frac{F\,l^2}{qv\,t}\right] = \left[\frac{Fl}{q}\right] = \left[\frac{E}{q}\right] = [\varepsilon] \checkmark$$

→ **No dimensional constant is needed on the RHS of Faraday's Law!**

---

## Page 29 (Slide 28) — Ways Magnetic Flux Can Change

**Ways Magnetic Flux Can Change**

**Faraday's Law:** $\varepsilon = -\dfrac{d\Phi_B}{dt}$

**Magnetic flux:** $\Phi_B = \displaystyle\int_S \vec{B} \cdot d\vec{S}$ (S = area enclosed by circuit C)

Ways the magnetic flux **Φ_B** through a closed circuit can change:

1. The **magnitude of B** through the circuit changes
2. The **shape of the circuit** changes
3. The **angle** between the circuit plane and B changes

---

## Page 30 (Slide 29) — Electrostatic Field vs. Induced Electric Field

**Electrostatic Field and Induced Electric Field**

**Electrostatic field** → electric field around charges
- Line integral over a closed curve is always **zero**: $\oint_C \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{r} = 0$
- Scalar potential can be defined: $\phi(\vec{r}) = -\displaystyle\int_{\vec{r}_s}^{\vec{r}} \vec{E}(\vec{r}')\cdot d\vec{r}'$

**Induced electric field** → electric field around a changing magnetic field
- Line integral over a closed curve is **not zero**: $\oint_C \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{r} \ne 0$
- The line integral equals the **induced EMF**: $\varepsilon = \oint_C \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{r}$

---

## Page 31 (Slide 30) — Integral Form of Faraday's Law

**Integral Form of Faraday's Law**

Magnetic field **B(t)**; magnetic flux through circuit: $\Phi_B(t) = \displaystyle\int_S \vec{B}(t)\cdot d\vec{S}$

Induced EMF: $\varepsilon = \oint_C \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{r}$

**Faraday's Law:**
$$\varepsilon = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}$$

**Integral form:**
$$\oint_C \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{r} = -\frac{d}{dt}\!\left(\int_S \vec{B}(t)\cdot d\vec{S}\right)$$

---

## Page 32 (Slide 31) — Fundamental Laws of Electricity and Magnetism

**Fundamental Laws of Electric and Magnetic Phenomena**

| Law | Scalar form | Integral form |
|-----|-------------|---------------|
| **Gauss's Law for E** | $\Phi_E = q/\varepsilon_0$ | $\oint_S \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{S} = \dfrac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\int_V \rho(\vec{r})\,dv$ |
| **Gauss's Law for B** | $\Phi_B = 0$ | $\oint_S \vec{B}\cdot d\vec{S} = 0$ |
| **Ampère's Law** | $\Gamma_B = \mu_0 i$ | $\oint_C \vec{B}\cdot d\vec{r} = \mu_0\int_S \vec{J}\cdot d\vec{S}$ |
| **Faraday's Law** | $\varepsilon = -d\Phi_B/dt$ | $\oint_C \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{r} = -\dfrac{d}{dt}\int_S \vec{B}\cdot d\vec{S}$ |

## Page 35 (Slide 34) — Lenz's Law (1833)

**Lenz's Law (1833)**

**Heinrich Friedrich Emil Lenz** (Russia, 1804–1865)

- German-born Russian physicist; served as professor at St. Petersburg, making major contributions to the study of electricity
- Published a paper on the **direction** of induced EMF
- Around the same time as Faraday, independently discovered that electromagnetic induction occurs; Faraday had found the result but not the directional law
- Also independently discovered the law governing heat generated by current through a resistance (the same as Joule's Law) → this directional principle is called **Lenz's Law**

---

## Page 36 (Slide 35) — Faraday's Law and Lenz's Law

**Faraday's Law and Lenz's Law**

$$\varepsilon = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}$$

The **minus sign** on the RHS of Faraday's Law:

1. Points in the direction **opposing the change**
2. Is **required for energy conservation**

The minus sign alone is called **Lenz's Law**.

## Page 37 (Slide 36) — Lenz's Law Exercise 1

**Lenz's Law Exercise 1**

*When the bar magnet (N pole facing right) moves to the right toward the loop, what is the direction of induced current?*

- The magnetic flux through the loop **increases**
- Induced current flows so that the magnetic field it creates inside the loop **cancels** the increase → field lines from induced current oppose those of the magnet
- The loop becomes a **south pole** (facing the magnet's north pole), opposing the approaching magnet

---

## Page 38 (Slide 37) — Lenz's Law Exercise 1 (continued)

**Lenz's Law Exercise 1 (continued)**

*When the bar magnet moves to the **left** (away from the loop):*

- The magnetic flux through the loop **decreases**
- Induced current flows so that the field lines created inside the loop **compensate** for the decrease
- The loop becomes a **north pole** (following the retreating north pole of the magnet), opposing the departure

---

## Page 39 (Slide 38) — Lenz's Law Exercise 2

**Lenz's Law Exercise 2**

*A circular circuit sits in a uniform magnetic field whose strength is gradually **increasing**. What is the direction of induced current?*

- Induced current flows so that the magnetic field it creates inside the loop is in the **opposite direction** to the original field
- Using the right-hand rule: turn a right-handed screw in the direction of current flow; the induced magnetic field points in the direction the screw advances

**Lenz's Law Exercise 2 (continued)**

## Page 45 (Slide 44) — Induced EMF: Rectangular Circuit in Changing Uniform Field

**Induced EMF in a Rectangular Circuit in a Changing Uniform Magnetic Field**

> A square closed circuit with side length **5 cm** is placed in a uniform magnetic field. The field changes uniformly from **10 T to −10 T** in **0.1 s**. The circuit resistance is **100 Ω**. Find the induced EMF and current.

- Magnetic flux: $\Phi_B = BS$
- Applying Faraday's Law $\varepsilon = -d\Phi_B/dt$:

$$\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} = \frac{\Delta\Phi_B}{\Delta t} = \frac{(B_2-B_1)S}{\Delta t} = \frac{(-10-10)\times(0.05)^2}{0.1} = -0.5 \text{ V}$$

$$\varepsilon = \left|\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}\right| = \boxed{0.5 \text{ V}}$$

---

## Page 46 (Slide 45) — Induced EMF: Rectangular Circuit (continued)

**Induced current:**

$$i = \frac{\varepsilon}{R} = \frac{0.5}{100} = 0.005 \text{ A} = \boxed{5 \text{ mA}}$$

**Direction of induced current** → apply Lenz's Law:
- The magnetic flux through the circuit **decreases** (B goes from +10 to −10)
- Induced current creates a field inside the circuit in the direction to **oppose** the decrease

---

## Page 47 (Slide 46) — Motional EMF: Conductor Moving in Magnetic Field

**Motional EMF — Right Conductor of a C-shaped Circuit Moving in a Magnetic Field**

> As shown, a rod of length **L** moves at constant velocity **v** to the right in a uniform magnetic field **B** perpendicular to a C-shaped circuit. (a) Find the induced EMF. (b) Find the direction of induced current.

**(a)** Let x = position of rod at time t (x = vt):

$$\Phi_B = B \cdot S = B \cdot L \cdot x = BLvt$$

$$\varepsilon = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} = -BLv \implies \text{Induced EMF} = \boxed{BLv}$$

---

## Page 48 (Slide 47) — Motional EMF (continued)

**(b) Direction of induced current** → Lenz's Law:

- The magnetic flux through the circuit **increases**
- Induced current creates a field inside the circuit **opposing** the original field
- Current exits from the bottom of the moving rod (using right-hand rule)

## Page 57 (Slide 56) — Inductance of a Solenoid

**Inductance of a Solenoid**

| Step | Parallel-plate Capacitor | Solenoid |
|------|--------------------------|---------|

| 3 | Potential difference $\Delta\phi = Ed = \dfrac{qd}{\varepsilon_0 S}$ | Flux through solenoid $\Phi_B = BSN = \mu_0 niSN = \mu_0 n^2 lSi$ |

**Inductance of a Solenoid (continued)**

## Page 59 (Slide 58) — Inductance of a Toroid

**Inductance of a Toroid**

Toroid with radius **r**, cross-sectional area **A**, **N** turns. Condition: $\sqrt{A} \ll r$

| Step | |
|------|--|
| 1 | Assume current i flows through the toroid |
| 2 | Internal magnetic field: $B(r) = \dfrac{\mu_0 Ni}{2\pi r}$ |
| 3 | Total magnetic flux: $\Phi_B = N\displaystyle\int \vec{B}(\vec{r})\cdot d\vec{S} = \dfrac{\mu_0 N^2 Ai}{2\pi r}$ |
| 4 | Inductance: $L = \dfrac{\Phi_B}{i} = \dfrac{\mu_0 N^2 A}{2\pi r}$ |

> Inductance is determined solely by the **geometric structure** of the inductor.

## Page 63 (Slide 62) — Self-Induction

**Self-Induction**

When the current through an inductor **changes** → **self-induction**

Self-induced EMF:
$$\varepsilon_L = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}$$

Magnetic flux through inductor: $\Phi_B = Li$

If the inductor shape does not change → L is constant:

$$\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} = \frac{d(Li)}{dt} = L\frac{di}{dt}$$

$$\boxed{\varepsilon_L = -L\frac{di}{dt}}$$

## Page 64 (Slide 63) — Circuit Elements and Potential Difference

**Circuit Elements and Potential Difference** (cross-reference: 17E)

- In an electric circuit, the current in the same branch is the same everywhere.
- Parts directly connected have the same potential.
- Potential changes when passing through a circuit element.

| Element | Potential change |
|---------|-----------------|
| Resistor R | $\phi_b = \phi_a - iR$ |
| Capacitor C | $\phi_b = \phi_a - q/C$ |
| Inductor L | $\phi_b = \phi_a - L\,di/dt$ |
| EMF source ε (−→+) | $\phi_b = \phi_a + \varepsilon$ |
| EMF source ε (+→−) | $\phi_b = \phi_a - \varepsilon$ |

> φ_b can be less than, equal to, or greater than φ_a.

## Page 69 (Slide 69) — Mutual Induction

**Mutual Induction**

When the current in one inductor **changes**, an EMF is induced in an **adjacent** inductor → **Mutual Induction**

- Current i₁ flows in the first inductor; it creates magnetic flux Φ₂₁ through the second inductor
- $\Phi_{21} = M_{21} i_1$ (M₂₁ = mutual inductance, constant if shapes unchanged)

Self-induced EMF in the second inductor:
$$\varepsilon_2 = -\frac{d\Phi_{21}}{dt} \implies \boxed{\varepsilon_2 = -M_{21}\frac{di_1}{dt}}$$

---

## Page 70 (Slide 70) — Mutual Induction (continued)

**Mutual Induction (continued)**

Current i₂ flows in the second inductor; it creates magnetic flux Φ₁₂ through the first inductor:
$$\Phi_{12} = M_{12} i_2$$

Self-induced EMF in the first inductor:
$$\varepsilon_1 = -\frac{d\Phi_{12}}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}(M_{12}i_2) = M_{12}\frac{di_2}{dt} \implies \varepsilon_1 = -M_{12}\frac{di_2}{dt}$$

$$\boxed{M_{12} = M_{21}}$$

**Self-induction:**
$$\varepsilon_L = -L\frac{di}{dt}$$

**Mutual induction:**
$$\varepsilon_2 = -M_{21}\frac{di_1}{dt}, \quad \varepsilon_1 = -M_{12}\frac{di_2}{dt}, \quad M_{12} = M_{21}$$

$$\boxed{L_{\text{series}} = L_1 + L_2}$$

---

## Page 75 (Slide 75) — Two Inductors in Parallel

**Two Inductors in Parallel**

Current law: $i = i_1 + i_2$

Voltage law: $\varepsilon = L_1\,di_1/dt = L_2\,di_2/dt$

$$\frac{di}{dt} = \frac{di_1}{dt} + \frac{di_2}{dt} = \frac{\varepsilon}{L_1} + \frac{\varepsilon}{L_2} = \varepsilon\!\left(\frac{1}{L_1}+\frac{1}{L_2}\right)$$

$$\boxed{\frac{1}{L_{\text{parallel}}} = \frac{1}{L_1}+\frac{1}{L_2}} \implies L_{\text{parallel}} = \frac{L_1 L_2}{L_1+L_2}$$

**Internal Resistance of a Battery**

## Page 84 (Slide 84) — RL Circuit

**RL Circuit**

A circuit connecting an **EMF source, resistance, and inductor** in series is called an **RL circuit**.

- When an inductor is first connected in a DC circuit, **no current flows initially**
- After a long time, the current equals **ε/R** (as if the inductor were absent)

---

## Page 85 (Slide 85) — RL Circuit (continued)

**RL Circuit Behavior**

- Immediately after the switch is closed: the inductor's self-induced EMF **prevents current from flowing**
- After a long time: constant current flows, and the inductor **acts as a plain wire**
- Kirchhoff's Law holds **instantaneously** even while current is changing

---

## Page 86 (Slide 86) — Kirchhoff's Voltage Law for RL Circuit

**Kirchhoff's Voltage Law**

| RC Circuit | RL Circuit |
|---|---|
| $\varepsilon - iR - q/C = 0$ | $\varepsilon - iR - L\,di/dt = 0$ |
| $\varepsilon = R\,dq/dt + q/C$ | $\varepsilon = L\,di/dt + Ri$ |
| Goal: find q(t) | Goal: find i(t) |

Both equations are in **integrable form**.

---

## Page 87 (Slide 87) — Integrable Form

**Integrable Form**

Both differential equations can be rearranged into integrable form:

$$\int \frac{dq}{\varepsilon C - q} = \int \frac{dt}{RC} \qquad \int \frac{di}{\varepsilon/R - i} = \int \frac{R\,dt}{L}$$

Integration limits are determined by initial conditions.

---

## Page 88 (Slide 88) — Current in RL Circuit

**Solutions**

For the RC circuit with $q(0)=0$:
$$q(t) = \varepsilon C\!\left(1 - e^{-t/RC}\right)$$

For the RL circuit with $i(0)=0$:
$$\boxed{i(t) = \frac{\varepsilon}{R}\!\left(1 - e^{-Rt/L}\right)}$$

---

## Page 89 (Slide 89) — Current and Induced EMF in RL Circuit

**Current and Induced EMF**

$$i(0) = 0, \qquad i(\infty) = \frac{\varepsilon}{R}$$

$$|\varepsilon_L(t)| = \left|-L\frac{di}{dt}\right| = \varepsilon\, e^{-Rt/L}$$

$$|\varepsilon_L(0)| = \varepsilon, \qquad |\varepsilon_L(\infty)| = 0$$

---

# 19J  LC Oscillation

## Page 103 (Slide 103) — LC Circuit and LC Oscillation

**LC Circuit and LC Oscillation**

A circuit connecting a **capacitor and an inductor** in series is called an **LC circuit**.

- Connect switch to position **a** → capacitor charges
- Connect switch to position **b** → **LC oscillation** begins
- The Kirchhoff voltage law equation for LC oscillation has the **same form** as the equation of motion for a mass on a spring

---

## Page 104 (Slide 104) — Kirchhoff's Voltage Law for LC Circuit

**Kirchhoff's Voltage Law**

$$-L\frac{di}{dt} - \frac{q}{C} = 0, \qquad i = \frac{dq}{dt}$$

$$\boxed{L\frac{d^2q}{dt^2} + \frac{q}{C} = 0}$$

Second-order differential equation; goal: find q(t)

---

## Page 105 (Slide 105) — Mechanical Oscillation vs. LC Oscillation

**Comparison: Elastic Force Oscillation and LC Oscillation**

| | **Mechanical Oscillation** | **LC Oscillation** |
|--|--|--|
| Equation of motion | $-kx = m\dfrac{d^2x}{dt^2}$ | $L\dfrac{d^2q}{dt^2} + \dfrac{q}{C} = 0$ |
| Standard form | $\dfrac{d^2x}{dt^2} + \omega^2 x = 0$ | $\dfrac{d^2q}{dt^2} + \omega^2 q = 0$ |
| Angular frequency | $\omega^2 = \dfrac{k}{m}$ | $\omega^2 = \dfrac{1}{LC}$ |

---

## Page 106 (Slide 106) — General Solution of the 2nd-Order ODE

**General Solution** (constant-coefficient linear 2nd-order ODE)

| | Mechanical | LC |
|--|--|--|
| Solution | $x(t) = A\cos\omega t + B\sin\omega t$ | $q(t) = A\cos\omega t + B\sin\omega t$ |
| Derivative | $v(t) = -A\omega\sin\omega t + B\omega\cos\omega t$ | $i(t) = -A\omega\sin\omega t + B\omega\cos\omega t$ |

**Initial conditions** at $t=0$: $x(0)=x_0,\ v(0)=0$ → $q(0)=q_0,\ i(0)=0$

$$x(t) = x_0\cos\omega t, \quad v(t) = -x_0\omega\sin\omega t$$

$$q(t) = q_0\cos\omega t, \quad i(t) = -q_0\omega\sin\omega t$$

---

## Page 107 (Slide 107) — Mechanical Oscillation: Energy

**Mechanical Oscillation**

$$x(t) = x_0\cos\omega t, \qquad v(t) = \frac{dx}{dt} = -x_0\omega\sin\omega t$$

$$\text{Potential energy: } V(t) = \frac{1}{2}kx^2 = \frac{1}{2}kx_0^2\cos^2\omega t \quad \left(\omega^2 = \frac{k}{m}\right)$$

$$\text{Kinetic energy: } K(t) = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 = \frac{1}{2}kx_0^2\sin^2\omega t$$

$$V(t) + K(t) = \frac{1}{2}kx_0^2 = \text{const}$$

---

## Page 108 (Slide 108) — Electrical Oscillation: Energy

**Electrical Oscillation (LC Circuit)**

$$q(t) = q_0\cos\omega t, \qquad i(t) = \frac{dq}{dt} = -q_0\omega\sin\omega t$$

$$\text{Electric energy: } U_E(t) = \frac{q^2}{2C} = \frac{q_0^2}{2C}\cos^2\omega t \quad \left(\omega^2 = \frac{1}{LC}\right)$$

$$\text{Magnetic energy: } U_B(t) = \frac{1}{2}Li^2 = \frac{q_0^2}{2C}\sin^2\omega t$$

$$U_E(t) + U_B(t) = \frac{q_0^2}{2C} = \text{const}$$

---

## Page 109 (Slide 109) — Mechanical Damped Oscillation

**Mechanical Damped Oscillation**

Forces acting on the object:
- Elastic force: $F_k = -kx$
- Friction (damping): $f = -bv = -b\,dx/dt$

Equation of motion:

$$F = m\frac{d^2x}{dt^2} \implies -kx - b\frac{dx}{dt} = m\frac{d^2x}{dt^2}$$

---

## Page 110 (Slide 110) — Electromagnetic Damped Oscillation: RLC Circuit

**Electromagnetic Damped Oscillation ↔ RLC Circuit**

| | Mechanical Damped | RLC Circuit |
|--|--|--|
| Equation | $\dfrac{d^2x}{dt^2} + 2\gamma\dfrac{dx}{dt} + \omega^2 x = 0$ | $\dfrac{d^2q}{dt^2} + 2\gamma\dfrac{dq}{dt} + \omega^2 q = 0$ |
| Parameters | $\gamma = \dfrac{b}{2m},\ \omega^2 = \dfrac{k}{m}$ | $\gamma = \dfrac{R}{2L},\ \omega^2 = \dfrac{1}{LC}$ |

Kirchhoff's voltage law (RLC):
$$-\frac{q}{C} - Ri - L\frac{di}{dt} = 0, \qquad i = \frac{dq}{dt}$$

---

## Page 111 (Slide 111) — Three Types of Damped Oscillation

**Three Types of Damped Oscillation**

Mechanical: $\gamma = b/(2m),\ \omega^2 = k/m$ · RLC: $\gamma = R/(2L),\ \omega^2 = 1/(LC)$

**General Solution:**

$$x(t) = Ae^{\alpha_+ t} + Be^{\alpha_- t}, \qquad q(t) = Ae^{\alpha_+ t} + Be^{\alpha_- t}$$

$$\alpha_\pm = -\gamma \pm \sqrt{\gamma^2 - \omega^2}$$

| Type | Condition |
|------|-----------|
| **Underdamped** | $\omega > \gamma$ |
| **Critically damped** | $\omega = \gamma$ |
| **Overdamped** | $\omega < \gamma$ |

---

**Three Types of Damped Oscillation**

*(Graph showing amplitude vs. time for all three types)*

- **Critically damped oscillation stops fastest**
- Underdamped: oscillates while decaying
- Overdamped: returns to equilibrium slowly without oscillating
