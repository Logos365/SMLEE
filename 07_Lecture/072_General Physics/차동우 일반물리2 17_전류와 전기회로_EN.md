## Page 7 (Slide 7) — Definition of Electric Current

**Definition of Electric Current**

*(Diagram: charges q moving through cross-section S of a wire)*

If the charge passing through the cross-section $S$ of a wire during a time interval $dt$ is $dq$, then the electric current $i$ flowing through the wire is defined as:

$$i = \frac{dq}{dt}$$

- As long as the wire does not branch or merge, the current flowing in the wire is the same at every part of the wire.
- **Unit of current** ⇨ C/s = **A (Ampere)**

---

## Page 8 (Slide 8) — Current Density

**Current Density**

*(Diagram: charges q flowing through cross-section S)*

If the current $i$ in a wire is uniformly distributed over a cross-section of area $S$, the **current density** $j$ is:

$$j = \frac{i}{S} \quad \Rightarrow \quad j = \frac{di}{dS}$$

If charges at position $\vec{r}$ with charge density $\rho(\vec{r})$ move with velocity $\vec{v}$, the current density $\vec{j}(\vec{r})$ of that portion is:

$$\vec{j}(\vec{r}) = \rho(\vec{r})\,\vec{v}$$

> **Current is a scalar; current density is a vector.**

---

## Page 9 (Slide 9) — Current Density and Current

**Current Density and Current**

- Current density vector: $\vec{j}(\vec{r})$
- Area vector element: $d\vec{S}$

Current $di$ through the area element $d\vec{S}$:

$$di = \vec{j}(\vec{r}) \cdot d\vec{S}$$

Current $i$ through surface $S$:

$$i = \int_S \vec{j}(\vec{r}) \cdot d\vec{S}$$

---

## Page 10 (Slide 10) — Microscopic Description of Current in Conductors

**Microscopic Description of Current in Conductors**

Suppose that current is carried by identical particles (charge carriers) each with charge $q$.

- Average velocity of a particle: $\vec{v}$
- Number density of particles (number per unit volume): $n$

Consider the particles passing through a small area element $d\vec{S} = dS\,\hat{S}$:

- Distance traveled perpendicular to the surface by particles in time $\delta t$:

$$\Rightarrow (\vec{v} \cdot \hat{S})\,\delta t$$

- Volume occupied by particles that pass through $d\vec{S}$ during $\delta t$:

$$\Rightarrow \delta V = (\vec{v} \cdot \hat{S})\,\delta t\, dS$$

---

## Page 11 (Slide 11) — Microscopic Description of Current in Conductors (continued)

**Microscopic Description of Current in Conductors**

$$\delta V = (\vec{v} \cdot \hat{S})\,\delta t\, dS$$

Charge contained in small volume $\delta V$:

$$\Rightarrow \delta q = qn\delta V = qn(\vec{v} \cdot \hat{S})\,\delta t\, dS = qn\vec{v} \cdot dS\hat{S}\,\delta t = qn\vec{v} \cdot d\vec{S}\,\delta t$$

Current through area element $d\vec{S} = dS\,\hat{S}$:

$$\Rightarrow di = \frac{\delta q}{\delta t} = qn\vec{v} \cdot d\vec{S}$$

Since $di = \vec{j}(\vec{r}) \cdot d\vec{S}$:

$$\Rightarrow \boxed{\vec{j}(\vec{r}) = qn\vec{v}}$$

---

## Page 25 (Slide 26) — Electrical Resistance

**Electrical Resistance**

*(Diagram: conductor connected to battery and ammeter)*

When a potential difference $\Delta\phi$ is created between the ends of a conductor using an EMF source (battery), a current $i$ flows through the conductor.

**Electrical resistance of the conductor:**

$$R = \frac{\Delta\phi}{i}$$

- The ratio between the potential difference $\Delta\phi$ and the current $i$ varies from conductor to conductor ⇨ this defines the **electrical resistance**.

$$\Rightarrow \Delta\phi = iR \quad \text{(familiar equation)}$$

## Page 27 (Slide 29) — Ohm's Law

**Ohm's Law**

$$\Delta\phi = iR$$

- This is the equation used to determine the resistance $R$ of a conductor by measuring the potential difference $\Delta\phi$ and the current $i$.

**Question:** If the potential difference $\Delta\phi$ applied to the same conductor is changed, does the resistance $R$ also change?

The German scientist **Georg Ohm** discovered that, for most conductors, as long as $\Delta\phi$ is not too large, the resistance $R$ remains constant even when $\Delta\phi$ is varied.

$$\Rightarrow \textbf{Ohm's Law}$$

---

*(Graph: current $i$ vs. potential difference $\Delta\phi$ — linear in the shaded region)*

- Measuring $i$ while varying $\Delta\phi$

$$\Rightarrow \text{Region where Ohm's Law is satisfied (linear region)}$$

## Page 29 (Slide 31) — Series Connection of Two Identical Conductors

**Series Connection of Two Identical Conductors**

*(Left: single conductor with current $i$ and potential difference $\Delta\phi$)*
*(Right: two identical conductors in series with current $i/2$ and potential difference $\Delta\phi$)*

- Single conductor: $R = \dfrac{\Delta\phi}{i}$

- Two conductors in series: $R' = \dfrac{\Delta\phi}{i/2} = 2R$

---

## Page 30 (Slide 32) — Parallel Connection of Two Identical Conductors

**Parallel Connection of Two Identical Conductors**

*(Left: single conductor, $i$, $\Delta\phi$)*
*(Right: two identical conductors in parallel, $2i$, $\Delta\phi$)*

- Single conductor: $R = \dfrac{\Delta\phi}{i}$

- Two conductors in parallel: $R' = \dfrac{\Delta\phi}{2i} = \dfrac{R}{2}$

---

## Page 31 (Slide 33) — Resistivity

**Resistivity**

From the two experiments above:

> **The resistance of a conductor is proportional to its length and inversely proportional to its cross-sectional area.**

$$\Rightarrow R = \eta\,\frac{l}{S}$$

where $\eta$ is the **proportionality constant** = **resistivity**

- **Resistance** is a property of the object.
- **Resistivity** is a property of the material.

**Unit of resistivity:**

$$R = \eta\,\frac{l}{S} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \eta = R\,\frac{S}{l} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \mathbf{\Omega\cdot m}$$

## Page 36 (Slide 38) — Electric Field inside a Current-Carrying Conductor

**Electric Field inside a Current-Carrying Conductor**

> The electric field inside a conductor carrying current is **not zero**.

*(Diagram: cylindrical conductor of length $l$, cross-sectional area $S$, with current $i$ and potential difference $\Delta\phi$)*

When a current $i$ flows through a cylindrical conductor with cross-sectional area $S$ and length $l$, and the potential difference between the two ends is $\Delta\phi$:

---

## Page 37 (Slide 39) — Electric Field inside a Current-Carrying Conductor (continued)

**Electric Field inside a Current-Carrying Conductor**

$$\text{Current density:} \quad J = \frac{i}{S} \quad \Rightarrow \quad i = JS$$

$$\text{Electric field:} \quad E = \frac{\Delta\phi}{l} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \Delta\phi = El$$

$$\text{Resistance:} \quad R = \frac{\Delta\phi}{i} = \frac{El}{JS}$$

$$\text{Electric field inside conductor:} \quad E = R\,\frac{S}{l}\,J = \eta J \quad \left(\because R = \eta\,\frac{l}{S}\right)$$

**Relation between electric field and current density:**

$$\vec{J} = g\vec{E}$$

**Conductivity of the conductor:**

$$g = \frac{1}{\eta} \quad \text{(Conductivity is the reciprocal of resistivity)}$$

---

## Page 38 (Slide 40) — Ohm's Law (Microscopic Form)

**Ohm's Law**

**Macroscopic form:**

$$\Delta\phi = Ri$$

- If the resistance $R$ does not change when the potential difference $\Delta\phi$ is varied, the conductor satisfies Ohm's Law.
- Used to determine the resistance $R$ by measuring $\Delta\phi$ and $i$.

**Microscopic form:**

$$\vec{J} = g\vec{E}$$

- If the conductivity $g$ does not change when the electric field $\vec{E}$ is varied, the conductor satisfies Ohm's Law.
- Used to determine the conductivity $g$ by measuring $\vec{E}$ and $\vec{J}$.

---

## Page 39 (Slide 41) — Example

**Example**

A wire of length $l = 1\ \text{m}$ and cross-sectional radius $r = 0.05\ \text{m}$ carries a current $i = 2\ \text{A}$ uniformly distributed over its cross-section. The potential difference between the two ends is $\Delta\phi = 20\ \text{V}$. Find:
(a) the electric field inside the wire, (b) the current density, (c) the conductivity, (d) the resistance, and (e) the resistivity.

**(a)** From $\Delta\phi = El$:

$$E = \frac{\Delta\phi}{l} = \frac{20}{1}\ \text{V/m} = 20\ \text{V/m}$$

**(b)** From $J = i/S$:

$$J = \frac{i}{\pi r^2} = \frac{2}{3.14 \times 0.05^2}\ \text{A/m}^2 = 255\ \text{A/m}^2$$

**(c)** From $J = gE$:

$$g = \frac{J}{E} = \frac{255}{20}\ (\Omega\text{m})^{-1} = 12.75\ (\Omega\text{m})^{-1}$$

---

## Page 40 (Slide 42) — Example (continued)

**Example (continued)**

**(d)** From $R = \Delta\phi/i$:

$$R = \frac{\Delta\phi}{i} = \frac{20}{2}\ \Omega = 10\ \Omega$$

**(e)** From $R = \eta\,l/S$:

$$\eta = \frac{\pi r^2 R}{l} = \frac{3.14 \times 0.05^2 \times 10}{1}\ \Omega\text{m} = 7.85 \times 10^{-2}\ \Omega\text{m}$$

**Verification!**

$$g\eta = 12.75 \times (7.85 \times 10^{-2}) = 1 \checkmark$$

---

## Page 41 (Slide 43) — Summary (17D)

**Summary**

**Electric field inside a conductor:**

- When charges are not moving: $\vec{E} = 0$
- When charges are moving: $\vec{E} = \eta\vec{J},\quad \vec{J} = g\vec{E}$

**Ohm's Law** — A conductor satisfies Ohm's Law in the following cases:

- The resistance $R$ does not change when $\Delta\phi$ is varied: $\Delta\phi = iR$
- The conductivity $g$ does not change when $\vec{E}$ is varied: $\vec{J} = g\vec{E}$

---

## Page 54 (Slide 56) — Circuit Problem

**Circuit Problem**

*(Circuit diagram with junctions and branches)*

**Goal of a circuit problem** ⇨ Find the current flowing through each branch of the electric circuit.

**Method** ⇨ Apply **Kirchhoff's Laws**

**Kirchhoff's Laws** ⇨ Proposed in **1845** by the German physicist **Kirchhoff (1824–1887)** when he was a graduate student.

---

## Page 55 (Slide 57) — Steps Before Applying Kirchhoff's Laws

**Steps Before Applying Kirchhoff's Laws**

*(Circuit diagram with currents $i_1$, $i_2$, $i_3$ assigned to each branch)*

- Assign a **direction** and a **name** to the current in each branch of the circuit.
  - The direction of the current can be chosen freely.
  - If the direction is chosen incorrectly, the result from Kirchhoff's Laws will come out negative.

> Kirchhoff's Laws consist of the **First Law** and the **Second Law**.

---

## Page 56 (Slide 58) — Kirchhoff's Laws

**Kirchhoff's Laws**

| First Law | Second Law |
|---|---|
| Applies to all **junctions** in the circuit | Applies to all **loops** in the circuit |
| **Junction Law** | **Loop Law** |
| The algebraic sum of currents at a junction is zero | The algebraic sum of potential differences around a loop is zero |
| **Current Law** | **Voltage Law** |
| **KCL** — Kirchhoff's Current Law | **KVL** — Kirchhoff's Voltage Law |
| Conservation of charge | Conservation of energy |

> Kirchhoff's Laws are not a new principle of electromagnetism. They are simply a convenient method for finding the currents in each branch of a circuit.

---

## Page 57 (Slide 59) — Application of Current Law (KCL)

**Application of the Current Law**

*(Circuit diagram: EMF source $\mathcal{E}$ and four resistors $R_1, R_2, R_3, R_4$, with junctions $a$ and $b$, and currents $i_1, i_2, i_3$)*

**Targets:** 2 junctions

**Sign convention:** incoming current = $+$, outgoing current = $-$

At junction $a$:

$$i_1 - i_2 - i_3 = 0 \quad (1)$$

At junction $b$:

$$-i_1 + i_2 + i_3 = 0 \quad (2)$$

---

## Page 58 (Slide 60) — Application of Voltage Law (KVL)

**Application of the Voltage Law**

*(Circuit diagram with loops $a$, $b$, $c$)*

**Targets:** 3 loops

**Convention:** traverse the loop **clockwise**

Loop $a$:

$$+\mathcal{E} - i_1 R_1 - i_2 R_2 = 0 \quad (3)$$

Loop $b$:

$$+i_2 R_2 - i_3 R_3 - i_3 R_4 = 0 \quad (4)$$

Loop $c$:

$$+\mathcal{E} - i_1 R_1 - i_3 R_3 - i_3 R_4 = 0 \quad (5)$$

---

## Page 59 (Slide 61) — Number of Kirchhoff's Law Equations

**Number of Kirchhoff's Law Equations**

KCL:
$$i_1 - i_2 - i_3 = 0 \quad (1)$$
$$-i_1 + i_2 + i_3 = 0 \quad (2)$$

KVL:
$$+\mathcal{E} - i_1 R_1 - i_2 R_2 = 0 \quad (3)$$
$$+i_2 R_2 - i_3 R_3 - i_3 R_4 = 0 \quad (4)$$
$$+\mathcal{E} - i_1 R_1 - i_3 R_3 - i_3 R_4 = 0 \quad (5)$$

- **Unknowns:** $i_1, i_2, i_3$ — three currents
- **Available equations:** five — too many!

**Observation:**
- Equations (1) and (2) are the same.
- Adding (3) and (4) gives (5).
- Use equations **(1), (3), (4)** to find the three currents.

---

## Page 60 (Slide 62) — Summary (17F)

**Summary**

- Kirchhoff's Laws consist of the First Law (KCL) and the Second Law (KVL).
- The First Law (Current Law) applies to the **junctions** of the circuit.
- The Second Law (Voltage Law) applies to the **loops** of the circuit.
- The total number of Kirchhoff's Law equations may exceed the number of unknowns, but the number of **independent** equations always equals the number of unknown currents.

Applying the voltage law (clockwise):

$$\mathcal{E} - iR_1 - iR_2 = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \mathcal{E} = i(R_1 + R_2)$$

Combined resistance:

$$\boxed{R = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{i} = R_1 + R_2}$$

---

## Page 64 (Slide 66) — Combined Resistance: Parallel Connection

**Combined Resistance of Two Resistors in Parallel**

*(Circuit diagram: $R_1$ and $R_2$ in parallel, with currents $i_1$ and $i_2$)*

Current law: $i = i_1 + i_2 \quad (1)$

Voltage law: $\mathcal{E} = i_1 R_1 \quad (2)$, $\mathcal{E} = i_2 R_2 \quad (3)$

Substituting (2) and (3) into (1):

$$i = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R_1} + \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R_2} = \mathcal{E}\left(\frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2}\right)$$

Reciprocal of combined resistance:

$$\boxed{\frac{1}{R} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2}} \quad \Rightarrow \quad R = \frac{R_1 R_2}{R_1 + R_2}$$

---

**Combined Capacitance**

*(Left: circuit with single capacitor $C$, charge $q$; Right: circuit with combined capacitor $C$, charge $q$)*

- For a combination of capacitors: $C = \dfrac{q}{\mathcal{E}}$ (same form — the combined capacitance)

## Page 66 (Slide 68) — Combined Capacitance: Series Connection

**Combined Capacitance of Two Capacitors in Series**

*(Circuit diagram: $C_1$ and $C_2$ in series, each with charge $q$ and $-q$)*

Applying the voltage law (clockwise):

$$\mathcal{E} - \frac{q}{C_1} - \frac{q}{C_2} = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \mathcal{E} = q\left(\frac{1}{C_1} + \frac{1}{C_2}\right)$$

Reciprocal of combined capacitance:

$$\boxed{\frac{1}{C} = \frac{1}{C_1} + \frac{1}{C_2}} \quad \Rightarrow \quad C = \frac{C_1 C_2}{C_1 + C_2}$$

---

## Page 67 (Slide 69) — Combined Capacitance: Parallel Connection (1)

## Page 86 (Slide 88) — RC Circuit

**RC Circuit**

*(Circuit diagram: EMF source $\mathcal{E}$, resistor $R$, capacitor $C$ with charges $q$ and $-q$, and switch $S$ in series)*

- A circuit with an EMF source, a resistor, and a capacitor connected in **series** is called an **RC circuit**.
- When a capacitor is connected in a DC circuit, it acts like a break in the wire, so after the switch is closed and some time passes, **no current flows**.

---

## Page 87 (Slide 89) — RC Circuit (continued)

**RC Circuit**

- However, while the capacitor is **charging** after the switch is closed, current **flows** in the circuit.
- Also, if a charged capacitor is connected to a resistor only and the switch is closed, current flows while the capacitor is **discharging**.
- Even during the charging or discharging process in an RC circuit, **Kirchhoff's Laws hold instantaneously**.

---

## Page 88 (Slide 90) — Kirchhoff's Voltage Law for RC Circuit

**Kirchhoff's Voltage Law**

*(Circuit diagram of RC circuit)*

Applying the voltage law:

$$\mathcal{E} - iR - \frac{q}{C} = 0$$

Relation between the current $i$ and the charge $q$ on the capacitor:

$$i = \frac{dq}{dt} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \mathcal{E} = R\frac{dq}{dt} + \frac{q}{C}$$

This is a **first-order differential equation** — the goal is to find $q(t)$.

---

## Page 89 (Slide 91) — First-Order Differential Equation

**First-Order Differential Equation**

*(Reference: Lecture 05B)*

General form of a first-order differential equation:

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = f(x, y)$$

Many first-order differential equations can be solved by integration.

**Check if it is in separable form:**

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = f(x,y) \quad \Rightarrow \quad \int f_1(x)\,dx = \int f_2(y)\,dy$$

*(left side: function of $x$ only; right side: function of $y$ only)*

---

## Page 90 (Slide 92) — Separable Form

**Separable Form**

Starting from:

$$\mathcal{E} = R\frac{dq}{dt} + \frac{q}{C}$$

Rearranging:

$$R\frac{dq}{dt} = -\frac{1}{C}(q - \mathcal{E}C) \quad \Rightarrow \quad \frac{dq}{q - \mathcal{E}C} = -\frac{dt}{RC}$$

This is in **separable (integrable) form**. Integrating both sides:

$$\int \frac{dq}{q - \mathcal{E}C} = -\frac{1}{RC}\int dt \quad \Rightarrow \quad \text{(limits not yet specified)}$$

Determining the limits of integration:

$$\int_{q_0}^{q(t)} \frac{dq}{q - \mathcal{E}C} = -\frac{1}{RC}\int_0^t dt = -\frac{t}{RC}$$

---

## Page 91 (Slide 93) — Charge on the Capacitor in an RC Circuit

**Charge on the Capacitor in an RC Circuit**

Evaluating the left-hand side:

$$\int_{q_0}^{q(t)} \frac{dq}{q - \mathcal{E}C} = \ln(q - \mathcal{E}C)\Big|_{q_0}^{q(t)} = \ln\frac{q(t) - \mathcal{E}C}{q_0 - \mathcal{E}C} = -\frac{t}{RC}$$

Using $\ln y = x \Rightarrow y = e^x$:

$$\frac{q(t) - \mathcal{E}C}{q_0 - \mathcal{E}C} = e^{-t/RC}$$

$$\Rightarrow q(t) = \mathcal{E}C\!\left(1 - e^{-t/RC}\right) + q_0\,e^{-t/RC}$$

If $q_0 = 0$:

$$\boxed{q(t) = \mathcal{E}C\!\left(1 - e^{-t/RC}\right)}$$

---

## Page 92 (Slide 94) — Charge and Current in an RC Circuit

**Charge and Current in an RC Circuit**

**Charge on the capacitor:**

$$q(t) = \mathcal{E}C\!\left(1 - e^{-t/RC}\right)$$

- At the instant the switch is closed: $q(0) = 0$
- After a long time: $q(\infty) = \mathcal{E}C$

**Current in the circuit:**

$$i(t) = \frac{dq}{dt} = \frac{\mathcal{E}}{R}\,e^{-t/RC}$$

- At the instant the switch is closed: $i(0) = \dfrac{\mathcal{E}}{R}$
- After a long time: $i(\infty) = 0$

---

## Page 93 (Slide 95) — Time Constant τ = RC

**Time Constant of an RC Circuit: $\tau = RC$**

Dimension of $RC$:

$$[R] = \frac{[\Delta\phi]}{[i]} \qquad [C] = \frac{[q]}{[\Delta\phi]}$$

$$[RC] = \frac{[\Delta\phi]}{[i]} \cdot \frac{[q]}{[\Delta\phi]} = \frac{[q]}{[i]} = [t]$$

> $\tau = RC$ has the dimension of **time** and is called the **time constant** of the RC circuit.

It characterizes how quickly the RC circuit charges or discharges.
