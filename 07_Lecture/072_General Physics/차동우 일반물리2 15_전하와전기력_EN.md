## Page 7 — Fundamental Forces

**Fundamental Forces**

- **Gravitational force** — Acts on objects at Earth's surface: $mg$ — Acts on artificial satellites: $G\dfrac{mM}{r^2}$

- **Strong and weak nuclear forces** — Short-range forces acting only inside atomic nuclei — Do not appear in the phenomena around us

- **Electric force** — $10^{38}$ times stronger than gravity — Most of today's science and technology uses electric force as its result

## Page 8 — Laws of Force

**Laws of Force**

For a force to act, two objects must exist.

*(Diagram: Two rocks (Object A and Object B) with arrows indicating mutual attraction/repulsion.)*

A force is a quantity that tells how strongly two objects interact.

The force is determined by the law of force, which specifies how two objects interact.

Fundamental forces such as gravity and electric force act between two objects that are not in contact, due to an intrinsic property the objects possess.

**The second fundamental force discovered**

- It was expected that the electric force, like gravity, would be inversely proportional to the square of the distance
- Coulomb discovered the law through direct measurement using a torsion balance

## Page 11 — Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation (Mathematical Form)

**Newton's Law of Universal Gravitation**

When two point masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ are separated by a distance $r$, the attractive force $F$ between them is:

$$F = -G\frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2}$$

*(Diagram: Two masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ at position vectors $\vec{r}_1$ and $\vec{r}_2$ with mutual force vectors $\vec{F}_{12}$ and $\vec{F}_{21}$.)*

$$\vec{F}_{12} = -G\frac{m_1 m_2}{|\vec{r}_1 - \vec{r}_2|^2}\underbrace{\frac{\vec{r}_1 - \vec{r}_2}{|\vec{r}_1 - \vec{r}_2|}}_{\text{unit vector}}$$

$$\vec{F}_{21} = -G\frac{m_2 m_1}{|\vec{r}_2 - \vec{r}_1|^2}\underbrace{\frac{\vec{r}_2 - \vec{r}_1}{|\vec{r}_2 - \vec{r}_1|}}_{\text{unit vector}}$$

---

## Page 12 — Coulomb's Law for Electric Force (Mathematical Form)

**Coulomb's Law for Electric Force**

When two point charges $q_1$ and $q_2$ are separated by a distance $r$, the force $F$ between them is:

$$F = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2}$$

$$\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} = 8.99 \times 10^9 \text{ N·m}^2/\text{C}^2$$

$\epsilon_0$: permittivity of free space (vacuum permittivity)

*(Diagram: Two point charges $q_1$ and $q_2$ at positions $\vec{r}_1$ and $\vec{r}_2$ with force vectors $\vec{F}_{12}$ and $\vec{F}_{21}$.)*

$$\vec{F}_{12} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{q_1 q_2}{|\vec{r}_1 - \vec{r}_2|^2}\frac{\vec{r}_1 - \vec{r}_2}{|\vec{r}_1 - \vec{r}_2|}$$

$$\vec{F}_{21} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{q_2 q_1}{|\vec{r}_2 - \vec{r}_1|^2}\frac{\vec{r}_2 - \vec{r}_1}{|\vec{r}_2 - \vec{r}_1|}$$

---

## Page 13 — Example: Electric Force Between Two Point Charges

**Example**

Find the electric force acting between the following two point charges (distances in meters):

$$\vec{r}_1 = 3\hat{x}, \quad q_1 = 1.0\ \mu\text{C}, \quad \vec{r}_2 = 4\hat{y}, \quad q_2 = 5.0\ \mu\text{C}$$

**Solution:**

$$\vec{r}_1 - \vec{r}_2 = 3\hat{x} - 4\hat{y}, \qquad |\vec{r}_1 - \vec{r}_2| = \sqrt{3^2 + (-4)^2} = 5$$

$$\vec{F}_{12} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{q_1 q_2}{|\vec{r}_1 - \vec{r}_2|^2}\frac{\vec{r}_1 - \vec{r}_2}{|\vec{r}_1 - \vec{r}_2|}$$

$$= (9\times10^9) \times \frac{(1\times10^{-6})\times(5\times10^{-6})}{5^2}\cdot\frac{3\hat{x} - 4\hat{y}}{5}\ \text{N}$$

$$\boxed{F_{12} = 1.8\times10^{-3}\ \text{N} = 1.8\ \text{mN}}$$

## Page 24 — Electric Force from Multiple Point Charges (Superposition)

**Electric Force from Two Point Charges (Superposition)**

The force $\vec{F}(\vec{r})$ acting on $q_0$ at $\vec{r}$, from $q_1$ at $\vec{r}_1$ and $q_2$ at $\vec{r}_2$:

*(Diagram: Two source charges $q_1$, $q_2$ at $\vec{r}_1$, $\vec{r}_2$ exerting forces $\vec{F}_1$, $\vec{F}_2$ on test charge $q_0$ at $\vec{r}$.)*

$$\vec{F}(\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{q_0 q_1}{|\vec{r} - \vec{r}_1|^2}\frac{\vec{r} - \vec{r}_1}{|\vec{r} - \vec{r}_1|} + \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{q_0 q_2}{|\vec{r} - \vec{r}_2|^2}\frac{\vec{r} - \vec{r}_2}{|\vec{r} - \vec{r}_2|}$$

For $N$ charges $q_i$ located at $\vec{r}_i$ ($i = 1, 2, \ldots, N$), the electric force on $q_0$ at $\vec{r}$ is:

$$\vec{F}(\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{q_0 q_i}{|\vec{r} - \vec{r}_i|^2}\frac{\vec{r} - \vec{r}_i}{|\vec{r} - \vec{r}_i|}$$

## Page 25 — Example: Four Charges at Corners of a Square

**Example**

Four charges $q = 5.0\ \text{nC}$ each are placed at the four corners of a square with side length $a = 1.0\ \text{cm}$. Find the magnitude of the electric force on each corner charge.

*(Diagram: Square with four charges at corners; force vectors $\vec{F}_1$, $\vec{F}_2$, $\vec{F}_3$ from three neighbors shown on one corner.)*

$$F_1 = F_2 = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{q^2}{a^2} = (9\times10^9)\frac{(5\times10^{-9})^2}{(1\times10^{-2})^2}\ \text{N} = 2.25\times10^{-3}\ \text{N} = 2.25\ \text{mN}$$

$$F_3 = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{q^2}{2a^2} = \frac{1}{2}F_1 = 1.13\ \text{mN}$$

$$|\vec{F}_1 + \vec{F}_2| = \sqrt{2}F_1 = 3.18\ \text{mN}$$

$$|\vec{F}_1 + \vec{F}_2 + \vec{F}_3| = \sqrt{2}F_1 + F_3 = (3.18 + 1.13)\ \text{mN} = \mathbf{4.31\ \text{mN}}$$

## Page 31 — Electric Field: Definition

**Electric Field**

**Definition of electric field**: A region of space where a charge experiences an electric force.

**Roles of a charge:**
1. Creates an electric field in the surrounding space.
2. When placed in an electric field, it receives an electric force.

**Analogy — gravitational field, electric field, magnetic field:**

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Gravitational field | A mass creates a gravitational field; any mass placed in the field experiences a gravitational force. |
| Electric field | A charge creates an electric field; any charge placed in the field experiences an electric force. |
| Magnetic field | A magnet creates a magnetic field; any magnet placed in the field experiences a magnetic force. |

---

## Page 32 — Mathematical Definition of a Field

**Mathematical Definition of a Field**

**Function**: In $y = f(x)$, $x$ is the independent variable and $y$ is the dependent variable.

**Scalar field** $\psi(\vec{r}) = \psi(x, y, z)$:
- When the independent variables $x, y, z$ represent positions in space, the function $\psi(x, y, z)$ is called a field.
- If the field $\psi(x, y, z)$ is a scalar, it is called a scalar field.

**Vector field** $\vec{A}(\vec{r}) = \vec{A}(x, y, z)$:
- When the independent variables $x, y, z$ represent positions in space and the function $\vec{A}(\vec{r})$ is a vector, it is called a vector field.
- **The electric field $\vec{E}(\vec{r})$ is a vector field.**

## Page 34 — Method 1 for Finding the Electric Field (Test Charge)

**Method 1 for Finding the Electric Field**

Second role of a charge: **a charge placed in an electric field receives an electric force.**

*(Diagram: A radiating source charge (sphere) on the left; a small positive test charge $q$ approaching from the right with force $\vec{F}$ pointing leftward.)*

## Page 38 — Electric Force on a Charge in an Electric Field

**Electric Force Received by a Charge in an Electric Field**

The electric force $\vec{F}$ received by a charge $q$ placed at a location where the electric field is $\vec{E}$:

$$\boxed{\vec{F} = q\vec{E}}$$

**Order in which electric force acts on a charge:**

## Page 41 — Method 2 for Finding the Electric Field (Coulomb's Law Approach, Step 1)

**Method 2 for Finding the Electric Field — Using Coulomb's Law**

Electric field created by point charge $q_1$ in the surrounding space:

- Place the source charge $q_1$ at $\vec{r}_1$: $q_1$ creates an electric field in the surrounding space.
- Place a test charge $q$ at $\vec{r}$.

By Coulomb's law, find the electric force $\vec{F}(\vec{r})$ on the test charge $q$:

$$\vec{F}_{21} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{q_2 q_1}{|\vec{r}_2 - \vec{r}_1|^2}\frac{\vec{r}_2 - \vec{r}_1}{|\vec{r}_2 - \vec{r}_1|}$$

$$\Rightarrow \vec{F}(\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{q q_1}{|\vec{r} - \vec{r}_1|^2}\frac{\vec{r} - \vec{r}_1}{|\vec{r} - \vec{r}_1|}$$

## Page 44 — Electric Field from Two Point Charges (Superposition)

**Electric Field from Two Point Charges — Applying the Superposition Principle**

Force $\vec{F}(\vec{r})$ on $q_0$ at $\vec{r}$ due to $q_1$ at $\vec{r}_1$ and $q_2$ at $\vec{r}_2$:

*(Diagram: Source charges $q_1$, $q_2$ and test charge $q$ at $\vec{r}$; force vectors $\vec{F}_1$, $\vec{F}_2$, $\vec{F}$ shown.)*

Divide by $q$ to get the electric field:

$$\vec{E}(\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{q_i}{|\vec{r} - \vec{r}_i|^2}\frac{\vec{r} - \vec{r}_i}{|\vec{r} - \vec{r}_i|}$$

Continuous charge distribution:
$$\vec{E}(\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int_V \frac{dq}{|\vec{r} - \vec{r}'|^2}\frac{\vec{r} - \vec{r}'}{|\vec{r} - \vec{r}'|}$$

## Page 48 — Electric Field from a Continuous Charge Distribution (Volume Density)

**Electric Field from a Continuous Charge Distribution**

*(Diagram: Volume element $dq$ at position $\vec{r}'$, test charge $q$ at $\vec{r}$, with resulting $\vec{E}(\vec{r})$.)*

$$\vec{E}(\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int_V \frac{dq}{|\vec{r} - \vec{r}'|^2}\frac{\vec{r} - \vec{r}'}{|\vec{r} - \vec{r}'|}$$

Substituting $dq = \rho(\vec{r}')\,dv'$:

$$\vec{E}(\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int_V dv'\,\frac{\rho(\vec{r}')}{|\vec{r} - \vec{r}'|^2}\frac{\vec{r} - \vec{r}'}{|\vec{r} - \vec{r}'|}$$

**This is the electric field found by Coulomb's law.**

*(Diagram: Circular ring of radius $R$ in the $xy$-plane centered at origin; element $dq$ at angle $\phi$; field point $(0,0,z)$ on the $z$-axis; $d\vec{E}$ shown pointing from $dq$ toward the field point. By symmetry of the ring, the electric field at $(0,0,z)$ points along the $z$-axis.)*

## Page 52 — Ring Charge: Geometry and Field Element

**Uniform Circular Ring Charge with Linear Charge Density $\lambda$**

*(Diagram: Side view showing the geometry — ring of radius $R$, field point at height $z$; angle $\theta$ between $d\vec{E}$ and the $z$-axis; $z$-component $dE_z$.)*

$$dq = \lambda\,dl = \lambda\,R\,d\phi$$

$$\cos\theta = \frac{z}{\sqrt{R^2 + z^2}}$$

$$d\vec{E}(\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{dq}{|\vec{r} - \vec{r}'|^2}\frac{\vec{r} - \vec{r}'}{|\vec{r} - \vec{r}'|}$$

## Page 53 — Ring Charge: Calculating the $z$-Component

**Uniform Circular Ring Charge with Linear Charge Density $\lambda$**

$$\vec{r} = z\hat{z}, \qquad |\vec{r} - \vec{r}'|^2 = R^2 + z^2$$

$$|d\vec{E}(\vec{r})| = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{dq}{|\vec{r} - \vec{r}'|^2} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{dq}{R^2 + z^2}$$

$$dE_z = |d\vec{E}(\vec{r})|\cos\theta = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{dq}{R^2 + z^2}\cdot\frac{z}{\sqrt{R^2 + z^2}}$$

$$= \frac{\lambda}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{Rz}{(R^2 + z^2)^{3/2}}\,d\phi$$

---

## Page 54 — Ring Charge: Final Result

**Uniform Circular Ring Charge with Linear Charge Density $\lambda$**

$$E(z) = \frac{\lambda}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{Rz}{(R^2 + z^2)^{3/2}}\int_0^{2\pi}d\phi$$

$$= \frac{2\pi R\lambda}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{z}{(R^2 + z^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{1}{z^2}\frac{1}{\left(1 + \dfrac{R^2}{z^2}\right)^{3/2}}$$

When $z \gg R$:

$$E(z) \approx \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{Q}{z^2}$$

*(The ring looks like a point charge from far away.)*

## Page 55 — Electric Field from a Uniform Finite Line Charge (Setup)

**Uniform Line Charge with Linear Charge Density $\lambda$**

A line charge of length from $-a$ to $b$ along the $x$-axis; find the field at point $P$ on the $y$-axis at height $y$.

*(Diagram: Line charge along the $x$-axis from $-a$ to $b$; element $dq = \lambda\,dx'$ at position $x'$; field point at $\vec{r} = y\hat{y}$.)*

$$\vec{r} = y\hat{y}, \quad \vec{r}' = x'\hat{x}$$

$$\vec{r} - \vec{r}' = y\hat{y} - x'\hat{x}, \quad |\vec{r} - \vec{r}'|^2 = x'^2 + y^2$$

$$dq = \lambda\,dx'$$

$$d\vec{E}(\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{dq}{|\vec{r}-\vec{r}'|^2}\frac{\vec{r}-\vec{r}'}{|\vec{r}-\vec{r}'|}$$

$$\Rightarrow\quad d\vec{E}(y) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{\lambda\,dx'}{x'^2 + y^2}\frac{y\hat{y} - x'\hat{x}}{\sqrt{x'^2 + y^2}}$$

---

## Page 56 — Line Charge: Separating into Components

**Uniform Line Charge with Linear Charge Density $\lambda$**

$$d\vec{E}(y) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{\lambda\,dx'}{x'^2 + y^2}\frac{y\hat{y} - x'\hat{x}}{\sqrt{x'^2+y^2}}$$

Separate into $x$- and $y$-components:

**(1) $x$-component:**

$$dE_x(y) = -\frac{\lambda}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{x'\,dx'}{(x'^2 + y^2)^{3/2}}$$

$$E_x(y) = -\frac{\lambda}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int_{-a}^{b}\frac{x'\,dx'}{(x'^2+y^2)^{3/2}}$$

**(2) $y$-component:**

$$dE_y(y) = \frac{\lambda y}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{dx'}{(x'^2+y^2)^{3/2}}$$

$$E_y(y) = \frac{\lambda y}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int_{-a}^{b}\frac{dx'}{(x'^2+y^2)^{3/2}}$$

---

## Page 57 — Integration (1): $x$-Component Integral

**Integration (1)**

$$I_1 = \int_a^b \frac{x\,dx}{(x^2+y^2)^{3/2}} \qquad \Rightarrow \text{substitute } t = x^2 + y^2$$

$$dt = d(x^2+y^2) = 2x\,dx$$

When $x=a$: $t = a^2+y^2$; when $x=b$: $t = b^2+y^2$

$$I_1 = \int_{a^2+y^2}^{b^2+y^2}\frac{\frac{1}{2}dt}{t^{3/2}} = \frac{1}{2}\int_{a^2+y^2}^{b^2+y^2}t^{-3/2}dt$$

$$= \left[\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{-\frac{3}{2}+1}t^{-\frac{3}{2}+1}\right]_{a^2+y^2}^{b^2+y^2} = \left[-\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}\right]_{a^2+y^2}^{b^2+y^2}$$

$$= \frac{1}{\sqrt{a^2+y^2}} - \frac{1}{\sqrt{b^2+y^2}}$$

---

## Page 58 — Integration (2): $y$-Component Integral

**Integration (2)**

$$I_2 = \int_a^b\frac{dx}{(x^2+y^2)^{3/2}} \qquad \Rightarrow \text{look up integral table online}$$

Standard result (Dwight, Tables of Integrals, 200.03):

$$\int\frac{dx}{(a^2+x^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{x}{a^2\sqrt{a^2+x^2}} \quad \text{where } r = \sqrt{x^2+a^2}$$

$$I_2 = \int_a^b\frac{dx}{(x^2+y^2)^{3/2}} = \left[\frac{x}{y^2\sqrt{x^2+y^2}}\right]_a^b = \frac{b}{y^2\sqrt{b^2+y^2}} - \frac{a}{y^2\sqrt{a^2+y^2}}$$

---

## Page 59 — Line Charge: $x$-Component Result

**Uniform Line Charge: $x$-Component**

$$E_x(y) = -\frac{\lambda}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int_{-a}^{b}\frac{x'\,dx'}{(x'^2+y^2)^{3/2}}, \qquad E_y(y) = \frac{\lambda y}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int_{-a}^{b}\frac{dx'}{(x'^2+y^2)^{3/2}}$$

**(1) $x$-component:** $I_1 = \displaystyle\int_a^b\frac{x\,dx}{(x^2+y^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{a^2+y^2}} - \frac{1}{\sqrt{b^2+y^2}}$

$$\Rightarrow\quad E_x(y) = \frac{\lambda}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{b^2+y^2}} - \frac{1}{\sqrt{a^2+y^2}}\right]$$

When $a = b$ (symmetric) or $a = b = \infty$ (infinite line):

$$\boxed{E_x(y) = 0}\ \checkmark$$

---

## Page 60 — Line Charge: $y$-Component Result

**Uniform Line Charge: $y$-Component**

**(2) $y$-component:**

$$I_2 = \int_a^b\frac{dx}{(x^2+y^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{b}{y^2\sqrt{b^2+y^2}} - \frac{a}{y^2\sqrt{a^2+y^2}}$$

$$\Rightarrow\quad E_y(y) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{\lambda}{y}\left[\frac{b}{\sqrt{b^2+y^2}} + \frac{a}{\sqrt{a^2+y^2}}\right]$$

When $a = b$:

$$E_y(y) = \frac{1}{2\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{\lambda}{y}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{y}{a}\right)^2}}$$

When $a = \infty$ (infinite line charge):

$$\boxed{E_y(y) = \frac{1}{2\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{\lambda}{y}}\ \checkmark$$

---

## Page 61 — Summary: Electric Field from Ring and Line Charges

**Summary**

**Electric field from a uniform circular ring charge:**

$$E(z) = \frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{1}{z^2}\frac{1}{\left(1+\dfrac{R^2}{z^2}\right)^{3/2}}, \qquad z\gg R \Rightarrow E(z)\approx\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{Q}{z^2}$$

$$E_y(y) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{\lambda}{y}\left[\frac{b}{\sqrt{b^2+y^2}} + \frac{a}{\sqrt{a^2+y^2}}\right]$$

## Page 64 — Electric Field of a Dipole on the Axis (1)

**Electric Field Created by an Electric Dipole (1)**

Two point charges $+q$ at $x = +\dfrac{d}{2}$ and $-q$ at $x = -\dfrac{d}{2}$ on the $x$-axis; field at point $P$ at position $x \gg d$ on the $+x$ axis:

$$E(x) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left[\frac{+q}{\left(x-\dfrac{d}{2}\right)^2} + \frac{-q}{\left(x+\dfrac{d}{2}\right)^2}\right] = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{q}{x^2}\left[\frac{1}{\left(1-\dfrac{d}{2x}\right)^2} - \frac{1}{\left(1+\dfrac{d}{2x}\right)^2}\right]$$

**Useful approximation**: when $|\alpha|\ll 1$, $(1+\alpha)^n \approx 1 + n\alpha$

$$\Rightarrow\quad \frac{1}{\left(1\pm\dfrac{d}{2x}\right)^2} \approx 1\mp\frac{d}{x}$$

---

## Page 65 — Electric Field of a Dipole on the Axis (2)

**Electric Field Created by an Electric Dipole (2) — on-axis result**

$$E(x) \approx \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{q}{x^2}\left[\left(1+\frac{d}{x}\right) - \left(1-\frac{d}{x}\right)\right] = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{qd}{x^3}\cdot 2 = \frac{1}{2\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{p}{x^3}$$

We define $p = qd$ as the **electric dipole moment**.

In the region $x \gg d$, the field depends on the product $p = qd$, not on $q$ and $d$ separately — so the same electric field is produced for the same $p = qd$. The field falls off as $x^{-3}$.

---

## Page 66 — Electric Field of a Dipole off the Axis (1)

**Electric Field Created by an Electric Dipole (2) — off-axis**

Two charges $+q$ at $y = +\dfrac{d}{2}$ and $-q$ at $y = -\dfrac{d}{2}$ on the $y$-axis; field at point $P$ at $(x,0)$ with $x \gg d$:

*(Diagram: dipole oriented along $y$-axis; field point $P$ on $+x$ axis; individual field vectors $E_{-q}$ and $E_q$ shown, with resultant $\vec{E}(x)$ pointing in $-\hat{y}$ direction.)*

$$\vec{E}(x) = -E(x)\hat{y}, \qquad E(x) = 2E_q\cos\theta$$

$$E_q = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{q}{x^2 + \left(\dfrac{d}{2}\right)^2}$$

$$\Rightarrow E(x) = 2\cdot\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{q}{x^2 + \left(\dfrac{d}{2}\right)^2}\cdot\frac{\dfrac{d}{2}}{\sqrt{x^2+\left(\dfrac{d}{2}\right)^2}}$$

$$= \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{qd}{x^3}\frac{1}{\left(1+\left(\dfrac{d}{2x}\right)^2\right)^{3/2}} \approx \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{p}{x^3}$$

---

## Page 67 — Electric Field of a Dipole off the Axis (2) — Final Result

**Electric Field Created by an Electric Dipole (2)**

$$\vec{E}(x) = -E(x)\hat{y} = -\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{qd}{x^3}\hat{y} = -\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{p}{x^3}\hat{y}$$

In the region $x \gg d$, the field depends on $p = qd$ only and falls off as $x^{-3}$.

## Page 68 — Electric Dipole Moment and Electric Field of a Dipole

**Electric Dipole Moment and the Electric Field of an Electric Dipole**

*(Diagram: dipole with $-q$ at $-\vec{d}/2$ and $+q$ at $+\vec{d}/2$; position vector $\vec{r}$ to field point $P$.)*

**Definition of the electric dipole moment:**

$$\boxed{\vec{p} = q\vec{d}}$$

Electric field $\vec{E}(\vec{r})$ at point $P$ with position vector $\vec{r}$, created by an electric dipole $\vec{p}$ at the origin, when $r \gg d$:

$$\vec{E}(\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{3(\vec{p}\cdot\hat{r})\hat{r} - \vec{p}}{r^3}$$

## Page 69 — Electric Field of a Dipole: Verification for Two Orientations

**Electric Field of a Dipole**

*(Diagram comparing two cases side-by-side.)*

**Case 1:** $\vec{p} = p\hat{x}$, field point at $\vec{r} = x\hat{x}$ (on-axis):

$$\vec{E}(\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{3(p\hat{x}\cdot\hat{x})\hat{x} - p\hat{x}}{x^3} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{3p\hat{x} - p\hat{x}}{x^3} = \frac{1}{2\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{p}{x^3}\hat{x}\ \checkmark$$

**Case 2:** $\vec{p} = p\hat{y}$, field point at $\vec{r} = x\hat{x}$ (perpendicular):

$$\vec{E}(\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{3(p\hat{y}\cdot\hat{x})\hat{x} - p\hat{y}}{x^3} = -\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{p}{x^3}\hat{y}\ \checkmark$$

## Page 70 — Electric Force on a Dipole in a Uniform Field

**Electric Force on an Electric Dipole in a Uniform Electric Field**

**Uniform electric field**: an electric field that has the same magnitude and direction everywhere.

*(Diagram: charge $-q$ at top with force $\vec{F}_{-q} = -q\vec{E}$ pointing left; charge $+q$ at bottom with force $\vec{F}_q = q\vec{E}$ pointing right.)*

Electric force on the dipole:

$$\vec{F} = \vec{F}_q + \vec{F}_{-q} = (q\vec{E}) + (-q\vec{E}) = \mathbf{0}$$

*(The net force on a dipole in a uniform field is zero.)*

---

## Page 71 — Torque on a Dipole in a Uniform Field

**Torque on an Electric Dipole in a Uniform Electric Field**

*(Diagram: $-q$ at $\vec{r}_1$ with force $\vec{F}_{-q} = -q\vec{E}$; $+q$ at $\vec{r}_2$ with force $\vec{F}_q = q\vec{E}$; separation vector $\vec{d}$ from $-q$ to $+q$.)*

Torque on the dipole:

$$\vec{\tau} = \vec{r}_1\times\vec{F}_{-q} + \vec{r}_2\times\vec{F}_q = \vec{r}_1\times(-q\vec{E}) + \vec{r}_2\times(q\vec{E})$$

$$= q(\vec{r}_2 - \vec{r}_1)\times\vec{E} = q\vec{d}\times\vec{E} = \boxed{\vec{p}\times\vec{E}}$$

## Page 93 — Gauss's Law (Statement)

**Gauss's Law**

1. Draw a closed surface anywhere in space.
   *(The closed surface drawn to apply Gauss's law is called the **Gaussian surface**.)*

2. Find the total electric flux $\Phi_E$ through the Gaussian surface.

3. Find the net charge $q$ enclosed inside the Gaussian surface.

**Gauss's Law:**

$$\boxed{\Phi_E = \frac{q}{\epsilon_0}}$$

---

## Page 94 — Example: Flux Through a Face of a Cube

**Example**

A point charge $q = \dfrac{1}{4\pi}\ \text{nC}$ is placed at one corner of a cube with side length $a = 1.0\ \text{cm}$. Find the electric flux through one face of the cube that does **not** contain the charge.

First, find the total flux $\Phi_E$ through a closed surface enclosing $q$:

$$\Phi_E = \frac{q}{\epsilon_0} = 4\pi \times (9\times10^9)\times\frac{10^{-9}}{4\pi}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C} = 9\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}$$

Since the area of the face through which flux passes is $\dfrac{1}{24}$ of the total surface area (the charge sits at a corner, shared by 8 cubes, and each cube has 6 faces but only 3 faces are "away" from the corner — actually the flux distributes over $\frac{1}{24}$ of the total), the flux through one such face is:

$$\Phi = \frac{\Phi_E}{24} = \frac{9}{24}\ \frac{\text{N·m}^2}{\text{C}} = \frac{3}{8}\ \frac{\text{N·m}^2}{\text{C}}$$

## Page 95 — Gauss's Law in Integral Form

**Gauss's Law in Integral Form**

When charges are distributed continuously with volume charge density $\rho(\vec{r})$ and the electric field is $\vec{E}(\vec{r})$:

Total electric flux through an arbitrary Gaussian surface $S$:

$$\Phi_E = \oint_S \vec{E}(\vec{r})\cdot d\vec{S}$$

Net charge $q$ enclosed inside the Gaussian surface $S$:

$$q = \int_V \rho(\vec{r})\,dv$$

**Gauss's Law:**

$$\Phi_E = \frac{q}{\epsilon_0}$$

$$\Rightarrow\quad \boxed{\oint_S \vec{E}(\vec{r})\cdot d\vec{S} = \frac{1}{\epsilon_0}\int_V\rho(\vec{r})\,dv}$$

## Page 98 — Can Gauss's Law Always Find the Electric Field?

**Gauss's Law**

$$\Phi_E = \frac{q}{\epsilon_0} \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad \oint_S \vec{E}(\vec{r})\cdot d\vec{S} = \frac{1}{\epsilon_0}\int_V\rho(\vec{r})\,dv$$

If the charge density $\rho(\vec{r})$ is known, can we always find the electric field $\vec{E}(\vec{r})$ using Gauss's law?

**YES and NO**

If the charge density $\rho$ has symmetry and it is possible to draw a special Gaussian surface satisfying the conditions below, then it is possible!!

## Page 99 — Conditions for Applying Gauss's Law to Find the Electric Field

**Cases Where Gauss's Law Can Find the Electric Field**

From the symmetry of the charge density $\rho(\vec{r})$, if we can draw a Gaussian surface satisfying the following conditions, then we can find the electric field using Gauss's law.

1. Draw a surface where **the direction of the electric field is parallel to the surface normal and the field magnitude is the same** at every point on the surface.

2. Draw a surface where **the direction of the electric field is perpendicular to the surface normal** (i.e., $\vec{E}\perp d\vec{S}$, so the contribution is zero).

**Point charge:**

*(Diagram: A point charge $q_0$ at the center of a spherical Gaussian surface of radius $a$.)*

Choose the surface of a sphere centered on the point charge as the Gaussian surface.

$\Rightarrow$ Condition 1 satisfied.

## Page 101 — Electric Field of a Point Charge via Gauss's Law (Setup)

**Electric Field Created by a Point Charge**

**Gauss's Law:** $\Phi_E = \dfrac{q}{\epsilon_0}$

*(Diagram: Point charge $q_0$ at the center of a sphere of radius $a$; Gaussian surface is the sphere.)*

From the mere fact that a point charge $q_0$ at the center creates an electric field on the spherical surface, we can determine — using symmetry alone:

1. **The magnitude of the electric field is the same at every point on the spherical surface.**

   $\Rightarrow E(a) = E_0$

2. **The direction of the electric field on the spherical surface is the radial direction.**

   $\Rightarrow \vec{E}(a) = E_0\hat{r}$

---

## Page 102 — Electric Field of a Point Charge via Gauss's Law (Calculation)

**Electric Field Created by a Point Charge**

**Total flux:**

$$\Phi_E = \oint_S \vec{E}(\vec{r})\cdot d\vec{S} = \oint_S (E_0\hat{r})\cdot(dS\hat{r})$$

$$= \oint_S E_0\,dS = E_0\oint_S dS = 4\pi a^2 E_0$$

**Enclosed charge:**

$$q = \int_V \rho(\vec{r})\,dv = q_0$$

**Gauss's Law:**

$$\Phi_E = \frac{q}{\epsilon_0} \quad\Rightarrow\quad 4\pi a^2 E_0 = \frac{q_0}{\epsilon_0}$$

$$\boxed{E_0 = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{q_0}{a^2}}$$

This is identical to the result obtained by applying Coulomb's law!

---

## Page 103 — Electric Field of an Infinite Line Charge via Gauss's Law (Setup)

**Electric Field Created by an Infinitely Long Uniform Line Charge**

*(Diagram: Infinite line charge with linear charge density $\lambda$; cylindrical Gaussian surface of radius $a$ and length $l$.)*

From the mere fact that an infinitely long line charge of uniform density $\lambda$ creates an electric field on the cylindrical surface, symmetry alone tells us:

1. **The magnitude of the electric field is the same at every point on the lateral surface.**

   $\Rightarrow E(a) = E_0$

2. **The direction of the electric field on the lateral surface is the $\hat{\rho}$ direction (radially outward).**

   $\Rightarrow \vec{E}(a) = E_0\hat{\rho}$

3. **The electric flux through the end caps is zero.**

---

## Page 104 — Electric Field of an Infinite Line Charge via Gauss's Law (Calculation)

**Electric Field Created by an Infinitely Long Uniform Line Charge**

**Total flux:**

$$\Phi_E = \oint_S \vec{E}(\vec{r})\cdot d\vec{S} = \int_{\text{lateral}} (E_0\hat{\rho})\cdot(dS\hat{\rho})$$

$$= \int_{\text{lateral}} E_0\,dS = E_0\int_{\text{lateral}} dS = 2\pi a l E_0$$

**Enclosed charge:**

$$q = \int_V \rho(\vec{r})\,dv = \lambda l$$

**Gauss's Law:**

$$\Phi_E = \frac{q}{\epsilon_0} \quad\Rightarrow\quad 2\pi a l E_0 = \frac{\lambda l}{\epsilon_0}$$

$$\boxed{E_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{\lambda}{a}}$$

This is identical to the result obtained by applying Coulomb's law!

---

## Page 105 — Electric Field of an Infinite Line Charge: Cross-Check with Coulomb's Law

**Electric Field Created by an Infinitely Long Uniform Line Charge**

**Electric field from a uniform straight line charge (Coulomb's law result):**

*(Diagram: Finite line charge from $-a$ to $b$ along the $x$-axis with field point $P$ at height $y$.)*

$$E_x(y) = \frac{\lambda}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{b^2+y^2}} - \frac{1}{\sqrt{a^2+y^2}}\right]$$

$$E_y(y) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{\lambda}{y}\left[\frac{b}{\sqrt{b^2+y^2}} + \frac{a}{\sqrt{a^2+y^2}}\right]$$

**For an infinitely long uniform straight line charge**, when $a = b = \infty$:

$$E_x(y) = 0, \qquad \boxed{E_y(y) = \frac{1}{2\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{\lambda}{y}}$$

## Page 106 — Electric Field of an Infinite Plane Sheet via Gauss's Law (Setup)

**Electric Field Created by an Infinitely Wide Uniform Plane Sheet**

**Gauss's Law:** $\Phi_E = \dfrac{q}{\epsilon_0}$

*(Diagram: An infinite plane with surface charge density $\sigma$; cylindrical Gaussian surface (pill-box) with end-caps of area $S$ at distances $+z$ and $-z$ from the plane.)*

From the mere fact that an infinitely wide plane with uniform surface charge density $\sigma$ creates an electric field on the surfaces at equal distances, symmetry alone tells us:

1. **The magnitude of the electric field is the same everywhere above and below the plane.**

   $\Rightarrow E(\pm z) = E_0$

2. **The direction of the electric field is the $\pm\hat{z}$ direction everywhere.**

   $\Rightarrow \vec{E}(z) = \pm E_0\hat{z}$

3. **The electric flux through the lateral surface is zero.**

## Page 107 — Electric Field of an Infinite Plane Sheet via Gauss's Law (Calculation)

**Electric Field Created by an Infinitely Wide Uniform Plane Sheet**

**Total flux:**

$$\Phi_E = \oint_S \vec{E}(\vec{r})\cdot d\vec{S} = \int_{\text{top+bottom}} \vec{E}(\vec{r})\cdot d\vec{S}$$

$$= \int_{\text{top+bottom}} E_0\,dS = E_0\int_{\text{top+bottom}} dS = 2SE_0$$

**Enclosed charge:**

$$q = \int_V \rho(\vec{r})\,dv = \sigma S$$

**Gauss's Law:**

$$\Phi_E = \frac{q}{\epsilon_0} \quad\Rightarrow\quad 2SE_0 = \frac{\sigma S}{\epsilon_0}$$

$$\boxed{E_0 = \frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0}}$$

## Page 108 — Electric Field Inside a Uniformly Charged Sphere ($r < R$)

**Electric Field Created by a Uniformly Charged Sphere**

A sphere of radius $R$ with total charge $Q$ uniformly distributed throughout its volume:

$$\rho = \frac{Q}{\dfrac{4}{3}\pi R^3}$$

**Gauss's Law:** $\Phi_E = \dfrac{q}{\epsilon_0}$, where $\Phi_E = \displaystyle\oint_S\vec{E}(\vec{r})\cdot d\vec{S}$ and $q = \displaystyle\int_V\rho(\vec{r})\,dv$

**(1) For $r < R$:** Choose a spherical Gaussian surface of radius $r < R$ centered on the sphere.

$$\Phi_E = \oint_S\vec{E}(\vec{r})\cdot d\vec{S} = 4\pi r^2 E(r)$$

$$q = \int_V\rho(\vec{r})\,dv = \rho\cdot\frac{4\pi}{3}r^3 = Q\frac{r^3}{R^3}$$

$$\Phi_E = \frac{q}{\epsilon_0} \quad\Rightarrow\quad 4\pi r^2 E(r) = \frac{Q}{\epsilon_0}\frac{r^3}{R^3} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \boxed{E(r) = \frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 R^3}r}$$

---

## Page 109 — Electric Field Outside a Uniformly Charged Sphere ($r > R$)

**Electric Field Created by a Uniformly Charged Sphere**

*(Same setup as Page 108.)*

**(2) For $r > R$:** Choose a spherical Gaussian surface of radius $r > R$ centered on the sphere.

$$\Phi_E = \oint_S\vec{E}(\vec{r})\cdot d\vec{S} = 4\pi r^2 E(r) \qquad q = \int_V\rho(\vec{r})\,dv = Q$$

$$\Phi_E = \frac{q}{\epsilon_0} \quad\Rightarrow\quad 4\pi r^2 E(r) = \frac{Q}{\epsilon_0}$$

$$\boxed{E(r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{Q}{r^2}}$$

This is identical to the electric field of a point charge!

## Page 112 — Kronecker Delta and Dirac Delta Function

**Kronecker Delta Symbol $\delta_{ij}$ and Dirac Delta Function $\delta(x-a)$**

| Kronecker Delta Symbol $\delta_{ij}$ | Dirac Delta Function $\delta(x-a)$ |
|---|---|
| $\delta_{ij} = \begin{cases}1 & \text{when } i = j \\ 0 & \text{when } i \neq j\end{cases}$ | $\delta(x-a) = \begin{cases}0 & \text{when } x \neq a \\ \infty & \text{when } x = a\end{cases}$ |
| **Properties of $\delta_{ij}$** | **Properties of $\delta(x-a)$** |
| $\displaystyle\sum_i \delta_{ij} = 1$ | $\displaystyle\int dx\,\delta(x-a) = 1$ |
| $\displaystyle\sum_i f_i\delta_{ij} = f_j$ | $\displaystyle\int dx\,f(x)\delta(x-a) = f(a)$ |

## Page 113 — Proofs of Delta Function Properties

**Proof**

**Proof that $\displaystyle\sum_i \delta_{ij} = 1$:**

$$\sum_i \delta_{i2} = \delta_{12} + \delta_{22} + \delta_{32} + \cdots = 1$$

**Proof that $\displaystyle\sum_i f_i\delta_{ij} = f_j$:**

$$\sum_i f_i\delta_{i2} = f_1\delta_{12} + f_2\delta_{22} + f_3\delta_{32} + \cdots = f_2$$

**Proof that $\displaystyle\int dx\,\delta(x-a) = 1$:**

By the definition of the Dirac delta function.

**Proof that $\displaystyle\int dx\,f(x)\delta(x-a) = f(a)$:**

$$\int dx\,f(x)\delta(x-a) = f(a)\int dx\,\delta(x-a) = f(a)$$

*(Since $\delta(x-a) = 0$ everywhere except $x = a$, we can replace $f(x)$ by $f(a)$ inside the integral.)*

---

## Page 114 — Proof: Graphical Interpretation of the Sifting Property

**Proof**

*(Diagram: Graph showing $f(x)$ as a smooth curve and $\delta(x-a)$ as an infinitely tall, infinitely narrow spike at $x = a$; the dashed horizontal line marks $f(a)$.)*

$$\int dx\,f(x)\delta(x-a) = f(a)\int dx\,\delta(x-a) = f(a)$$

*(The delta function acts as a sieve: it "picks out" the value of $f$ at $x = a$.)*

## Page 116 — A Useful 3D Dirac Delta Identity

**Useful 3D Dirac Delta Function Identity**

$$\nabla^2\!\left(\frac{1}{|\vec{r}-\vec{r}'|}\right) = -4\pi\delta^3(\vec{r}-\vec{r}') \qquad \nabla^2 = \nabla\cdot\nabla \text{ (Laplacian)}$$

**Things to verify:**

(1) $\nabla^2\!\left(\dfrac{1}{|\vec{r}-\vec{r}'|}\right) = 0$ when $\vec{r} \neq \vec{r}'$

$$\Rightarrow\quad \nabla^2\frac{1}{r} = 0 \quad\text{when } r \neq 0$$

(2) $\displaystyle\int dv\,\nabla^2\!\left(\frac{1}{|\vec{r}-\vec{r}'|}\right) = -4\pi \quad\Rightarrow\quad \int dv\,\nabla^2\frac{1}{r} = -4\pi$

## Page 117 — Gradient, Divergence, and Laplacian in Spherical Coordinates

**Gradient, Divergence, and Laplacian in Spherical Coordinates**

**Gradient:**

$$\nabla u = \hat{r}\frac{\partial u}{\partial r} + \hat{\theta}\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial u}{\partial\theta} + \hat{\phi}\frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\frac{\partial u}{\partial\phi}$$

**Divergence:**

$$\nabla\cdot\vec{F} = \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}(r^2 F_r) + \frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}(\sin\theta\,F_\theta) + \frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\frac{\partial F_\phi}{\partial\phi}$$

**Laplacian:**

$$\nabla^2 u = \nabla\cdot\nabla u$$

$$= \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\!\left(r^2\frac{\partial u}{\partial r}\right) + \frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\!\left(\sin\theta\frac{\partial u}{\partial\theta}\right) + \frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}\!\left(\frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\frac{\partial u}{\partial\phi}\right)$$

$$= \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\!\left(r^2\frac{\partial u}{\partial r}\right) + \frac{1}{r^2\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\!\left(\sin\theta\frac{\partial u}{\partial\theta}\right) + \frac{1}{r^2\sin^2\theta}\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial\phi^2}$$

## Page 118 — Proof of the Delta Identity: Step 1

**Useful 3D Dirac Delta Function — Proof of Part (2)**

**(2)** $\displaystyle\int dv\,\nabla^2\frac{1}{r} = -4\pi$ — using $\nabla^2\dfrac{1}{r} = \dfrac{1}{r^2}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial r}\!\left(r^2\dfrac{\partial}{\partial r}\!\left(\dfrac{1}{r}\right)\right)$

**Method:** $\nabla^2\dfrac{1}{r} = \lim_{a\to 0}\nabla^2\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{r^2+a^2}}$

$$\int dv\,\nabla^2\frac{1}{r} = \lim_{a\to 0}\int dv\,\nabla^2\frac{1}{\sqrt{r^2+a^2}}$$

$$\nabla^2\frac{1}{\sqrt{r^2+a^2}} = \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\!\left(r^2\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{r^2+a^2}}\right)\right)$$

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{r^2+a^2}}\right) = -\frac{1}{2}\frac{2r}{(r^2+a^2)^{3/2}} = -\frac{r}{(r^2+a^2)^{3/2}}$$

---

## Page 119 — Proof of the Delta Identity: Step 2

**Useful 3D Dirac Delta Function — Proof (continued)**

$$\nabla^2\frac{1}{\sqrt{r^2+a^2}} = \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\!\left(r^2\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{r^2+a^2}}\right)\right) = -\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\!\left(\frac{r^3}{(r^2+a^2)^{3/2}}\right)$$

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\!\left(\frac{r^3}{(r^2+a^2)^{3/2}}\right) = \frac{3r^2}{(r^2+a^2)^{3/2}} - \frac{3}{2}\frac{2r^4}{(r^2+a^2)^{5/2}} = \frac{3a^2 r^2}{(r^2+a^2)^{5/2}}$$

$$\Rightarrow\quad \nabla^2\frac{1}{\sqrt{r^2+a^2}} = -\frac{3a^2}{(r^2+a^2)^{5/2}}$$

---

## Page 120 — Proof of the Delta Identity: Step 3

**Useful 3D Dirac Delta Function — Proof (continued)**

**(2)** $\displaystyle\int dv\,\nabla^2\frac{1}{r} = -4\pi$ — proof using volume integral:

$$\int dv\,\nabla^2\frac{1}{\sqrt{r^2+a^2}} = -3a^2\int dv\,\frac{1}{(r^2+a^2)^{5/2}} = -12\pi a^2\int_0^\infty dr\,\frac{r^2}{(r^2+a^2)^{5/2}}$$

$$= 4\pi r\,dr \text{ (spherical shell volume element used)}$$

Using Dwight, Tables of Integrals 202.05:

$$\int_0^\infty dr\,\frac{r^2}{(r^2+a^2)^{5/2}} = \left[\frac{1}{3a^2}\frac{r^3}{(r^2+a^2)^{3/2}}\right]_0^\infty = \frac{1}{3a^2}$$

---

## Page 121 — Proof of the Delta Identity: Final Step

**Useful 3D Dirac Delta Function — Proof (completed)**

$$\int dv\,\nabla^2\frac{1}{\sqrt{r^2+a^2}} = -12\pi a^2\int_0^\infty dr\,\frac{r^2}{(r^2+a^2)^{5/2}} = -12\pi a^2\cdot\frac{1}{3a^2} = -4\pi$$

This result is always $-4\pi$ regardless of $a$.

$$\Rightarrow\quad \boxed{\int dv\,\nabla^2\frac{1}{r} = -4\pi} \quad\checkmark$$

## Page 124 — Stokes' Theorem

**Stokes' Theorem**

The line integral of any vector field $\vec{F}(\vec{r})$ along an arbitrary closed curve $C$ equals the surface integral of $\nabla\times\vec{F}(\vec{r})$ over the surface $S$ bounded by the closed curve $C$:

$$\oint_C \vec{F}(\vec{r})\cdot d\vec{r} = \int_S (\nabla\times\vec{F}(\vec{r}))\cdot d\vec{S}$$

*(Diagram with labels: $\oint_C$ = line integral, $d\vec{r}$ = line element, "line integral over closed curve $C$"; $\int_S$ = surface integral, $d\vec{S}$ = area element, "surface integral over surface $S$ bounded by closed curve $C$".)*

---

## Page 125 — Divergence Theorem

**Divergence Theorem**

The surface integral of any vector field $\vec{F}(\vec{r})$ over an arbitrary closed surface $S$ equals the volume integral of $\nabla\cdot\vec{F}(\vec{r})$ over the volume $V$ enclosed by the closed surface $S$:

$$\oint_S \vec{F}(\vec{r})\cdot d\vec{S} = \int_V \nabla\cdot\vec{F}(\vec{r})\,dV$$

*(Diagram with labels: $\oint_S$ = surface integral, $d\vec{S}$ = area element, "surface integral over closed surface $S$"; $\int_V$ = volume integral, $dV$ = volume element, "volume integral over volume $V$ enclosed by closed surface $S$".)*

---

## Page 126 — Divergence Theorem: Surface Integral Example

**Divergence Theorem / Surface Integral**

**Try:** $\vec{F}(\vec{r}) = \vec{r}$, $S$ = surface of a sphere of radius $a$

*(Diagram: Sphere of radius $a$ in 3D coordinates with unit radial vector $\hat{r}$ shown.)*

On the spherical surface:

$$\vec{F}(\vec{r}) = a\hat{r}$$

$$\vec{F}(\vec{r})\cdot d\vec{S} = (a\hat{r})\cdot(dS\hat{r}) = a\,dS$$

$$\Rightarrow\quad \oint_S \vec{F}(\vec{r})\cdot d\vec{S} = a\oint_S dS = 4\pi a^3$$

---

## Page 127 — Divergence Theorem: Volume Integral Example

**Divergence Theorem / Volume Integral**

**Try:** $\vec{F}(\vec{r}) = \vec{r}$, $V$ = interior of a sphere of radius $a$

*(Diagram: Sphere of radius $a$ in 3D coordinates.)*

Inside the sphere of radius $a$:

$$\nabla\cdot\vec{F}(\vec{r}) = \nabla\cdot\vec{r} = \frac{\partial x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial z}{\partial z} = 3$$

$$\int_V \nabla\cdot\vec{F}(\vec{r})\,dV = 3\int_V dV = 3\times\frac{4\pi}{3}a^3 = 4\pi a^3\ \checkmark$$

## Page 128 — Divergence Theorem: Volume Integral Derivation

**Divergence Theorem / Volume Integral**

**Try:** $\displaystyle\int_V \nabla\cdot\vec{F}(\vec{r})\,dV$ inside a rectangular box with side lengths $dx$, $dy$, $dz$

*(Diagram: Rectangular box with corners labeled and dimensions $dx$, $dy$, $dz$.)*

$$\nabla\cdot\vec{F} = \frac{\partial F_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial F_y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial F_z}{\partial z}$$

$$\frac{\partial F_x}{\partial x} = \frac{F_x(x_1,y_0,z_0) - F_x(x_0,y_0,z_0)}{dx}$$

$$\frac{\partial F_y}{\partial y} = \frac{F_y(x_0,y_1,z_0) - F_y(x_0,y_0,z_0)}{dy}$$

$$\frac{\partial F_z}{\partial z} = \frac{F_z(x_0,y_0,z_1) - F_z(x_0,y_0,z_0)}{dz}$$

$$\nabla\cdot\vec{F}\,dx\,dy\,dz = F_x(x_1,y_0,z_0)\,dy\,dz - F_x(x_0,y_0,z_0)\,dy\,dz$$
$$\quad + F_y(x_0,y_1,z_0)\,dx\,dz - F_y(x_0,y_0,z_0)\,dx\,dz$$
$$\quad + F_z(x_0,y_0,z_1)\,dx\,dy - F_z(x_0,y_0,z_0)\,dx\,dy$$

## Page 129 — Divergence Theorem: Surface Integral Derivation

**Divergence Theorem / Surface Integral**

**Try:** $\displaystyle\oint_S\vec{F}(\vec{r})\cdot d\vec{S}$ over the surface of a rectangular box with side lengths $dx$, $dy$, $dz$

*(Diagram: Rectangular box showing the six faces with outward normal directions.)*

$$\oint_S \vec{F}(\vec{r})\cdot d\vec{S}$$
$$= \vec{F}(x_1,y_0,z_0)\cdot dy\,dz\hat{x} + \vec{F}(x_0,y_0,z_0)\cdot dy\,dz(-\hat{x})$$
$$+ \vec{F}(x_0,y_1,z_0)\cdot dx\,dz\hat{y} + \vec{F}(x_0,y_0,z_0)\cdot dx\,dz(-\hat{y})$$
$$+ \vec{F}(x_0,y_0,z_1)\cdot dx\,dy\hat{z} + \vec{F}(x_0,y_0,z_0)\cdot dx\,dy(-\hat{z})$$

$$\oint_S\vec{F}(\vec{r})\cdot d\vec{S} = F_x(x_1,y_0,z_0)\,dy\,dz - F_x(x_0,y_0,z_0)\,dy\,dz$$
$$+ F_y(x_0,y_1,z_0)\,dx\,dz - F_y(x_0,y_0,z_0)\,dx\,dz$$
$$+ F_z(x_0,y_0,z_1)\,dx\,dy - F_z(x_0,y_0,z_0)\,dx\,dy$$

In a space where the electric field is $\vec{E}(\vec{r}) = a\vec{r}$, find the total electric flux through the surface of a cube with side length $b$ as shown.

## Page 133 — Comparison: Coulomb's Law vs. Gauss's Law

**Coulomb's Law vs. Gauss's Law**

| Coulomb's Law | Gauss's Law |
|---|---|
| $\vec{E}(\vec{r}) = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\displaystyle\int_V dv'\,\dfrac{\rho(\vec{r}')}{|\vec{r}-\vec{r}'|^2}\dfrac{\vec{r}-\vec{r}'}{|\vec{r}-\vec{r}'|}$ | $\Phi_E = \dfrac{q}{\epsilon_0}$ $\Leftrightarrow$ $\displaystyle\oint_S\vec{E}(\vec{r})\cdot d\vec{S} = \dfrac{1}{\epsilon_0}\int_V\rho(\vec{r})\,dv$ |
| Right-hand side integral is over **all space** | Left-hand side integral is over the **Gaussian surface** |
| Given charge density $\rho(\vec{r})$, the right-hand side can always be integrated to find the electric field | Right-hand side: Gaussian surface can be any shape — left-hand side: Gaussian surface interior |
| | The Gaussian surface can be drawn anywhere — how it is drawn determines whether Gauss's law applies |
| | **Only applicable when special symmetry in $\rho(\vec{r})$ allows a suitable Gaussian surface to be drawn** |

---

## Page 134 — Deriving Gauss's Law from Coulomb's Law (Step 1)

**From Coulomb's Law to Gauss's Law**

**Gauss's Law:** $\displaystyle\oint_S\vec{E}(\vec{r})\cdot d\vec{S} = \frac{1}{\epsilon_0}\int_V\rho(\vec{r})\,dv$

Substitute Coulomb's law for $\vec{E}(\vec{r})$:

$$\vec{E}(\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int_V dv'\,\frac{\rho(\vec{r}')}{|\vec{r}-\vec{r}'|^2}\frac{\vec{r}-\vec{r}'}{|\vec{r}-\vec{r}'|}$$

**Remember!** $\nabla\!\left(\dfrac{1}{|\vec{r}-\vec{r}'|}\right) = -\dfrac{\vec{r}-\vec{r}'}{|\vec{r}-\vec{r}'|^3} = -\dfrac{1}{|\vec{r}-\vec{r}'|^2}\dfrac{\vec{r}-\vec{r}'}{|\vec{r}-\vec{r}'|}$

$$\Rightarrow\quad \vec{E}(\vec{r}) = -\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int_V dv'\,\rho(\vec{r}')\nabla\!\left(\frac{1}{|\vec{r}-\vec{r}'|}\right)$$

Take the divergence of both sides:

$$\nabla\cdot\vec{E}(\vec{r}) = -\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int_V dv'\,\rho(\vec{r}')\nabla\cdot\nabla\!\left(\frac{1}{|\vec{r}-\vec{r}'|}\right)$$

$$= -\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int_V dv'\,\rho(\vec{r}')\nabla^2\!\left(\frac{1}{|\vec{r}-\vec{r}'|}\right)$$

---

## Page 135 — Deriving Gauss's Law from Coulomb's Law (Step 2)

**From Coulomb's Law to Gauss's Law (continued)**

$$\nabla\cdot\vec{E}(\vec{r}) = -\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int_V dv'\,\rho(\vec{r}')\nabla^2\!\left(\frac{1}{|\vec{r}-\vec{r}'|}\right)$$

**Remember!** $\nabla^2\!\left(\dfrac{1}{|\vec{r}-\vec{r}'|}\right) = -4\pi\delta^3(\vec{r}-\vec{r}')$

$$\Rightarrow\quad \nabla\cdot\vec{E}(\vec{r}) = -\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int_V dv'\,\rho(\vec{r}')\left(-4\pi\delta^3(\vec{r}-\vec{r}')\right)$$

$$= \frac{1}{\epsilon_0}\int_V dv'\,\rho(\vec{r}')\,\delta^3(\vec{r}-\vec{r}')$$

$$= \frac{\rho(\vec{r})}{\epsilon_0}$$

---

## Page 136 — Deriving Integral Gauss's Law from Differential Form (Step 1)

**From Integral Gauss's Law to Differential Gauss's Law**

Starting from **Gauss's Law** (integral form):

$$\oint_S\vec{E}(\vec{r})\cdot d\vec{S} = \frac{1}{\epsilon_0}\int_V\rho(\vec{r})\,dv$$

where $S$ is an arbitrary Gaussian surface and $V$ is the volume enclosed by $S$.

Apply the **Divergence Theorem** to the left-hand side:

$$\oint_S\vec{E}(\vec{r})\cdot d\vec{S} = \int_V \nabla\cdot\vec{E}(\vec{r})\,dv = \frac{1}{\epsilon_0}\int_V\rho(\vec{r})\,dv$$

$$\Rightarrow\quad \int_V\!\left(\nabla\cdot\vec{E}(\vec{r}) - \frac{\rho(\vec{r})}{\epsilon_0}\right)dv = 0$$

---

## Page 137 — Deriving the Differential Form of Gauss's Law (Final Step)

**From Integral Gauss's Law to Differential Gauss's Law (continued)**

$$\int_V\!\left(\nabla\cdot\vec{E}(\vec{r}) - \frac{\rho(\vec{r})}{\epsilon_0}\right)dv = 0$$

This equation holds for an **arbitrary volume $V$** defined in space.

Since the integrand must be zero for any choice of $V$, the integrand itself must be zero everywhere:

$$\Rightarrow\quad \nabla\cdot\vec{E}(\vec{r}) - \frac{\rho(\vec{r})}{\epsilon_0} = 0$$

$$\Rightarrow\quad \boxed{\nabla\cdot\vec{E}(\vec{r}) = \frac{\rho(\vec{r})}{\epsilon_0}} \qquad \text{(Differential form of Gauss's Law)}$$

---

## Page 138 — Summary: Coulomb's Law and Gauss's Law

**Summary**

**Coulomb's Law:**

$$\vec{E}(\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int_V dv'\,\frac{\rho(\vec{r}')}{|\vec{r}-\vec{r}'|^2}\frac{\vec{r}-\vec{r}'}{|\vec{r}-\vec{r}'|}$$

**Integral form of Gauss's Law:**

$$\oint_S \vec{E}(\vec{r})\cdot d\vec{S} = \frac{1}{\epsilon_0}\int_V\rho(\vec{r})\,dv$$

**Differential form of Gauss's Law:**

$$\nabla\cdot\vec{E}(\vec{r}) = \frac{\rho(\vec{r})}{\epsilon_0}$$
