## Page 6 (Slide 72) — Relativistic Mass, Momentum, Energy, and Kinetic Energy

When a particle with rest mass $m_0$ moves at velocity $v$:

**Beta and Lorentz factor:**
$$\beta = \frac{v}{c} \qquad \gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\beta^2}}$$

| Quantity | Formula |
|---|---|
| Relativistic mass | $m = m_0\gamma$ |
| Rest-mass energy | $E_0 = m_0 c^2$ |
| Relativistic momentum | $p = mv = m_0\gamma v$ |
| Total energy | $E = mc^2 = m_0\gamma c^2$ |
| Kinetic energy | $K = E - E_0 = m_0(\gamma-1)c^2$ |

**Relation among rest mass, momentum, total energy, and kinetic energy:**

$$E^2 = p^2c^2 + m_0^2c^4$$

$$p^2c^2 = K(K + 2m_0c^2)$$

**For a photon (light quantum): $m_0 = 0 \Rightarrow E = pc$**

## Page 7 (Slide 73) — Compton Scattering — Setting Up the Collision Equations

*(Left: 2D elastic collision diagram; Right: photon–electron collision)*

**Photon:**
$$E_\gamma = hf \qquad p_\gamma = \frac{hf}{c} \qquad K_e = 0 \qquad p_e = 0$$

After collision: photon scatters at angle $\phi$, electron recoils at angle $\theta$

$$E_\gamma' = hf' \qquad p_\gamma' = \frac{hf'}{c} \qquad K_e' = K \qquad p_e' = p$$

**Collision equations:**

$$\frac{hf}{c} = \frac{hf'}{c}\cos\phi + p\cos\theta$$

$$0 = \frac{hf'}{c}\sin\phi - p\sin\theta$$

$$hf = hf' + K$$

---

## Page 8 (Slide 74) — Compton Scattering — Derivation

$$pc\cos\theta = hf - hf'\cos\phi \quad (1)$$
$$pc\sin\theta = hf'\sin\phi \quad (2)$$
$$K = hf - hf' \quad (3)$$
$$p^2c^2 = K(K + 2m_ec^2) \quad (4)$$

Squaring (1) and (2) and adding:

$$p^2c^2 = h^2(f^2 + f'^2 - 2ff'\cos\phi) \quad (5)$$

Substituting (3) into (4):

$$p^2c^2 = h^2(f^2 + f'^2 - 2ff') + 2h(f-f')m_ec^2 \quad (6)$$

Setting (5) = (6):

$$\boxed{f - f' = \frac{h}{m_ec^2}ff'(1-\cos\phi)}$$

---

## Page 9 (Slide 75) — Compton Scattering Formula

$$f - f' = \frac{h}{m_ec^2}ff'(1-\cos\phi)$$

Using $c = \lambda f$:

$$\frac{c}{\lambda} - \frac{c}{\lambda'} = \frac{h}{m_ec^2}\cdot\frac{c^2}{\lambda\lambda'}(1-\cos\phi)$$

$$\frac{\lambda'-\lambda}{\lambda\lambda'} = \frac{h}{m_ec^2}\cdot\frac{1}{\lambda\lambda'}(1-\cos\phi)$$

$$\boxed{\lambda' - \lambda = \frac{h}{m_ec}(1-\cos\phi)} \quad \textbf{Compton Scattering Formula}$$

The fact that the wavelength changes after light collides with an electron means that, if light were a wave, it could never change wavelength in the same medium. Therefore, the result of Compton scattering is decisive evidence that light undoubtedly has particle properties.

---

## Page 10 (Slide 76) — Compton Wavelength

$$\lambda' - \lambda = \frac{h}{m_ec}(1-\cos\phi) \qquad \lambda_C = \frac{h}{m_ec}$$

→ **$\lambda_C$ is called the Compton wavelength of the target particle**

**Compton wavelength of the electron** ($m_ec^2 = 0.511\ \text{MeV}$, $\hbar c = 197.3\ \text{eV·nm}$):

$$\lambda_C = \frac{h}{m_ec} = 2\pi\frac{\hbar c}{m_ec^2} = 2\times3.14\times\frac{197.3}{0.511\times10^6}\ \text{nm}$$

$$= 2.43\times10^{-3}\ \text{nm} = 2.43\ \text{pm}$$

The electron's Compton wavelength falls in the X-ray range. The Compton scattering effect for electrons can be observed well when X-rays are used.

## Page 18 (Slide 84) — Energy and Momentum of a Photon / Frequency and Wavelength

**Always valid equation:** $E^2 = p^2c^2 + m_0^2c^4$

**Photon:** $m_0 = 0 \Rightarrow E = pc$ $\qquad$ (using $c = \lambda f$)

**Energy quantization condition:** $E = hf$

**Photon momentum and wavelength:**

$$p = \frac{E}{c} = \frac{hf}{c} = \frac{h}{\lambda}$$

$$\boxed{\lambda = \frac{h}{p}}$$

**de Broglie proposed applying this equation to material particles as well.**

## Page 19 (Slide 85) — de Broglie's Matter-Wave Proposal

A material particle with rest mass $m$ moving at velocity $v$ behaves as a matter wave with wavelength $\lambda$ as follows:

**For photons:** relation between momentum $p$ and wavelength $\lambda$: $\lambda = \dfrac{h}{p}$

**Momentum of a material particle:** $p = \gamma mv$, where $\gamma = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-\beta^2}}$, $\beta = \dfrac{v}{c}$

**de Broglie wavelength:**

$$\lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{h}{\gamma mv}$$

---

## Page 20 (Slide 86) — Example

Find the de Broglie wavelength in each of the two cases: (a) a baseball of mass 150 g flying at 150 km/h, and (b) an electron moving at 0.1c.

**(a) de Broglie wavelength of the baseball** ($h = 6.63\times10^{-34}\ \text{J·s}$):

$$\lambda = \frac{h}{mv} = \frac{6.63\times10^{-34}}{0.15\times\left(150\times\dfrac{1000}{3600}\right)}\ \text{m} = 1.061\times10^{-34}\ \text{m}$$

**(b) de Broglie wavelength of the electron** ($\hbar c = 197.3\ \text{eV·nm}$, $m_ec^2 = 0.511\ \text{MeV}$):

$$\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\beta^2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-0.1^2}} = 1.005$$

$$\lambda = \frac{h}{\gamma m_e v} = 2\pi\frac{\hbar c}{\gamma m_ec^2}\cdot\frac{c}{v} = 2\times3.14\times\frac{197.3}{1.005\times(0.511\times10^6)\times0.1}\ \text{nm}$$

$$= 2.413\times10^{-2}\ \text{nm}$$

## Page 21 (Slide 87) — Bohr's Assumption ③ and the de Broglie Wavelength

**Bohr's assumption ③:** The allowed angular momentum of electrons in stable orbits is $L = n\hbar$ only

**Angular momentum of an electron in the n-th orbit:**

$$L = m_e v r_n = n\hbar = n\frac{h}{2\pi}$$

**Circumference of the n-th orbit:**

$$2\pi r_n = n\frac{h}{m_e v} = n\frac{h}{p} = n\lambda$$

**The circumference of a stable orbit equals an integer multiple of the de Broglie wavelength!!**

## Page 28 (Slide 94) — Newtonian Mechanics

**Newton's equation of motion:** $F(x) = m\dfrac{d^2x}{dt^2}$ $\Rightarrow$ solve for $x(t)$

**Characteristics of Newtonian mechanics:**

- $x(t)$ gives the past, present, and future positions of an object all at once
- $x(t)$ contains all information about the object
- From $x(t)$ one can find the velocity, momentum, potential energy, etc. — all physical quantities of the object

**All physical quantities of the object are determined exactly.**

## Page 30 (Slide 96) — Uncertainty

**Mean (average):**

$$\langle x \rangle = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i$$

**Uncertainty $\Rightarrow$ Standard deviation:**

$$\Delta x = \sqrt{\langle (x - \langle x\rangle)^2 \rangle} = \sqrt{\langle x^2 \rangle - \langle x\rangle^2}$$

*(Graph: normal distribution showing ±1σ, ±2σ, ±3σ regions with percentage probabilities 34.1%, 13.6%, 2.1%, 0.1%)*

$$\langle (x-\langle x\rangle)^2\rangle = \langle x^2 - 2x\langle x\rangle + \langle x\rangle^2\rangle$$
$$= \langle x^2\rangle - 2\langle x\rangle\langle x\rangle + \langle x\rangle^2 = \langle x^2\rangle - \langle x\rangle^2$$

---

## Page 31 (Slide 97) — Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle

**Heisenberg's uncertainty principle:**

$$\Delta x\,\Delta p_x \geq \frac{\hbar}{2} \qquad \Delta y\,\Delta p_y \geq \frac{\hbar}{2} \qquad \Delta z\,\Delta p_z \geq \frac{\hbar}{2}$$

This is NOT the product of the uncertainty in position and the uncertainty in momentum being zero.

**Position and momentum cannot be known exactly at the same time.**

This property appears necessarily because of the dual particle-wave nature of entities existing in the microscopic world.

Heisenberg published the matrix mechanics theory of quantum mechanics in 1925, and in 1927 derived the uncertainty principle.

→ **The uncertainty principle is naturally explained by quantum mechanics**

## Page 32 (Slide 98) — Describing a Particle as a Wave

$$\psi(x,t) = Ae^{i(kx-\omega t)} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \text{represents a particle with momentum } p = \hbar k \text{ and energy } E = \hbar\omega$$

$$\hbar k = \frac{h}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{2\pi}{\lambda} = \frac{h}{\lambda} = p \qquad \hbar\omega = \frac{h}{2\pi}\cdot 2\pi f = hf = E$$

*(Diagram: infinite sinusoidal wave — same amplitude everywhere)*

Momentum is determined to one exact value → $\Delta p_x = 0$

The probability of being found at any position is the same → $\Delta x = \infty$

$$\Delta x\,\Delta p_x \neq 0$$

## Page 35 (Slide 101) — Dimensions of Planck's Constant and the Extended Uncertainty Principle

## Page 44 (Slide 110) — The Wave Equation

**Equation representing a wave:** $\psi(x,t) = Ae^{i(kx-\omega t)}$ $\quad$ ($k = \dfrac{2\pi}{\lambda}$, $\omega = \dfrac{2\pi}{T}$)

**Derivative with respect to $x$:**

$$\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x} = ikAe^{i(kx-\omega t)} = ik\psi \qquad \frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x^2} = -k^2Ae^{i(kx-\omega t)} = -k^2\psi$$

**Derivative with respect to $t$:**

$$\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t} = -i\omega Ae^{i(kx-\omega t)} = -i\omega\psi \qquad \frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial t^2} = -\omega^2Ae^{i(kx-\omega t)} = -\omega^2\psi$$

$$\frac{1}{k^2}\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x^2} - \frac{1}{\omega^2}\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial t^2} = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \boxed{\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x^2} - \frac{1}{v^2}\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial t^2} = 0} \quad \textbf{Wave Equation}$$

---

## Page 45 (Slide 111) — Schrödinger Equation — Derivation

**Equation representing a matter wave:** $\psi(x,t) = Ae^{i(kx-\omega t)}$ $\quad$ ($k = \dfrac{p}{\hbar}$, $\omega = \dfrac{E}{\hbar}$)

**Derivative with respect to $x$:**

$$\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x} = ikAe^{i(kx-\omega t)} = ik\psi \qquad \frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x^2} = -k^2\psi = -\frac{p^2}{\hbar^2}\psi$$

$$\Rightarrow \quad p^2\psi = -\hbar^2\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x^2}$$

**Derivative with respect to $t$:**

$$\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t} = -i\omega Ae^{i(kx-\omega t)} = -i\omega\psi = -i\frac{E}{\hbar}\psi$$

$$\Rightarrow \quad E\psi = i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}$$

---

## Page 46 (Slide 112) — Schrödinger Equation

$$p^2\psi = -\hbar^2\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x^2} \qquad E\psi = i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}$$

**Newton's equation of motion:** $F(x) = m\dfrac{d^2x}{dt^2}$

**Mechanical energy conservation:** $\dfrac{p^2}{2m} + U = E$

$$\frac{p^2}{2m}\psi + U\psi = E\psi$$

$$\Rightarrow \quad \boxed{-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x^2} + U\psi = i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}} \quad \textbf{Schrödinger Equation}$$

---

## Page 47 (Slide 113) — Matrix Mechanics and Wave Mechanics

**Heisenberg's matrix mechanics equation:**

$$\begin{pmatrix}e_1+U_{11} & U_{12} & \cdots \\ U_{21} & e_2+U_{22} & \cdots \\ \vdots & & \ddots\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\psi_1\\\psi_2\\\vdots\end{pmatrix} = E\begin{pmatrix}\psi_1\\\psi_2\\\vdots\end{pmatrix}$$

**Schrödinger's wave mechanics equation:**

$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}\psi(x) + U(x)\psi(x) = i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\psi(x)$$

**Schrödinger proved in 1927 that matrix mechanics and wave mechanics are identical in content.** ▶

## Page 56 (Slide 122) — Basis Condition → Orthonormal Property

**Scalar product of two vectors:** $\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b} = ab\cos\theta = 0$ $\Rightarrow$ vectors $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ are perpendicular

$$\hat{x}_1\cdot\hat{x}_1 = 1 \quad \hat{x}_1\cdot\hat{x}_2 = 0 \quad \hat{x}_1\cdot\hat{x}_3 = 0$$
$$\hat{x}_2\cdot\hat{x}_1 = 0 \quad \hat{x}_2\cdot\hat{x}_2 = 1 \quad \hat{x}_2\cdot\hat{x}_3 = 0$$
$$\hat{x}_3\cdot\hat{x}_1 = 0 \quad \hat{x}_3\cdot\hat{x}_2 = 0 \quad \hat{x}_3\cdot\hat{x}_3 = 1$$

$$\boxed{\hat{x}_i\cdot\hat{x}_j = \delta_{ij}} \qquad \textbf{Orthonormal property}$$

---

## Page 57 (Slide 123) — Kronecker Delta and Dirac Delta Function

| **Kronecker delta $\delta_{ij}$** | **Dirac delta function $\delta(x-a)$** |
|---|---|
| $\delta_{ij} = \begin{cases}1 & \text{when }i=j\\0 & \text{when }i\neq j\end{cases}$ | $\delta(x-a) = \begin{cases}0 & \text{when }x\neq a\\\infty & \text{when }x=a\end{cases}$ |
| Property of $\delta_{ij}$: | Property of $\delta(x-a)$: |
| $\displaystyle\sum_i \delta_{ij} = 1$ | $\displaystyle\int dx\,\delta(x-a) = 1$ |
| $\displaystyle\sum_i f_i\delta_{ij} = f_j$ | $\displaystyle\int dx\,f(x)\delta(x-a) = f(a)$ |

*(Reference: 15J)*

## Page 58 (Slide 124) — Basis Condition → Completeness Property

$$\hat{x}_i\cdot\hat{x}_j = \delta_{ij} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \sum_{i=1}^{3}\hat{x}_i\hat{x}_i = \mathbf{1}$$

**Orthonormal property** $\qquad$ **Completeness property**

$$\vec{F} = \sum_{i=1}^{3}\hat{x}_i F_i \quad \Rightarrow \quad \hat{x}_j\cdot\vec{F} = \sum_{i=1}^{3}\hat{x}_j\cdot\hat{x}_i F_i = \sum_{i=1}^{3}\delta_{ij}F_i = F_j$$

$$\vec{F} = \sum_{i=1}^{3}\hat{x}_i\hat{x}_i\cdot\vec{F} = \left(\sum_{i=1}^{3}\hat{x}_i\hat{x}_i\right)\cdot\vec{F} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \sum_{i=1}^{3}\hat{x}_i\hat{x}_i = \mathbf{1}$$

**Completeness property**

## Page 59 (Slide 125) — Summary (23J)

Let us call the vectors of the macroscopic world **arrow vectors**.

The space that represents arrow vectors is **position space**.

Position space is defined by its basis.

The basis of position space must satisfy the **orthonormal property**.

A basis satisfying the orthonormal property also satisfies the **completeness property**.

Simply writing $\vec{F}$ for an arrow vector is an abstract expression.

To use it in calculations, the arrow vector must be represented in terms of a basis. ▶

## Page 65 (Slide 131) — Representation in Terms of Basis — Macroscopic World

**Macroscopic world:**

Arrow vector $\lvert F\rangle$ — abstract expression; cannot be used directly in calculations

Defining basis $\lvert x_i\rangle$ and using $\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{3}\lvert x_i\rangle\langle x_i\rvert = \mathbf{1}$:

$$\lvert F\rangle = \mathbf{1}\lvert F\rangle = \sum_{i=1}^{3}\lvert x_i\rangle\langle x_i\lvert F\rangle = \sum_{i=1}^{3}\lvert x_i\rangle F_i$$

The number $\langle x_i\lvert F\rangle = F_i$ represents arrow vector $\lvert F\rangle$.

When the basis $\lvert x_i\rangle$ changes, the numbers $\langle x_i\lvert F\rangle = F_i$ also change.

## Page 66 (Slide 132) — Representation in Terms of Basis — Microscopic World (Ket Vector)

**Microscopic world:**

Ket vector $\lvert\phi\rangle$ — abstract expression; cannot be used directly in calculations

Defining basis $\lvert\alpha_n\rangle$ and using $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}\lvert\alpha_n\rangle\langle\alpha_n\rvert = \mathbf{1}$:

$$\lvert\phi\rangle = \mathbf{1}\lvert\phi\rangle = \sum_{n=1}^{N}\lvert\alpha_n\rangle\langle\alpha_n\lvert\phi\rangle = \sum_{n=1}^{N}\lvert\alpha_n\rangle\phi_n$$

The number $\langle\alpha_n\lvert\phi\rangle = \phi_n$ represents ket vector $\lvert\phi\rangle$.

When the basis $\lvert\alpha_n\rangle$ changes, the numbers $\langle\alpha_n\lvert\phi\rangle = \phi_n$ also change.

## Page 67 (Slide 133) — Representation in Terms of Basis — Operator

**Microscopic world:**

Operator $\hat{O}$ — abstract expression; cannot be used directly in calculations

Defining basis $\lvert\alpha_n\rangle$ and using $\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{N}\lvert\alpha_n\rangle\langle\alpha_n\rvert = \mathbf{1}$:

$$\hat{O} = \mathbf{1}\hat{O}\mathbf{1} = \left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\lvert\alpha_n\rangle\langle\alpha_n\rvert\right)\hat{O}\left(\sum_{n'=1}^{N}\lvert\alpha_{n'}\rangle\langle\alpha_{n'}\rvert\right) = \sum_{n,n'=1}^{N}\lvert\alpha_n\rangle\langle\alpha_n\lvert\hat{O}\lvert\alpha_{n'}\rangle\langle\alpha_{n'}\rvert$$

The number $\langle\alpha_n\lvert\hat{O}\lvert\alpha_{n'}\rangle = O_{nn'}$ represents operator $\hat{O}$ as a **matrix**.

When the basis $\lvert\alpha_n\rangle$ changes, the numbers $\langle\alpha_n\lvert\hat{O}\lvert\alpha_{n'}\rangle = O_{nn'}$ also change.

## Page 70 (Slide 136) — The Microscopic-World Equation of Motion

**Macroscopic-world equation of motion:** $\dfrac{p^2}{2m} + U(x) = E$ — macroscopic objects move so as to conserve mechanical energy

The target object in the microscopic world exists only as an eigenstate of the energy operator.
