## Page 3 — Vibration

**Vibration** — Motion in which the same action repeats around a single position
**Subject** — A single object

*(Diagram: pendulum and eyelid blinking as examples of vibration)*

**Characteristic property of vibration** — Period

**Period $T$** — The time required to complete one cycle of vibration

---
**Frequency $f$** — $f = \dfrac{1}{T}$ → The number of vibrations repeated per unit time

**Angular frequency $\omega$** — $\omega = 2\pi f$ → Frequency multiplied by $2\pi$

---

## Page 4 — Simple Harmonic Oscillation

**Simple Harmonic Oscillation (SHO)**

Vibration in which the displacement varies as a sine or cosine function of time is called simple harmonic oscillation.

$$x(t) = A\sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{T}t\right) = A\sin\omega t$$

*(Diagram: mass-spring system illustrating SHO)*

## Page 5 — Fourier Series

$$F(t) = \frac{A_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[A_n\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi n}{T}t\right) + B_n\sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi n}{T}t\right)\right]$$

*(Annotation: $F(t)$ is labeled "vibration"; the cosine and sine terms are labeled "simple harmonic oscillation")*

> **Any vibration can be expressed as a sum of simple harmonic oscillations.**

## Page 8 — Characteristic Properties of a Wave: Period and Wavelength

**Period $T$** — The time required to complete one cycle of vibration

**Frequency $f$** — $f = \dfrac{1}{T}$ → The number of vibrations repeated per unit time

**Angular frequency $\omega$** — $\omega = 2\pi f$ → Frequency multiplied by $2\pi$

---

**Wavelength $\lambda$** — The distance the wave travels during one period

**Wavenumber (spatial frequency)** $\dfrac{1}{\lambda}$ — The number of waves contained per unit length

**Wave number $k$** — $k = \dfrac{2\pi}{\lambda}$ → Wavenumber multiplied by $2\pi$

## Page 10 — Wave Speed

**Period $T$** — The time for one cycle of vibration

**Wavelength $\lambda$** — The distance the wave travels in one period

**Wave speed $v$** — The speed at which vibration is transmitted in a wave

$$v = \frac{\lambda}{T} = \lambda f = \frac{2\pi}{k}\cdot\frac{\omega}{2\pi} = \frac{\omega}{k}$$

> **As long as the medium of the wave does not change, the wave speed is the same regardless of the period or wavelength.**

- $A$ — Amplitude of the wave; maximum displacement of the medium

## Page 22 — Wave Speed → Phase Velocity (derivation)

$$y(x,t) = A\sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}x - \frac{2\pi}{T}t\right) = A\sin(kx - \omega t)$$

Setting the phase constant: $kx - \omega t = \phi_0 = \text{const}$

Differentiating both sides:

$$k\,dx - \omega\,dt = 0$$

$$\Rightarrow \frac{dx}{dt} = +\frac{\omega}{k} = +v \qquad \text{[Wave traveling in the $+x$ direction]}$$

For a wave traveling in the $-x$ direction:

$$y(x,t) = A\sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}x + \frac{2\pi}{T}t\right) = A\sin(kx + \omega t)$$

$$\Rightarrow \frac{dx}{dt} = -\frac{\omega}{k} = -v \qquad \text{[Wave traveling in the $-x$ direction]}$$

*(The quantity $kx - \omega t$ is the **phase** of the wave)*

**Wave speed:**
The wave speed is the movement of the position where the phase is constant.

## Page 25 — Partial Derivative of a Function of Multiple Variables

**Derivative of a function of one variable → ordinary derivative**

For $y = f(x)$:
$$dy = f'(x)\,dx = \frac{dy}{dx}dx$$

*(Diagram: graph of $y = f(x)$ with a tangent line at point P, showing $dx$ and $dy$)*

---

For $u = f(x, y)$:

$$du = f_x(x,y)\,dx + f_y(x,y)\,dy = \frac{\partial u}{\partial x}dx + \frac{\partial u}{\partial y}dy \qquad \leftarrow \text{partial derivative}$$

---

## Page 26 — Partial Derivatives

Partial derivatives are used when there are two or more independent variables.

Example: $u = x^2 y$

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = 2xy \quad \Rightarrow \quad \text{Treat } y \text{ as a constant and differentiate with respect to } x \text{ only.}$$

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = x^2 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \text{Treat } x \text{ as a constant and differentiate with respect to } y \text{ only.}$$

**Ordinary derivative** $y = f(x)$:
$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{f(x+dx) - f(x)}{dx}$$

**Partial derivative** $u = f(x,y)$:
$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} = \frac{f(x+dx,\,y) - f(x,y)}{dx}$$

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = \frac{f(x,\,y+dy) - f(x,y)}{dy}$$

---

## Page 27 — Chain Rule for Derivatives

When $y = f(x)$ and $x = g(t)$:

$$\Delta y = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}\Delta x, \quad \Delta x = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}\Delta t$$

Since $\Delta x \approx \Delta x$:

$$\Delta y \approx \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}\cdot\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}\Delta t$$

---

$$dy = \frac{dy}{dx}dx, \quad dx = \frac{dx}{dt}dt$$

Since $dx = dx$:

$$dy = \frac{dy}{dx}\cdot\frac{dx}{dt}dt$$

$$\boxed{\frac{dy}{dt} = \frac{dy}{dx}\cdot\frac{dx}{dt}} \qquad \textbf{Chain Rule}$$

---

## Page 28 — Partial Derivative of $y(x,t) = A\sin(kx-\omega t)$ with respect to $x$

$$y(x,t) = A\sin(kx - \omega t) = A\sin\theta(x,t)$$

*(dependent variable $y$; independent variables $x, t$)*

Partial derivative of $y$ with respect to $x$:

$$\frac{\partial y}{\partial x} = \frac{dy}{d\theta}\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial x} = A\cos\theta \cdot k = kA\cos(kx - \omega t)$$

Second partial derivative of $y$ with respect to $x$:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\!\left(\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\right) = \frac{d}{d\theta}\!\left(\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\right)\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial x} = -kA\sin\theta \cdot k = -k^2 A\sin(kx-\omega t)$$

$$\Rightarrow \quad \frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2} = -k^2 A\sin(kx - \omega t)$$

---

## Page 29 — Partial Derivative of $y(x,t) = A\sin(kx-\omega t)$ with respect to $t$

$$y(x,t) = A\sin(kx - \omega t) = A\sin\theta(x,t)$$

Partial derivative of $y$ with respect to $t$:

$$\frac{\partial y}{\partial t} = \frac{dy}{d\theta}\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial t} = A\cos\theta\cdot(-\omega) = -\omega A\cos(kx - \omega t)$$

Second partial derivative of $y$ with respect to $t$:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\!\left(\frac{\partial y}{\partial t}\right) = \frac{d}{d\theta}\!\left(\frac{\partial y}{\partial t}\right)\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial t} = +\omega A\sin\theta\cdot(-\omega) = -\omega^2 A\sin(kx - \omega t)$$

$$\Rightarrow \quad \frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} = -\omega^2 A\sin(kx - \omega t)$$

---

## Page 30 — Combining the Partial Derivatives of $y(x,t)$

$$\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2} = -k^2 A\sin(kx-\omega t) \quad \Rightarrow \quad \frac{1}{k^2}\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2} = -A\sin(kx-\omega t) \quad \cdots (1)$$

$$\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} = -\omega^2 A\sin(kx-\omega t) \quad \Rightarrow \quad \frac{1}{\omega^2}\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} = -A\sin(kx-\omega t) \quad \cdots (2)$$

**(1) $-$ (2):**

$$\frac{1}{k^2}\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2} - \frac{1}{\omega^2}\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2} - \frac{k^2}{\omega^2}\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} = 0$$

Using $v = \dfrac{\lambda}{T} = \lambda f = \dfrac{2\pi}{k}\cdot\dfrac{\omega}{2\pi} = \dfrac{\omega}{k}$:

$$\boxed{\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2} - \frac{1}{v^2}\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} = 0} \qquad \textbf{Wave Equation}$$

---

## Page 31 — The Wave Equation

$$\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2} - \frac{1}{v^2}\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} = 0$$

- Any phenomenon that satisfies a partial differential equation of this form is a wave.
- The wave equation is derived by applying Newton's laws of motion to a continuous medium.
- The characteristic of the wave equation is that it is expressed as the difference between the second partial derivative with respect to the spatial coordinate and the second partial derivative with respect to the time coordinate.
- In the wave equation, when the coefficient of the second partial derivative with respect to the spatial coordinate is 1, the reciprocal of the coefficient of the second partial derivative with respect to time equals the square of the wave speed.

---

## Page 32 — Maxwell's Theoretical Prediction of Electromagnetic Waves (UK, 1831–1879)

**Maxwell's Equations:**

$$\nabla\cdot\vec{E} = \frac{\rho(\vec{r})}{\varepsilon_0} \quad (1) \qquad \nabla\cdot\vec{B} = 0 \quad (2) \qquad \nabla\times\vec{E} = -\frac{\partial\vec{B}}{\partial t} \quad (3) \qquad \nabla\times\vec{B} = \mu_0\left(\vec{J}(\vec{r}) + \varepsilon_0\frac{\partial\vec{E}}{\partial t}\right) \quad (4)$$

*(Labels: (1) Gauss's law for electric field; (2) Gauss's law for magnetic field; (3) Faraday's law; (4) Ampere's law)*

Maxwell's equations valid in free space where $\rho(\vec{r}) = 0$ and $\vec{J}(\vec{r}) = 0$:

$$\nabla\cdot\vec{E} = 0 \quad (1) \qquad \nabla\cdot\vec{B} = 0 \quad (2) \qquad \nabla\times\vec{E} = -\frac{\partial\vec{B}}{\partial t} \quad (3) \qquad \nabla\times\vec{B} = \mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial\vec{E}}{\partial t} \quad (4)$$

Taking the curl of both sides of equation (3):

$$\nabla\times(\nabla\times\vec{E}) = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\vec{E}) - \nabla^2\vec{E} \quad (5) \qquad \text{using } \vec{A}\times(\vec{B}\times\vec{C}) = \vec{B}(\vec{A}\cdot\vec{C}) - (\vec{A}\cdot\vec{B})\vec{C}$$

Left side of (5): $\nabla\times(\nabla\times\vec{E}) = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\vec{E}) - \nabla^2\vec{E} = -\nabla^2\vec{E} \quad (6)$

Right side of (5): $-\nabla\times\dfrac{\partial\vec{B}}{\partial t} = -\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}\nabla\times\vec{B} = -\mu_0\varepsilon_0\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}\dfrac{\partial\vec{E}}{\partial t} \quad (7)$

---

## Page 33 — Maxwell's Theoretical Prediction (continued)

Left side of (5): $\nabla\times(\nabla\times\vec{E}) = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\vec{E}) - \nabla^2\vec{E} = -\nabla^2\vec{E} \quad (6)$

Right side of (5): $-\nabla\times\dfrac{\partial\vec{B}}{\partial t} = -\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}\nabla\times\vec{B} = -\mu_0\varepsilon_0\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}\dfrac{\partial\vec{E}}{\partial t} \quad (7)$

---

From equations (6) and (7):

$$\nabla^2\vec{E} - \mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial^2\vec{E}}{\partial t^2} = 0 \quad (8) \qquad \textbf{3-dimensional wave equation}$$

where $\nabla^2 = \dfrac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2} + \dfrac{\partial^2}{\partial y^2} + \dfrac{\partial^2}{\partial z^2}$

If $\vec{E}(x,y,z) = \vec{E}(x)$, then $\nabla^2\vec{E}(x) = \dfrac{\partial^2\vec{E}(x)}{\partial x^2} + \dfrac{\partial^2\vec{E}(x)}{\partial y^2} + \dfrac{\partial^2\vec{E}(x)}{\partial z^2} = \dfrac{\partial^2\vec{E}(x)}{\partial x^2}$

Equation (8) becomes:

$$\frac{\partial^2\vec{E}}{\partial x^2} - \mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial^2\vec{E}}{\partial t^2} = 0$$

---

## Page 34 — Maxwell's Prediction (Historical Notes)

$$\frac{\partial^2\vec{E}}{\partial x^2} - \mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial^2\vec{E}}{\partial t^2} = 0 \qquad \frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2} - \frac{1}{v^2}\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} = 0$$

*(Maxwell's derived equation on the left; wave equation on the right)*

- England's Maxwell compared the two equations above in 1865, predicted that electric fields form waves (electromagnetic waves), and predicted the speed of electromagnetic waves to be $v = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}$.
- Germany's Hertz received the task of generating electromagnetic waves from his doctoral supervisor Helmholtz in 1879, and after going to Karlsruhe University in 1885, successfully generated and detected electromagnetic waves in 1888.
- Italy's Marconi successfully transmitted a wireless signal across the Atlantic Ocean in 1901 and won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1909 as the sole inventor.
