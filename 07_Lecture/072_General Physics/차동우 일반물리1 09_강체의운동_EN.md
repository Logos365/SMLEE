## Page 3 — Ideal Objects in Physics

**Rigid Body** — An object in which the distance between any two points within the body never changes.

**Ideal Fluid** — (Fluid $\Rightarrow$ a term that refers to both gases and liquids together)

> A fluid that flows without change in density, has no energy loss due to viscosity, and whose streamlines (flow lines) do not intersect each other.

> A gas in which the volume of the particles that make up the gas is zero, and the particles interact with each other only through elastic collisions.

## Page 6 — Degree of Freedom

**Degree of freedom:** The number of mutually independent coordinates needed to describe the motion of an object

- A single point particle (with no size) moving in space
  ⟹ **3**

- $N$ point particles (with no size) moving in space
  ⟹ $\boldsymbol{3N}$

---

## Page 7 — Constraint Condition

**Constraint condition** — An equation that the coordinates describing the motion of an object must satisfy

**One equation among coordinates** ⟹ **represents a surface**

- Example) $z = 0$ ⟹ $xy$ plane

- $r = a$ ⟹ spherical surface with radius $a$

**Simultaneously satisfying two equations among coordinates** ⟹ **represents a line**

- Example) $y = 0$ and $z = 0$ satisfied simultaneously ⟹ $x$-axis

---

## Page 8 — Constraints and Degrees of Freedom

When $N$ point particles (with no size) move in space while satisfying $f$ constraint conditions, the degrees of freedom $n$ are:

$$n = 3N - f$$

*(Diagram: two red spheres connected by a rigid rod)*

When two point masses are connected by a rigid, massless rod of fixed length and move in space, the degrees of freedom $n$ are:

$$n = 6 - 1 = 5$$

## Page 9 — Degrees of Freedom of a Rigid Body

**Rigid Body** — An object in which the distance between any two points within the body never changes.

*(Diagram: A rigid body (irregular polygon shape) containing 4 blue points connected by red lines forming a complete graph, illustrating the constraints between points.)*

- **Degrees of freedom of 4 arbitrarily chosen points in a rigid body** $\Rightarrow$ $4 \times 3 = 12$

- **Constraint conditions on the distances between 4 points** $\Rightarrow$ $6$

- **Degrees of freedom of 4 points** $\Rightarrow$ $12 - 6 = 6$

## Page 13 — Moment of Inertia of a Point Mass

$$I = mr^2$$

*(Diagram: Three point masses (balls) attached to rotating vertical axes at increasing distances from the axis. The discs represent the circular path of rotation. The moment of inertia increases from left to right as the distance from the axis increases.)*

$$I_1 \quad < \quad I_2 \quad < \quad I_3$$

---

**To find the moment of inertia:**

1. **Define the axis of rotation.**

2. **The moment of inertia is $I = mr^2$, where $r$ is the distance from the axis of rotation to the mass.**

## Page 14 — Moment of Inertia of Multiple Point Masses

*(Diagram: Three point masses $m_1$, $m_2$, $m_3$ connected by rigid rods, each at perpendicular distances $r_1$, $r_2$, $r_3$ respectively from a vertical rotation axis on the left.)*

**Moment of inertia of multiple point masses comprising a rigid body**

⟹ **Add up the moment of inertia of each point mass.**

**Three point masses**

$$\Rightarrow \quad I = m_1 r_1^2 + m_2 r_2^2 + m_3 r_3^2$$

***n* point masses**

$$\Rightarrow \quad I = \sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i r_i^2$$

**Rotation axis**

## Page 15 — Rotation Axis and Moment of Inertia

**The moment of inertia of the same object changes when the rotation axis changes.**

*(Diagram: Two point masses, each of mass $m$, connected by a rod of length $L$. The left mass sits on the rotation axis $A$ (a vertical axis), and the right mass is at a distance $L$ from the axis.)*

$$I_A = m \times 0 + mL^2 = mL^2$$

## Page 16 — Rotation Axis and Moment of Inertia

**The moment of inertia of the same object changes when the rotation axis changes.**

*(Diagram: Two masses m connected by a rod of total length L. Axis A passes through the left mass, axis B passes through the center of the rod. Each mass is a distance L/2 from axis B, and the left mass is at distance 0 from axis A while the right mass is at distance L from axis A.)*

$$I_A = m \times 0 + mL^2 = \boxed{mL^2}$$

$$I_B = m\!\left(\frac{L}{2}\right)^{\!2} + m\!\left(\frac{L}{2}\right)^{\!2} = \boxed{\frac{1}{2}mL^2}$$

## Page 17 — Density of a Continuous Body

**Uniform mass distribution** &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; **Non-uniform mass distribution**

**Linear density** &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; $\lambda = \dfrac{M}{L}$ &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; $\lambda = \dfrac{dm}{dx}$ $\Rightarrow$ $dm = \lambda \, dx$

**Surface density** &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; $\sigma = \dfrac{M}{S}$ &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; $\sigma = \dfrac{dm}{ds}$ $\Rightarrow$ $dm = \sigma \, ds$

**Volume density** &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; $\rho = \dfrac{M}{V}$ &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp; $\rho = \dfrac{dm}{dv}$ $\Rightarrow$ $dm = \rho \, dv$

## Page 18 — Moment of Inertia of a Continuous Body

**Collection of Point Masses** &emsp;&emsp;&emsp;&emsp; **Continuous Body**

*(Diagram: On the left, a vertical axis (rotation axis) with three point masses $m_i$ connected by lines, each at perpendicular distance $r_i$ from the axis, indicated by dashed horizontal lines. On the right, a continuous body of irregular shape on a coordinate system, with a position vector $\vec{r}$ pointing to a small mass element $dm$, and $h$ indicating the perpendicular distance from the rotation axis to that element. The mass element is labeled $dm = \rho(\vec{r})\,dv$.)*

$$I = \sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i r_i^2$$

$$I = \int h^2\, dm = \int_V h^2 \rho(\vec{r})\, dv$$

## Page 19 — Rod

$$I = \int h^2 dm = \int_V h^2 \rho(\vec{r})\, dv \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad I = \sum_{i=1}^{n} m_i r_i^2$$

**Moment of inertia of a rod of length $L$ with uniformly distributed mass $M$**

*(Diagram: A horizontal rod of length $L$ fixed at its left end (rotation axis). A small element of width $dx$ is highlighted at position $x$ along the rod.)*

**Linear mass density of the rod:** $\quad \lambda = \dfrac{M}{L}$

$$dm = \lambda\, dx = \frac{M}{L}\, dx$$

**Rotation axis**

$$I = \int x^2\, dm = \int_0^L x^2 \lambda\, dx = \frac{M}{L} \int_0^L x^2\, dx$$

$$= \frac{M}{L} \left(\frac{x^3}{3}\right)_0^L = \boxed{\frac{1}{3}ML^2}$$

---

## Page 20 — Ring

**Moment of inertia of a ring with uniformly distributed mass $M$ and radius $R$**

*(Diagram: A ring of radius $R$ with a vertical rotation axis through its center. A small arc element $dl = Rd\varphi$ is highlighted, where $d\varphi$ is the subtended angle.)*

**Linear mass density of the ring:**

$$\lambda = \frac{M}{2\pi R}$$

$$dm = \lambda\, dl = \frac{M}{2\pi R} R\, d\varphi = \frac{M}{2\pi} d\varphi$$

$$\Downarrow$$

$$I = \int R^2\, dm = \int_0^{2\pi} R^2 \frac{M}{2\pi}\, d\varphi$$

$$= \frac{1}{2\pi} MR^2 \int_0^{2\pi} d\varphi = \boxed{MR^2}$$

---

## Page 21 — Disk

**Moment of inertia of a disk of radius $R$ with uniformly distributed mass $M$**

*(Diagram: A flat circular disk with a rotation axis passing through its center perpendicular to its face. A thin ring element of radius $r$ and thickness $dr$ is highlighted on the disk.)*

**Surface mass density of the disk:**
$$\sigma = \frac{M}{\pi R^2}$$

**Area of a ring with radius $r$ and thickness $dr$:**
$$ds = 2\pi r \, dr$$

**Mass of the ring:**
$$dm = \sigma \, ds = \frac{M}{\pi R^2} 2\pi r \, dr = \frac{2M}{R^2} r \, dr$$

**Rotation axis** $\Rightarrow$
$$I = \int r^2 \, dm = \int_0^R r^2 \frac{2M}{R^2} r \, dr = \frac{2M}{R^2} \int_0^R r^3 \, dr$$

$$= \frac{2M}{R^2} \left(\frac{r^4}{4}\right)_0^R = \frac{2M}{R^2} \frac{R^4}{4} = \boxed{\dfrac{1}{2}MR^2}$$

## Page 27 — Spherical Shell

**Surface mass density of a spherical shell**

$$\sigma = \frac{M}{4\pi R^2} \qquad x = R\sin\theta \quad ds = 2\pi x\, R\, d\theta$$

$$dm = \sigma\, ds$$

*(Diagram: A circle representing a spherical shell, with radius R drawn at angle θ, a horizontal strip of width ds at height x from the axis, and the angle increment dθ labeled.)*

---

$$I = \int x^2\, dm = \int_0^{\pi} x^2\, \sigma(2\pi x)\, R\, d\theta$$

$$= 2\pi\sigma R \int_0^{\pi} x^3\, d\theta = 2\pi\sigma R^4 \int_0^{\pi} \sin^3\theta\, d\theta$$

---

**Integral evaluation:**

$$\int_0^{\pi} \sin^3\theta\, d\theta = -\int_0^{\pi}(1-\cos^2\theta)\, d(\cos\theta)$$

$$= -\left(\cos\theta - \frac{\cos^3\theta}{3}\right)_0^{\pi} = \frac{4}{3}$$

---

$$I = 2\pi\left(\frac{M}{4\pi R^2}\right)R^4\left(\frac{4}{3}\right)$$

$$\boxed{= \frac{2}{3}MR^2}$$

## Page 28 — Solid Sphere: First Method

**Moment of inertia of a sphere of radius $R$ with uniformly distributed mass $M$**

*(Diagram: A solid sphere centered at the origin with the rotation axis along the $+z$ direction. A thin disk-shaped slice of thickness $dz$ is shown at height $z$ from the center, with radius $x$ in the horizontal direction. The $+x$ axis points to the right.)*

**Volume density of the sphere**
$$\rho = \frac{M}{\dfrac{4\pi}{3}R^3}$$

**Volume of the disk element**
$$dv = \pi x^2 \, dz$$
$$x^2 = R^2 - z^2$$

**Mass of the disk element**
$$dm = \rho \, dv$$

---

## Page 29 — Solid Sphere: First Method

*(Diagram: A solid sphere of radius R centered at the origin, with rotation axis along the z-axis. A thin disk element of thickness $dz$ at height $z$ with radius $x$ is highlighted.)*

**Rotation axis**

$$\rho = \frac{M}{\dfrac{4\pi}{3}R^3} \qquad dv = \pi x^2 dz \qquad x^2 = R^2 - z^2$$

$$dm = \rho\, dv$$

**Moment of inertia of a disk with mass $dm$ and radius $x$:**

$$dI = \frac{1}{2}x^2 dm$$

---

$$dI = \frac{1}{2}x^2 dm = \frac{1}{2}x^2 \rho\, dv = \frac{1}{2}x^2 \rho(\pi x^2)dz = \frac{\pi\rho}{2}(R^2 - z^2)^2 dz = \frac{\pi\rho}{2}(R^4 - 2R^2z^2 + z^4)dz$$

$$\Rightarrow \quad I = \frac{\pi\rho}{2}\int_{-R}^{R}(R^4 - 2R^2z^2 + z^4)dz = \frac{\pi\rho}{2}\left(R^4 z - \frac{2}{3}R^2 z^3 + \frac{1}{5}z^5\right)_{-R}^{R}$$

$$= \frac{\pi\rho}{2}R^5\!\left(1 - \frac{2}{3} + \frac{1}{5} + 1 - \frac{2}{3} + \frac{1}{5}\right) = \frac{\pi}{2}\cdot\frac{3}{4\pi}\cdot\frac{M}{R^3}\cdot R^5\!\left(\frac{16}{15}\right) = \boxed{\dfrac{2}{5}MR^2}$$

---

## Page 31 — Solid Sphere: Second Method

*(Diagram: A solid sphere with rotation axis (vertical dashed line), showing a thin spherical shell of radius $r$ and thickness $dr$. Labeled "rotation axis" at the bottom.)*

$$\rho = \frac{M}{\dfrac{4\pi}{3}R^3} \qquad dv = 4\pi r^2 dr$$

$$dm = \rho\, dv$$

**Moment of inertia of a spherical shell with mass $dm$ and radius $r$**

$$\Longrightarrow \quad dI = \frac{2}{3}r^2\,dm$$

---

$$dI = \frac{2}{3}r^2\,dm = \frac{2}{3}r^2\,\rho\,(4\pi r^2)\,dr = \frac{8\pi}{3}\rho\, r^4\,dr$$

$$\Longrightarrow \quad I = \frac{8\pi}{3}\rho\int_0^R r^4\,dr = \frac{8\pi}{3}\rho\,\frac{R^5}{5} = \frac{8\pi}{15}\!\left(\frac{3M}{4\pi R^3}\right)\!R^5 = \boxed{\dfrac{2}{5}MR^2}$$

## Page 41 — Parallel Axis Theorem

*(Diagram: A cloud-shaped rigid body with center of mass labeled **cm**, a point **A** inside the body, mass element **dm** at position $\vec{r}$ from the origin, $\vec{r}_A$ pointing to point A, and $\vec{r} - \vec{r}_A$ pointing from A to dm. Two parallel axes are shown: one through **cm** with moment of inertia $I_{cm}$, and one through **A** with moment of inertia $I_A$.)*

$$I_A = \int dm \left|\vec{r} - \vec{r}_A\right|^2$$

$$= \int dm \left(r^2 - 2\vec{r} \cdot \vec{r}_A + r_A^2\right)$$

$$I_A = \underbrace{\int dm\, r^2}_{I_{CM}} - 2\underbrace{\left(\int dm\,\vec{r}\right)}_{M\vec{r}_{cm}} \cdot \vec{r}_A + \underbrace{\left(\int dm\right)}_{M} r_A^2$$

Setting $\vec{r}_{cm}$ as the origin and letting $r_A = h$:

$$\boxed{I_A = I_{cm} + Mh^2}$$

## Page 44 — Perpendicular Axis Theorem

*(Diagram: A thin flat plate lying in the x-y plane, with three coordinate axes shown: +x (labeled $I_x$), +y (labeled $I_y$), and +z (labeled $I_z$). A position vector $r$ points from the origin to a mass element on the plate, with components $x$ and $y$.)*

$$I_x = \int y^2 \, dm$$

$$I_y = \int x^2 \, dm$$

$$I_z = \int r^2 \, dm$$

---

Using $r^2 = x^2 + y^2$:

$$\Rightarrow \quad I_z = \int r^2 \, dm = \int (x^2 + y^2) \, dm = \int y^2 \, dm + \int x^2 \, dm$$

$$\Rightarrow \quad \boldsymbol{I_z = I_x + I_y}$$

## Page 48 — Simple Pendulum: First Method

*(Diagram: A simple pendulum fixed to a ceiling, with a string of length $l$ making angle $\theta$ from the vertical axis. The bob of mass $m$ is shown with tension $T$ along the string directed upward, and gravitational force $mg$ directed downward. Coordinate axes show $+x$ to the right and $+y$ upward.)*

**Simple Pendulum**

> The string has no mass and its length $l$ is constant.
> Mass $m$ moves only in a vertical plane.

| $= \dfrac{1}{2}mv^2 - \dfrac{1}{2}mv_0^2$ | $= \dfrac{1}{2}I\omega^2 - \dfrac{1}{2}I\omega_0^2$ |

## Page 85 — Rotational Motion Problem

*(Diagram: A pulley of radius $R$ and moment of inertia $I$ mounted on a wall, rotating with initial angular velocity $\omega_1$. A rope of length $L$ hangs from the pulley with a constant force $F$ applied downward at its end.)*

A pulley with radius $R$ and moment of inertia $I$ is pulled a distance $L$ by a constant force $F$. Find the final angular velocity $\omega_2$ of the pulley. The initial angular velocity of the pulley is $\omega_1$.

---

**Method 1** — Using the Rotational Equation of Motion

**Torque acting on the pulley**

$$\tau = RF$$

**Angular acceleration of the pulley**

$$\alpha = \frac{\tau}{I} = \frac{RF}{I}$$

**Rotation angle of the pulley**

$$\Delta\theta = \theta_2 - \theta_1 = \frac{L}{R}$$

**Uniform angular acceleration kinematic formula**

$$2\alpha(\theta_2 - \theta_1) = \omega_2^2 - \omega_1^2$$

$$\Downarrow$$

$$\omega_2 = \sqrt{\omega_1^2 + 2\alpha\Delta\theta}$$

$$= \sqrt{\omega_1^2 + \frac{2FL}{I}}$$

## Page 86 — Rotational Motion Problem

A pulley with radius $R$ and moment of inertia $I$ is pulled a distance $L$ by a constant force $F$. Find the final angular velocity $\omega_2$ of the pulley. The initial angular velocity of the pulley is $\omega_1$.

*(Diagram: A pulley of radius R mounted on a wall, with a rope hanging down of length L, and a force F applied downward. The pulley has initial angular velocity $\omega_1$.)*

---

**Method 2** — Using the Work-Energy Theorem

**Work done on the pulley:** $W = FL$

**Change in kinetic energy of the pulley:** $\Delta K = \dfrac{1}{2}I\omega_2^2 - \dfrac{1}{2}I\omega_1^2$

**Work-Energy Theorem:** $W = \Delta K$

$$\Rightarrow FL = \frac{1}{2}I\!\left(\omega_2^2 - \omega_1^2\right)$$

$$\Rightarrow \omega_2 = \sqrt{\omega_1^2 + \frac{2FL}{I}}$$

---

## Page 87 — Summary

When a pulley rotates, the magnitudes of the tensions in the strings on both sides of the pulley are different.

When a pulley rotates, apply the rotational equation of motion to the pulley.

The work-energy theorem for rotational motion is also $W = \Delta K$.

## Page 94 — Case Where **Static Friction** Acts Between the Floor and the Wheel

*(Diagram: A wheel on a flat surface with normal force $N$ pointing upward, applied force $F$ pointing to the right at the axle, and static friction force $f$ pointing to the left at the contact point with the ground.)*

**Rolling without slipping (Smooth rolling)**

$$x = R\theta$$

$$v = R\omega$$

$$a = R\alpha$$

**Method 1** — Using equations of motion

$$F - f = ma \quad \textbf{(1)} \qquad Rf = I\alpha \quad \textbf{(2)} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad f = \frac{I}{R^2}a \quad \textbf{(3)}$$

**Substituting equation (3) into equation (1):**

$$\boxed{a = \dfrac{F}{m + \dfrac{I}{R^2}}} \qquad \boxed{f = \dfrac{F}{1 + \dfrac{mR^2}{I}}}$$

---

## Page 95 — Case Where Static Friction Acts Between the Floor and the Wheel

$$a = \frac{F}{m + \dfrac{I}{R^2}} \qquad f = \frac{F}{1 + \dfrac{mR^2}{I}}$$

- The acceleration is smaller than $a = \dfrac{F}{m}$, which is the acceleration in the case of sliding without friction.

- As $F$ increases, the static friction force $f$ also increases.

- When $f$ becomes greater than the maximum static friction force $f_M$, the motion changes from **rolling without slipping** to **rolling with slipping**.

## Page 96 — Case Where Static Friction Acts Between the Floor and the Wheel

*(Diagram: Two wheels on a flat surface. A force $F$ is applied horizontally to the right at the axle of the left wheel. A normal force $N$ acts upward, and a static friction force $f$ acts to the left at the contact point. The displacement $x$ is indicated along the floor.)*

**Method 2**

Using the Work-Energy Theorem

$$v = R\omega$$

$$v = at, \quad x = \frac{1}{2}at^2$$

**Work done** $\quad W = Fx = \dfrac{1}{2}Fat^2 \quad \textbf{(1)}$

**Change in kinetic energy**

$$\Delta K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 + \frac{1}{2}I\omega^2 = \frac{1}{2}\!\left(m + \frac{I}{R^2}\right)v^2$$

$$= \frac{1}{2}\!\left(m + \frac{I}{R^2}\right)a^2\,t^2 \quad \textbf{(2)}$$

**Work-Energy Theorem** $\quad W = \Delta K \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \displaystyle a = \frac{F}{m + \dfrac{I}{R^2}}$

## Page 98 — Case Where Static Friction Acts Between the Floor and the Wheel

## Page 102 — Conservation of Linear Momentum → Conservation of Angular Momentum

| **Translational Motion** | **Rotational Motion** |

| $$\vec{F} = \frac{d\vec{p}}{dt}$$ | $$\vec{\tau} = \frac{d\vec{L}}{dt}$$ |

$$\vec{L} = I\vec{\omega}$$

## Page 115 — Procedure for Solving Equilibrium Problems

1. Identify the object to which the equilibrium conditions will be applied.

2. **Find all forces acting on that object and draw them together with their points of application.**

3. Choose an appropriate coordinate system and apply the condition that the sum of the force components along each coordinate axis equals 0.

4. **Choose an appropriate axis of rotation and define the positive direction of torque.**

5. Apply the condition that the sum of torques about each rotational axis equals 0.

6. **For a single object, you may freely choose as many different axes of rotation as you like.**
