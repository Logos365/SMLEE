## Page 4 — Principia

The law of universal gravitation and the laws of motion explain why Kepler's laws of planetary motion hold.

> Newton's mechanics became the new fundamental principle of nature, replacing the celestial laws and terrestrial laws believed for 16 centuries.

Halley used Newtonian mechanics to accurately predict when the comet (later named after him) would next appear, demonstrating the power of Newtonian mechanics.

*(Photo: Halley's Comet)*

---

## Page 5 — Principia (continued)

By the late 19th century, it was believed that all natural phenomena could be explained by Newtonian mechanics and Maxwell's electromagnetism — **humankind had conquered nature**.

However, Einstein's General Theory of Relativity, published in 1915, revealed that the gravitational force on which Newton's mechanics was based does not fundamentally exist.

For phenomena in our everyday surroundings, the results obtained by Newtonian mechanics and General Relativity are identical, so we continue to use Newtonian mechanics because it is **much simpler**.

---

## Page 6 — Optics

In 1672, Newton published experimental results using prisms, claiming that **light is a particle (corpuscle)**.

However, this triggered fierce controversy, with Hooke and others opposing this view.

After long debate about the nature of light, Newton refused to participate. He published the Opticks in 1704 — his second book — two years after Hooke died.

> He describes in detail the specific content of long experiments and experimental results he had carefully conducted.

---

## Page 7 — Optics ⟺ Corpuscular Theory of Light

*(Photo: Prism dispersing white light into a spectrum)*

Through various experiments using prisms, Newton concluded that:
- The color of light cannot be changed by any method
- Each color corresponds to a specific refraction angle
- Therefore, **light is made of particles (corpuscles)**

Newton's Optics gained considerable persuasive power after its publication, promoting the idea that light consists of particles.

## Page 10 — The Reversal in the 20th Century

In the 20th century, Newton's theory of gravity was proven wrong by Einstein's General Theory of Relativity, but **Newton's idea that light is a particle was not wrong**.

> Newton's Optics had been out of print for 200 years when it was reprinted in 1931, and this reprint included a preface by Einstein.
>
> In the preface, Einstein wrote: *"Nature has spread this book before Newton like an open book, and he was able to read it without the slightest difficulty — that is, he was able to read Nature itself."*

## Page 15 — Reflection and Refraction of Light

**Medium** → A substance through which light passes

**Ray** → A line along which light travels

In the same medium, rays travel in straight lines.

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Incident ray** | The ray entering the boundary between two media |
| **Reflected ray** | The ray reflected at the boundary between two media |
| **Refracted ray** | The ray that passes into the other medium at the boundary |

*(Diagram showing incident ray, reflected ray, and refracted ray at an interface)*

---

## Page 16 — Reflection and Refraction of Light (continued)

**Normal** → A line perpendicular to the boundary surface between two media

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Angle of incidence θ₁** | The angle between the incident ray and the normal |
| **Angle of reflection θ₁'** | The angle between the reflected ray and the normal |
| **Angle of refraction θ₂** | The angle between the refracted ray and the normal |

*(Diagram: incident ray θ₁, reflected ray θ₁', refracted ray θ₂, normal as dashed line)*

---

## Page 17 — Law of Reflection

**Reflecting surface** → The plane on which light is reflected

**Plane of incidence** → The plane containing the incident ray, reflected ray, and normal

**Law of Reflection:**
1. The incident ray, reflected ray, and normal always lie in the same plane.
2. The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection.

$$\theta_1 = \theta_1'$$

*(3D diagram showing the reflecting surface and plane of incidence)*

---

## Page 18 — Law of Refraction (Snell's Law)

When light is incident on the boundary between two media with refractive indices n₁ and n₂:
- A portion of light is **reflected** and a portion is **refracted**.

**Law of Refraction:**
1. The incident ray, refracted ray, and normal always lie in the same plane.
2. The angle of incidence and the angle of refraction satisfy **Snell's Law**.

$$n_1 \sin\theta_1 = n_2 \sin\theta_2$$

*(Diagram: n₁ upper medium, n₂ lower medium, showing θ₁, θ₁', θ₂)*

---

## Page 19 — The Person Who Discovered Snell's Law

**Willebrord Snellius**
*(Netherlands, 1580–1626)*
*Astronomer and mathematician; law of refraction*

- Commonly known in English as Snell
- Succeeded his father as a professor of mathematics at the University of Leiden, Netherlands
- Developed a new method of determining the radius of the Earth from the angle between two distant points along a meridian
- Determined the value of π using a new method
- Discovered the law of refraction (Snell's Law) in **1621**

> This fact became known from Huygens' writings.

## Page 20 — Snell's Law

*(Diagram: unit circle with n₁ above, n₂ below)*

Draw a circle of radius 1.

- b₁ = sin θ₁
- b₂ = sin θ₂

Snell's Law: $n_1 \sin\theta_1 = n_2 \sin\theta_2$

$$\Rightarrow n_1 b_1 = n_2 b_2$$

If n₁ < n₂, then b₁ > b₂

**When light travels from a less dense medium to a denser medium, the refraction angle is smaller than the angle of incidence.**

---

## Page 21 — Snell's Law (continued)

*(Diagram: unit circle with n₁ above, n₂ below)*

If n₁ > n₂, then b₁ < b₂

**When light travels from a denser medium to a less dense medium, the refraction angle is larger than the angle of incidence.**

## Page 24 — Critical Angle for Total Internal Reflection

**Critical angle θ_c** → The minimum angle of incidence at which total internal reflection occurs
→ The angle of incidence when the angle of refraction is 90°

From Snell's Law: $n_1 \sin\theta_c = n_2 \sin 90° = n_2$

$$\sin\theta_c = \frac{n_2}{n_1} \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad \theta_c = \sin^{-1}\frac{n_2}{n_1}$$

*(Diagram showing increasing incident angle: θ₁ → θ_c → total internal reflection, with refracted ray going to 90° then disappearing)*

---

## Page 25 — Summary

Light undergoes both reflection and refraction at the boundary between two media.

**Law of Reflection:**
- Incident ray, normal, and reflected ray lie in the same plane
- Angle of incidence equals angle of reflection

**Law of Refraction:**
- Incident ray, normal, and refracted ray lie in the same plane
- Angle of incidence and angle of refraction satisfy Snell's Law

**Total Internal Reflection:**
- When light traveling from a dense medium to a less dense medium has an angle of incidence greater than the critical angle, total internal reflection occurs.

## Page 32 — Why Frequency Does Not Change at the Boundary Between Two Media

The speed of light changes at the boundary between two media.

From $v = \lambda f$: **frequency f does not change; only wavelength λ changes.**

**Why frequency does not change at the boundary between two media:**

> The speed of waves transmitted by two strings of different densities is different.
>
> At the boundary between the strings, both sides must go up and down equally.
>
> Therefore, as the wave passes the boundary of the two strings, the frequency on both sides remains the same.

---

## Page 33 — Proof of Snell's Law

*(Diagram: wavefronts of incident and refracted light crossing a medium boundary)*

$$v = \lambda f, \quad n = \frac{c}{v}$$

$$\sin\theta_1 = \frac{\lambda_1}{h}, \qquad \sin\theta_2 = \frac{\lambda_2}{h}$$

$$\Rightarrow \frac{\sin\theta_1}{\sin\theta_2} = \frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_2} = \frac{v_1}{v_2} = \frac{n_2}{n_1}$$

$$\Rightarrow \boxed{n_1 \sin\theta_1 = n_2 \sin\theta_2} \checkmark$$

---

## Page 34 — Summary

- Light refracts when its propagation speed changes.
- The refractive index of a medium is inversely proportional to the speed of light in that medium: $n = c/v$
- When light travels into a different medium, the frequency does not change.
- Snell's Law for the refraction of light holds because the speed changes with the medium.

## Page 38 — Wave Equation and Wave Speed

The speed of a wave is contained in the wave equation.

Wave equation for strings: $\dfrac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2} - \dfrac{1}{v^2}\dfrac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} = 0$

Wave equation from strings: $\dfrac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2} - \dfrac{\mu}{\tau}\dfrac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} = 0 \Rightarrow v = \sqrt{\dfrac{\tau}{\mu}}$

Wave equation for electromagnetic waves: $\dfrac{\partial^2 E}{\partial x^2} - \mu_0\varepsilon_0\dfrac{\partial^2 E}{\partial t^2} = 0 \Rightarrow v = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}}$

$$v = \frac{\lambda}{T} = \lambda f = \frac{\omega}{k} \quad \text{— wave speed is determined by the medium}$$

> When a medium is fixed, the wave speed does not change even if the wavelength or frequency changes. → **The wave speed and the wavelength/frequency are inversely proportional in the same medium.**

- The phenomenon in which white light is split into a spectrum of colors during refraction is called **chromatic dispersion**.

## Page 48 (Slide 47) — Longitudinal and Transverse Waves

**Direction that appears in a wave** → The direction of vibration of the medium and the direction of wave propagation

**Longitudinal wave** → A wave in which the direction of vibration and the direction of propagation are parallel
- Example: Sound waves

**Transverse wave** → A wave in which the direction of vibration is perpendicular to the direction of propagation
- Examples: Surface water waves, waves on strings, electromagnetic waves

---

## Page 49 (Slide 48) — Transverse Waves and Polarization

**Longitudinal wave** → The direction of vibration of the medium has only one direction — parallel to propagation

**Transverse wave** → The direction of vibration is perpendicular to propagation, so there are infinitely many possible directions

**Polarized light** → A transverse wave that vibrates only in a restricted direction is called **polarized**.

*(Diagram: light source (광원) → ordinary (unpolarized) light radiating in all directions, represented by arrows in all perpendicular directions)*

---

## Page 50 (Slide 49) — Transverse Waves and Polarization (continued)

*(Diagram: light source → ordinary light → polarizing filter → polarized light)*

**Ordinary light** vibrates in all directions perpendicular to propagation — it is **unpolarized**.

Light that passes through a polarizing filter becomes **polarized**.

---

## Page 51 (Slide 50) — Transverse Waves and Polarization (continued)

*(Diagram: same sequence — light source → ordinary light → polarizing filter → polarized light)*

Ordinary light includes vibrations in all different directions — it is **unpolarized**.

Light that passes through a polarizing filter becomes **polarized**.

When light is reflected at the boundary between two media, if the angle of incidence has a special value called the **Brewster angle**, the reflected light becomes polarized.

## Page 54 (Slide 53) — Condition for Brewster's Angle to Exist

*(Same diagram as previous)*

**Condition for reflected light to be completely polarized:**
- The reflected ray and the refracted ray are **perpendicular** to each other.

→ The electric field and magnetic field of light cannot point in the direction of propagation.

---

## Page 55 (Slide 54) — Deriving Brewster's Angle

*(Diagram with incident, reflected, and refracted rays)*

If θ₁ = θ_B:

$$\pi - (\theta_B + \theta_2) = \frac{\pi}{2}$$

$$\Rightarrow \theta_B + \theta_2 = \frac{\pi}{2}$$

$$\Rightarrow \theta_2 = \frac{\pi}{2} - \theta_B$$

From Snell's Law:

$$n_1 \sin\theta_B = n_2 \sin\!\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - \theta_B\right) = n_2 \cos\theta_B$$

**Brewster's Angle:**

$$\tan\theta_B = \frac{n_2}{n_1} \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad \theta_B = \tan^{-1}\frac{n_2}{n_1}$$
