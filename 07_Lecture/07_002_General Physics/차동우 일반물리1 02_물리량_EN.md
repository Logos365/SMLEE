## Page 13 — Base Quantities and Derived Quantities

**Base quantity**
- A quantity expressed with a single unit.
- Examples: $l = 5\ \text{cm}$, $m = 20\ \text{g}$, $i = 3\ \text{A}$

**Derived quantity**
- A quantity expressed as a product of two or more units.
- Examples: $v = 10\ \text{m/s}$, $F = 3\ \text{kg\,m/s}^2 = 3\ \text{N}$

> **Base quantities are chosen by humans!!**
> The unit of force, N (newton), is NOT a base unit!!

## Page 15 — Unit Systems

**Unit Systems** *(slide 15)*

It is convenient to group units into a unit system.

The proportionality constants in natural laws change depending on which units are used.

**Example) Law of universal gravitation:**

$$F = G\frac{mm'}{r^2}$$

The value of the gravitational constant $G$ changes depending on the units used for force, mass, and distance.

> If only units belonging to the same unit system are used, the proportionality constants remain unchanged and the units of the quantity being sought are easy to remember.

## Page 18 — Derived Units

**Derived Units** *(slide 18)*

**How to find the derived unit of a derived quantity**

> Use any equation that contains the derived quantity.

$$\text{speed} = \frac{\text{distance}}{\text{time}} \implies \text{unit of speed} = \text{m/s}$$

$$\text{acceleration} = \frac{\text{speed}}{\text{time}} \implies \text{unit of acceleration} = \frac{\text{m/s}}{\text{s}} = \text{m/s}^2$$

$$\text{force} = \text{mass} \times \text{acceleration} \implies \text{unit of force} = \text{kg\,m/s}^2 = \text{N}$$

## Page 20 — Unit Conversion

**Unit Conversion** *(slide 21)*

**Method for converting the unit of a physical quantity to a different unit**

> Include the units in the calculation and compute together with the numbers.

**Example 1 — Express the highway speed limit of 100 km/hr in m/s:**

$$100\ \frac{\text{km}}{\text{hr}} = 100 \times \frac{1{,}000\ \text{m}}{60 \times 60\ \text{s}} = \frac{100{,}000}{3{,}600}\ \frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} = 27.8\ \text{m/s}$$

**Example 2 — Express the volume of a small water droplet, $0.5\ \text{mm}^3$, in $\text{m}^3$:**

$$0.5\ \text{mm}^3 = 0.5 \times (10^{-3}\ \text{m})^3 = 5 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{m}^3$$

## Page 23 — Dimensions of Physical Quantities

**Dimensions of Physical Quantities** *(slide 24)*

By looking at the derived unit of a derived quantity, one can tell what product of base quantities it is.

| Quantity | Units | Dimension |
|----------|-------|-----------|
| Speed | m/s, cm/s, mile/hr | $\dfrac{\text{length}}{\text{time}}$ |
| Area | m², cm², ft² | length² |
| Force | N, kg m/s² | $\dfrac{\text{mass} \times \text{length}}{\text{time}^2}$ |

- The dimension of speed is length divided by time.
- The dimension of area is the square of length.
- The dimension of force is the product of mass and length, divided by the square of time.

---

## Page 24 — Spatial Dimension vs. Dimension of a Physical Quantity

**Spatial Dimension and Dimension of a Physical Quantity** *(slide 25)*

The word "dimension" is used in two different senses:

**Spatial dimension**
- The space around us is three-dimensional.
- An object moving on a straight line undergoes one-dimensional motion.

**Dimension of a physical quantity**
- Dividing the length dimension by the time dimension gives the velocity dimension.
- All terms in an equation must have the same dimension.

---

## Page 25 — Notation for Dimensions of Physical Quantities

**Notation for Dimensions of Physical Quantities** *(slide 26)*

**[physical quantity]**
- Square brackets $[\,]$ denote the dimension of the quantity inside.

**Dimensions of base quantities:**

$$[\text{length}] = \mathrm{L}, \quad [\text{mass}] = \mathrm{M}, \quad [\text{time}] = \mathrm{T}$$

**Dimensions of derived quantities:**
- Expressed as products of powers of $\mathrm{L}$, $\mathrm{M}$, $\mathrm{T}$:

$$[\text{speed}] = \mathrm{L}\,\mathrm{T}^{-1}, \qquad [\text{force}] = [ma] = \mathrm{M}\,\mathrm{L}\,\mathrm{T}^{-2}$$

## Page 27 — Dimensional Analysis

**Dimensional Analysis** *(slide 29)*

**A method of finding the correct result using only the dimensions of physical quantities.**

*(Diagram: cartoon of a physicist surrounded by equations on a blackboard)*

**Principle of dimensional analysis:**

> All terms in an equation must have the same dimension.

$$K = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$$

Among $E = mc^2$, $E = mc^3$, $E = mc^4$, only $E = mc^2$ is dimensionally consistent. *(illustrated with a cartoon umbrella)*

---

## Page 28 — Example Using Dimensional Analysis (1)

**Example Using Dimensional Analysis (1)** *(slide 30)*

*(Diagram: sinusoidal wave)*

The speed $v$ of waves on a string is determined by the string's tension $T$ and the string's linear mass density $\lambda$. Find the wave speed $v$.

**Assume** $v = T^a \lambda^b$ and determine $a$ and $b$ by dimensional analysis.

Dimensions:
$$[v] = \mathrm{L\,T^{-1}}, \quad [T] = \mathrm{M\,L\,T^{-2}}, \quad [\lambda] = \mathrm{M\,L^{-1}}$$

Therefore:
$$\mathrm{L\,T^{-1}} = (\mathrm{M\,L\,T^{-2}})^a\,(\mathrm{M\,L^{-1}})^b$$

Matching exponents:
- L: $1 = a - b$
- T: $-1 = -2a$
- M: $0 = a + b$

$$\therefore\quad a = \frac{1}{2},\quad b = -\frac{1}{2} \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad v = \sqrt{\frac{T}{\lambda}}$$

---

## Page 29 — Example Using Dimensional Analysis (2)

**Example Using Dimensional Analysis (2)** *(slide 31)*

*(Diagram: simple pendulum with length $l$ and bob mass $m$)*

The period $T$ of a simple pendulum is determined by the string length $l$, the bob mass $m$, and the gravitational acceleration $g$. Find the period $T$.

**Assume** $T = l^a m^b g^c$:

$$\mathrm{T} = \mathrm{L}^a\,\mathrm{M}^b\,(\mathrm{L\,T}^{-2})^c$$

Matching exponents:
- L: $0 = a + c$
- T: $1 = -2c$
- M: $0 = b$

$$\therefore\quad a = \frac{1}{2},\quad b = 0,\quad c = -\frac{1}{2}$$

$$\boxed{T = \alpha\sqrt{\frac{l}{g}}}$$

## Page 36 — Significant Figures in Calculated Values — Addition

**Significant Figures in Calculated Values — Addition** *(slide 37)*

$$\begin{array}{r}
101.25 \\
3{,}536.2 \\
+\quad 123.448 \\
\hline
3{,}760.898 \\
\rightarrow\quad 3{,}760.9
\end{array}$$

> When adding two or more measured values, the result's significant figures follow the value whose **last significant digit is in the highest decimal place** (i.e., least precise in absolute terms).

*(Here 3,536.2 ends at the tenths place, so the sum is rounded to the tenths place: **3,760.9**.)*

\hline
23 \\
92 \\
+\quad 69 \\
\hline
78.43 \\

\end{array}$$

> When multiplying two or more measured values, the result has the **same number of significant figures as the value with the fewest significant figures**.
