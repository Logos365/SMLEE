## Page 3 (Slide 3) — Electric Phenomena: Coulomb's Law (1784)

**Electric Phenomena**

Law governing the electric field produced by a charge distribution in the surrounding space:

**Coulomb's Law** (1784)

$$\vec{E}(\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \int_V dv'\, \frac{\rho(\vec{r}\,')}{\left|\vec{r} - \vec{r}\,'\right|^2} \cdot \frac{\vec{r} - \vec{r}\,'}{\left|\vec{r} - \vec{r}\,'\right|}$$

*(Diagram: source charge element $\rho(\vec{r}\,')dv'$ at position $\vec{r}\,'$, field point $P$ at $\vec{r}$, electric field $\vec{E}(\vec{r})$ pointing away)*

- $\rho(\vec{r}\,')dv'$ — proportional to the charge density
- $\left|\vec{r} - \vec{r}\,'\right|^2$ — inversely proportional to the square of the distance
- $\dfrac{\vec{r} - \vec{r}\,'}{\left|\vec{r} - \vec{r}\,'\right|}$ — determines the direction of the electric field

---

## Page 4 (Slide 4) — Electric Phenomena: Gauss's Law (1813)

**Electric Phenomena**

Law governing the electric field produced by a charge distribution in the surrounding space:

**Gauss's Law** (1813)

$$\oint_S \vec{E}(\vec{r}) \cdot d\vec{S} = \frac{1}{\epsilon_0} \int_V \rho(\vec{r})\, dv$$

- $\Phi_E = \displaystyle\oint_S \vec{E}(\vec{r}) \cdot d\vec{S}$ — total electric flux through the closed surface
- $q = \displaystyle\int_V \rho(\vec{r})\, dv$ — total charge inside the closed surface

$$\boxed{\Phi_E = \frac{q}{\epsilon_0}}$$

*(Diagram: closed surface enclosing positive and negative charges, with electric field lines)*

## Page 6 (Slide 6) — Magnetic Phenomena: Biot–Savart Law (1820)

**Magnetic Phenomena**

Law governing the magnetic field produced by a current distribution in the surrounding space:

**Biot–Savart Law** (1820)

$$\vec{B}(\vec{r}) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \int dv'\, \frac{\vec{J}(\vec{r}\,')}{\left|\vec{r} - \vec{r}\,'\right|^2} \times \frac{\vec{r} - \vec{r}\,'}{\left|\vec{r} - \vec{r}\,'\right|}$$

*(Diagram: current element $id\vec{l}$ at $\vec{r}\,'$, field point $P$ at $\vec{r}$, magnetic field $d\vec{B}$ pointing into the page)*

- $\vec{J}(\vec{r}\,')dv'$ — proportional to the current density
- $\left|\vec{r} - \vec{r}\,'\right|^2$ — inversely proportional to the square of the distance
- $\times\, \dfrac{\vec{r} - \vec{r}\,'}{\left|\vec{r} - \vec{r}\,'\right|}$ — determines the direction of the magnetic field

## Page 7 (Slide 7) — Magnetic Phenomena: Ampère's Law (1820)

**Magnetic Phenomena**

Law governing the magnetic field produced by a current distribution in the surrounding space:

**Ampère's Law** (1820)

$$\oint_C \vec{B}(\vec{r}) \cdot d\vec{l} = \mu_0 \int_S \vec{J}(\vec{r}) \cdot d\vec{S}$$

- $\Gamma_B = \displaystyle\oint_C \vec{B}(\vec{r}) \cdot d\vec{l}$ — circulation of the magnetic field along the closed curve
- $i = \displaystyle\int_S \vec{J}(\vec{r}) \cdot d\vec{S}$ — total current passing through the interior of the closed curve

$$\boxed{\Gamma_B = \mu_0 i}$$

*(Diagram: straight wire carrying current $I$ with circular magnetic field lines)*

## Page 8 (Slide 8) — Electric and Magnetic Phenomena

**Electric and Magnetic Phenomena**

**Belief until the late 18th century:**
- Charges create electric fields; only charges experience forces in an electric field.
- Magnets create magnetic fields; only magnets experience forces in a magnetic field.
- $\Rightarrow$ Electric and magnetic phenomena have no relation to each other.

**Ørsted's discovery in 1820:**
- Moving charges create magnetic fields; moving charges experience forces in a magnetic field.
- $\Rightarrow$ There must be some relationship between electric and magnetic phenomena.

*(Cross-reference: 19B)*

## Page 11 (Slide 11) — Electric and Magnetic Phenomena: Faraday's Law (1831)

**Electric and Magnetic Phenomena**

Law governing the electric field produced by a changing magnetic field in the surrounding space:

**Faraday's Law** (1831)

$$\oint_C \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{r} = -\frac{d}{dt} \int_S \vec{B}(t) \cdot d\vec{S}$$

- $\Gamma_E = \displaystyle\oint_C \vec{E} \cdot d\vec{r}$ — circulation of the electric field along the closed curve
- $\Phi_B = \displaystyle\int_S \vec{B}(t) \cdot d\vec{S}$ — magnetic flux through the closed curve

$$\boxed{\Gamma_E = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}}$$

*(Diagram: moving magnet with magnetic flux $\Phi_B(t)$ inducing electric field $\vec{E}$ in a conducting loop)*

## Page 17 (Slide 17) — Coordinate System, Position Vector, Differential, Vector Differential Operator

**Coordinate System, Position Vector, Differential, and Vector Differential Operator**

*(Diagram: 3D Cartesian coordinate system with a position vector $\vec{r}$ at angles $\theta$ and $\phi$)*

**Position vector:**
$$\vec{r} = x\hat{x} + y\hat{y} + z\hat{z}$$

**Differential:**
$$d\vec{r} = dx\hat{x} + dy\hat{y} + dz\hat{z}$$

**Vector differential operator (del/nabla):**
$$\nabla = \hat{x}\frac{\partial}{\partial x} + \hat{y}\frac{\partial}{\partial y} + \hat{z}\frac{\partial}{\partial z}$$

---

## Page 18 (Slide 18) — Gradient, Curl, Divergence, Laplacian

**Gradient, Curl, Divergence, Laplacian**

*(Diagram: same 3D coordinate system)*

**Gradient of $\phi$:**
$$\nabla\phi = \hat{x}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x} + \hat{y}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y} + \hat{z}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial z}$$

**Curl of $\vec{F}$:**
$$\nabla \times \vec{F} = \hat{x}\!\left(\frac{\partial F_z}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial F_y}{\partial z}\right) + \hat{y}\!\left(\frac{\partial F_x}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial F_z}{\partial x}\right) + \hat{z}\!\left(\frac{\partial F_y}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial F_x}{\partial y}\right)$$

**Divergence of $\vec{F}$:**
$$\nabla \cdot \vec{F} = \frac{\partial F_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial F_y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial F_z}{\partial z}$$

**Laplacian of $\vec{F}$:**
$$\nabla \cdot \nabla\vec{F} = \nabla^2\vec{F} = \frac{\partial^2\vec{F}}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2\vec{F}}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2\vec{F}}{\partial z^2}$$

---

## Page 19 (Slide 19) — Stokes' Theorem and Divergence Theorem

**Stokes' Theorem and Divergence Theorem**

*(Cross-references: 06G, 15K)*

**Stokes' Theorem:**
For any vector field $\vec{F}(\vec{r})$, the line integral along any closed curve $C$ equals the surface integral of $\nabla\times\vec{F}(\vec{r})$ over the surface $S$ bounded by $C$:

$$\oint_C \vec{F}(\vec{r}) \cdot d\vec{r} = \int_S \bigl(\nabla \times \vec{F}(\vec{r})\bigr) \cdot d\vec{S}$$

*(Diagram: labels — closed curve $C$, surface $S$ bounded by $C$, line integral → surface integral)*

**Divergence Theorem (Gauss's Theorem):**
For any vector field $\vec{F}(\vec{r})$, the surface integral over any closed surface $S$ equals the volume integral of $\nabla\cdot\vec{F}(\vec{r})$ over the volume $V$ enclosed by $S$:

$$\oint_S \vec{F}(\vec{r}) \cdot d\vec{S} = \int_V \nabla \cdot \vec{F}(\vec{r})\, dV$$

*(Diagram: labels — closed surface $S$, enclosed volume $V$, surface integral → volume integral)*

---

## Page 20 (Slide 20) — Applying Stokes' and Divergence Theorems

**Applying Stokes' Theorem and Divergence Theorem**

Converting the four fundamental laws from integral form to equivalent forms:

| Law | Integral form | Equivalent form |
|---|---|---|
| Gauss's Law for $\vec{E}$ | $\displaystyle\oint_S \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{S} = \frac{1}{\epsilon_0}\int_V\rho\,dv$ | $\displaystyle\int_V \nabla\cdot\vec{E}\,dV = \frac{1}{\epsilon_0}\int_V \rho\,dv$ |
| Gauss's Law for $\vec{B}$ | $\displaystyle\oint_S \vec{B}\cdot d\vec{S} = 0$ | $\displaystyle\int_V \nabla\cdot\vec{B}\,dV = 0$ |
| Ampère's Law | $\displaystyle\oint_C \vec{B}\cdot d\vec{r} = \mu_0\int_S \vec{J}\cdot d\vec{S}$ | $\displaystyle\int_S (\nabla\times\vec{B})\cdot d\vec{S} = \mu_0\int_S \vec{J}\cdot d\vec{S}$ |
| Faraday's Law | $\displaystyle\oint_C \vec{E}\cdot d\vec{r} = -\frac{d}{dt}\int_S \vec{B}\cdot d\vec{S}$ | $\displaystyle\int_S (\nabla\times\vec{E})\cdot d\vec{S} = -\frac{d}{dt}\int_S \vec{B}\cdot d\vec{S}$ |

## Page 21 (Slide 21) — Maxwell's Equations

**Maxwell's Equations**

Deriving the local (differential) form from the equivalent integral relations:

| Law | Differential (local) form |
|---|---|
| Gauss's Law for $\vec{E}$ | $\nabla \cdot \vec{E}(\vec{r}) = \dfrac{\rho(\vec{r})}{\epsilon_0}$ |
| Gauss's Law for $\vec{B}$ | $\nabla \cdot \vec{B}(\vec{r}) = 0$ |
| Ampère's Law for $\vec{B}$ | $\nabla \times \vec{B}(\vec{r}) = \mu_0 \vec{J}(\vec{r})$ |
| Faraday's Law for $\vec{E}$ | $\nabla \times \vec{E}(\vec{r}) = -\dfrac{\partial \vec{B}(\vec{r},t)}{\partial t}$ |

These four equations are the **Maxwell's Equations**.

---

## Page 22 (Slide 22) — Maxwell's Equations and Maxwell's Modification of Ampère's Law

**Maxwell's Equations and Maxwell's Modification of Ampère's Law**

| | Integral form | Differential form |
|---|---|---|
| Gauss's Law for $\vec{E}$ | $\Phi_E = \dfrac{q}{\epsilon_0}$ | $\nabla\cdot\vec{E}(\vec{r}) = \dfrac{\rho(\vec{r})}{\epsilon_0}$ |
| Gauss's Law for $\vec{B}$ | $\Phi_B = 0$ | $\nabla\cdot\vec{B}(\vec{r}) = 0$ |
| Ampère's Law (original) | $\Gamma_B = \mu_0 i$ | $\nabla\times\vec{B}(\vec{r}) = \mu_0\vec{J}(\vec{r})$ |
| **Ampère's Law (modified)** | $\Gamma_B = \mu_0 i + \mu_0\epsilon_0\dfrac{d\Phi_E}{dt}$ | $\nabla\times\vec{B}(\vec{r}) = \mu_0\vec{J}(\vec{r}) + \mu_0\epsilon_0\dfrac{\partial\vec{E}(\vec{r},t)}{\partial t}$ |
| Faraday's Law for $\vec{E}$ | $\Gamma_E = -\dfrac{d\Phi_B}{dt}$ | $\nabla\times\vec{E}(\vec{r}) = -\dfrac{\partial\vec{B}(\vec{r},t)}{\partial t}$ |

## Page 23 (Slide 23) — Summary: Maxwell's Equations

**Summary**

**Maxwell's Equations** — the four fundamental laws of electromagnetic phenomena expressed as divergence and curl equations:

**For the electric field:**

$$\nabla \cdot \vec{E}(\vec{r}) = \frac{\rho(\vec{r})}{\epsilon_0} \quad \text{(Gauss's Law for } \vec{E}\text{)}$$

$$\nabla \times \vec{E}(\vec{r}) = -\frac{\partial \vec{B}(\vec{r},t)}{\partial t} \quad \text{(Faraday's Law for } \vec{E}\text{)}$$

**For the magnetic field:**

$$\nabla \cdot \vec{B}(\vec{r}) = 0 \quad \text{(Gauss's Law for } \vec{B}\text{)}$$

$$\nabla \times \vec{B}(\vec{r}) = \mu_0\vec{J}(\vec{r}) + \mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial \vec{E}(\vec{r},t)}{\partial t} \quad \text{(Ampère's Law for } \vec{B}\text{)}$$

## Page 25 (Slide 25) — Ampère's Law (Simple Wire)

**Ampère's Law**

**Ampère's Law**

Applying Ampère's Law to the closed curve surrounding the wire in an RC circuit containing a capacitor:

- Circulation: $\Gamma_B = \displaystyle\oint_C \vec{B} \cdot d\vec{r}$
- Total current through surface $S_1$ (cutting the wire): $I_1 = \displaystyle\int_{S_1} \vec{J} \cdot d\vec{S} = i$

## Page 28 (Slide 28) — Ampère's Law with a Capacitor (Surface $S_2$)

**Ampère's Law**

- Total current through surface $S_2$ (passing between the capacitor plates):

$$I_2 = \int_{S_2} \vec{J} \cdot d\vec{S} = 0$$

⚠ $I_1 \neq I_2$ — **Contradiction!**

*(Diagram: $S_2$ passes between the capacitor plates where no conduction current flows, giving $I_2 = 0$)*

The original Ampère's Law $\Gamma_B = \mu_0 i$ **can fail** depending on the choice of surface.

## Page 29 (Slide 29) — Maxwell's Modification of Ampère's Law

**Maxwell's Modification of Ampère's Law**

Since Ampère's Law $\displaystyle\oint_C \vec{B}\cdot d\vec{r} = \mu_0\int_S \vec{J}\cdot d\vec{S}$ does not always hold, **Maxwell modified it**:

$$\Gamma_B = \mu_0 i \quad \Rightarrow \quad \Gamma_B = \mu_0 i + \mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{d\Phi_E}{dt}$$

$$\nabla \times \vec{B}(\vec{r}) = \mu_0\vec{J}(\vec{r}) \quad \Rightarrow \quad \nabla \times \vec{B}(\vec{r}) = \mu_0\vec{J}(\vec{r}) + \mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial \vec{E}(\vec{r},t)}{\partial t}$$

---

## Page 30 (Slide 30) — Displacement Current Density

**Displacement Current Density**

From the modified Ampère's Law:
$$\nabla \times \vec{B}(\vec{r}) = \mu_0\vec{J}(\vec{r}) + \mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial \vec{E}(\vec{r},t)}{\partial t}$$

**Definition of displacement current density:**
$$\vec{J}_d = \epsilon_0\frac{\partial \vec{E}(\vec{r},t)}{\partial t}$$

**Modified Ampère's Law:**
$$\nabla \times \vec{B} = \mu_0\bigl(\vec{J} + \vec{J}_d\bigr)$$

$$\boxed{\Gamma_B = \mu_0(i + i_d)}$$

---

## Page 31 (Slide 31) — Displacement Current Density in a Capacitor

**Displacement Current Density**

*(Diagram: parallel-plate capacitor with plate area $S$, charge $q$, current $i$ flowing in/out, magnetic field circles inside the gap, radii $a$ and $r$)*

**Electric field inside a parallel-plate capacitor charged to $q$:**
$$E = \frac{\sigma}{\epsilon_0} = \frac{q}{\epsilon_0 S}$$

**Displacement current density inside the capacitor:**
$$J_d = \epsilon_0\frac{\partial E}{\partial t} = \epsilon_0\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\!\left(\frac{q}{\epsilon_0 S}\right) = \frac{1}{S}\frac{dq}{dt} = \frac{i}{S}$$

The displacement current density inside the capacitor equals the conduction current density in the wire.

---

## Page 32 (Slide 32) — Verification of Ampère's Law (Surface $S_1$)

**Verification of Ampère's Law**

Applying the modified Ampère's Law to surface $S_1$ (cutting the wire):

$$I_1 = \int_{S_1}\!\bigl(\vec{J} + \vec{J}_d\bigr)\cdot d\vec{S} = i \quad \bigl(J_d = 0 \text{ on } S_1\bigr)$$

*(Diagram: surface $S_1$ passing through the wire; $J_d = 0$ there because the wire has no changing electric field)*

---

## Page 33 (Slide 33) — Verification of Ampère's Law (Surface $S_2$)

**Verification of Ampère's Law**

Through surface $S_1$: $I_1 = \displaystyle\int_{S_1}(\vec{J}+\vec{J}_d)\cdot d\vec{S} = i \quad (J_d=0)$

Through surface $S_2$ (between capacitor plates): $I_2 = \displaystyle\int_{S_2}(\vec{J}+\vec{J}_d)\cdot d\vec{S} = i \quad (J=0)$

$$\boxed{\Gamma_B = \mu_0 I_1 = \mu_0 I_2}$$

The modified Ampère's Law is now **consistent** regardless of which surface bounded by $C$ is chosen.

## Page 35 (Slide 35) — Oscillation

**Oscillation**

**Oscillation:** motion in which an object repeatedly performs the same motion around an equilibrium position.

**Simple Harmonic Oscillation (SHO):** the most representative form of oscillation in which the displacement varies sinusoidally or cosinusoidally with time:

$$x(t) = A\sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{T}t\right) = A\sin\omega t$$

**Period $T$, frequency $f$, angular frequency $\omega$:**

$$\omega = \frac{2\pi}{T},\quad f = \frac{1}{T},\quad \omega = 2\pi f$$

---

## Page 36 (Slide 36) — Fourier Series

**Fourier Series**

$$F(t) = \frac{A_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\!\left[A_n\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi n}{T}t\right) + B_n\sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi n}{T}t\right)\right]$$

Any oscillation can be expressed as a sum of simple harmonic oscillations.

**Expansion coefficients:**
$$A_0 = \frac{2}{T}\int_0^T F(t)\,dt, \quad A_n = \frac{2}{T}\int_0^T F(t)\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi n}{T}t\right)dt$$

$$B_n = \frac{2}{T}\int_0^T F(t)\sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi n}{T}t\right)dt$$

## Page 37 (Slide 37) — Waves

**Waves**

**Wave:** a phenomenon in which adjacent oscillations are transmitted among many objects.

**Characteristic of waves:** each object repeats the same oscillation in place with a time delay; only the pattern of oscillation propagates.

**Medium:** the oscillating substance that carries the wave.

*(Diagram: transverse wave animation showing wavelength, oscillating particles along the wave)*

## Page 38 (Slide 38) — Period and Wavelength

**Period and Wavelength**

| Temporal | Spatial |
|---|---|
| **Period $T$** (Period) | **Wavelength $\lambda$** (Wavelength) |
| Time for one complete oscillation | Distance traveled by the wave in one period |
| **Frequency $f$** (Frequency) | **Spatial frequency $1/\lambda$** |
| Number of oscillations per unit time | Number of waves per unit length |
| **Angular frequency $\omega = 2\pi f$** (Angular Frequency) | **Wave number $k = 2\pi/\lambda$** (Wave number) |
| Frequency multiplied by $2\pi$ | Spatial frequency multiplied by $2\pi$ |

## Page 39 (Slide 39) — Wave Speed

**Wave Speed**

**Wave speed $v$:** the speed at which oscillation propagates in a wave.
- Wave speed is determined by the medium.
- Wave speed is constant regardless of the period or wavelength as long as the medium does not change.

$$v = \frac{\lambda}{T} = \lambda f = \frac{2\pi}{k}\cdot\frac{\omega}{2\pi} = \frac{\omega}{k}$$

---

## Page 40 (Slide 40) — The Expression for a Wave

**The Expression Representing a Wave**

The expression representing a wave carrying simple harmonic oscillation:

$$\boxed{y(x,t) = A\sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}x - \frac{2\pi}{T}t\right) = A\sin(kx - \omega t)}$$

- $y(x,t)$ — displacement of the medium at position $x$ at time $t$
- $A$ — amplitude of the wave; maximum displacement of the medium
- $\dfrac{2\pi}{\lambda}x - \dfrac{2\pi}{T}t = kx - \omega t$ — phase of the wave at position $x$ at time $t$

---

## Page 41 (Slide 41) — The Wave Equation Derived from the Wave Expression

**The Wave Expression Satisfies: the Wave Equation**

Starting from $y(x,t) = A\sin(kx - \omega t)$:

$$\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2} = -k^2 A\sin(kx-\omega t), \qquad \frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} = -\omega^2 A\sin(kx-\omega t)$$

Therefore:

$$\frac{1}{k^2}\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2} - \frac{1}{\omega^2}\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2} - \frac{k^2}{\omega^2}\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} = 0$$

Using $v = \omega/k$:

$$\boxed{\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2} - \frac{1}{v^2}\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} = 0}$$

---

## Page 42 (Slide 42) — The Wave Equation

**The Wave Equation**

$$\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2} - \frac{1}{v^2}\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} = 0$$

- Any phenomenon satisfying this equation is a wave.
- The wave equation is Newton's law of motion applied to a continuous medium.
- The wave equation contains the speed of the corresponding wave.

---

## Page 43 (Slide 43) — 3D Wave Equation

**3D Wave Equation**

Extending the 1D wave expression to 3D:

$$y(x,t) = A\sin(kx-\omega t) \quad \Rightarrow \quad \psi(\vec{r},t) = A\sin\!\left(\vec{k}\cdot\vec{r} - \omega t\right)$$

- $\psi(\vec{r},t)$ — displacement of the medium at position $\vec{r}$ at time $t$
- $\vec{k} = k\hat{k} = k_x\hat{x} + k_y\hat{y} + k_z\hat{z}$ — **wave vector**: magnitude = wave number $k$, direction = propagation direction
- $\vec{k}\cdot\vec{r} = k_x x + k_y y + k_z z$

---

## Page 44 (Slide 44) — 3D Wave Equation (Derivation)

**3D Wave Equation**

The 1D wave equation generalizes to 3D as:

$$\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2} - \frac{1}{v^2}\frac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \nabla^2\psi(\vec{r},t) - \frac{1}{v^2}\frac{\partial^2\psi(\vec{r},t)}{\partial t^2} = 0$$

where the **Laplacian** is:

$$\nabla^2\psi = \frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial z^2}$$

The 3D wave expression is:

$$\psi(\vec{r},t) = A\sin\!\left(\vec{k}\cdot\vec{r} - \omega t\right) = A\sin(k_x x + k_y y + k_z z - \omega t)$$

---

## Page 45 (Slide 45) — Verification of the 3D Wave Equation

**Verification of the 3D Wave Equation**

From $\psi(\vec{r},t) = A\sin(\vec{k}\cdot\vec{r} - \omega t)$:

$$\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x^2} = -k_x^2 A\sin(\vec{k}\cdot\vec{r}-\omega t), \quad \frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial y^2} = -k_y^2 A\sin(\vec{k}\cdot\vec{r}-\omega t), \quad \frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial z^2} = -k_z^2 A\sin(\vec{k}\cdot\vec{r}-\omega t)$$

$$\nabla^2\psi = -(k_x^2+k_y^2+k_z^2)A\sin(\vec{k}\cdot\vec{r}-\omega t) = -k^2 A\sin(\vec{k}\cdot\vec{r}-\omega t) \checkmark$$

---

## Page 46 (Slide 46) — 1D vs. 3D Wave Equations

**Comparison of 1D and 3D Wave Equations**

| 1D Wave Equation | 3D Wave Equation |
|---|---|
| $y(x,t) = A\sin(kx-\omega t)$ | $\psi(\vec{r},t) = A\sin(\vec{k}\cdot\vec{r}-\omega t)$ |
| $\dfrac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2} = -k^2 A\sin(\cdots)$ | $\nabla^2\psi = -k^2 A\sin(\cdots)$ |
| $\dfrac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} = -\omega^2 A\sin(\cdots)$ | $\dfrac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial t^2} = -\omega^2 A\sin(\cdots)$ |
| $\dfrac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2} - \dfrac{1}{v^2}\dfrac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} = 0$ | $\nabla^2\psi - \dfrac{1}{v^2}\dfrac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial t^2} = 0$ |

## Page 47 (Slide 47) — Summary: Wave Equation

**Summary**

**Wave equation:** $\dfrac{\partial^2 y}{\partial x^2} - \dfrac{1}{v^2}\dfrac{\partial^2 y}{\partial t^2} = 0$

$$\nabla^2\psi(\vec{r},t) - \frac{1}{v^2}\frac{\partial^2\psi(\vec{r},t)}{\partial t^2} = 0$$

- The wave equation contains the speed of the wave.
- The wave speed is determined by the medium through which the wave travels.
- In the same medium, the wavelength and frequency of a wave are inversely proportional.

$$v = \frac{\lambda}{T} = \lambda f = \frac{\omega}{k}$$

## Page 49 (Slide 49) — Goal of Maxwell's Equations

**Goal of Maxwell's Equations**

Maxwell's equations — a system of four coupled differential equations obtained from the fundamental laws of electromagnetism:

$$\nabla \cdot \vec{E}(\vec{r}) = \frac{\rho(\vec{r})}{\epsilon_0}$$

$$\nabla \cdot \vec{B}(\vec{r}) = 0$$

$$\nabla \times \vec{B}(\vec{r}) = \mu_0\vec{J}(\vec{r}) + \mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial\vec{E}(\vec{r},t)}{\partial t}$$

$$\nabla \times \vec{E}(\vec{r}) = -\frac{\partial\vec{B}(\vec{r},t)}{\partial t}$$

**Goal:** Solve these differential equations to find the electric field $\vec{E}$ and magnetic field $\vec{B}$ as functions of position $\vec{r}$ and time $t$.

---

## Page 50 (Slide 50) — Source-Free Space ($\rho=0$, $\vec{J}=0$)

**Space with No Charge Distribution and No Current Distribution**

Setting $\rho(\vec{r})=0$ and $\vec{J}(\vec{r})=0$, Maxwell's equations simplify to:

$$\nabla \cdot \vec{E}(\vec{r}) = 0 \tag{1}$$

$$\nabla \cdot \vec{B}(\vec{r}) = 0 \tag{2}$$

$$\nabla \times \vec{B}(\vec{r}) = \mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial\vec{E}(\vec{r},t)}{\partial t} \tag{3}$$

$$\nabla \times \vec{E}(\vec{r}) = -\frac{\partial\vec{B}(\vec{r},t)}{\partial t} \tag{4}$$

*(Diagram: region of space with no charges or currents, surrounded by external sources)*

---

## Page 51 (Slide 51) — Solving the Coupled Equations (Part 1)

**Solving the Coupled Differential Equations**

**First goal:** derive a differential equation involving only $\vec{E}$ or only $\vec{B}$.

① Take the curl of both sides of equation (4):
$$\nabla \times (\nabla \times \vec{E}) = -\nabla \times \frac{\partial\vec{B}}{\partial t} \tag{5}$$

② Simplify the left side of (5) using the vector identity $\vec{a}\times(\vec{b}\times\vec{c}) = \vec{b}(\vec{a}\cdot\vec{c}) - (\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b})\vec{c}$:
$$\nabla \times (\nabla \times \vec{E}) = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\vec{E}) - (\nabla\cdot\nabla)\vec{E} = -\nabla^2\vec{E} \tag{6}$$

(using $\nabla\cdot\vec{E}=0$ from equation (1))

---

## Page 52 (Slide 52) — Solving the Coupled Equations (Part 2)

**Solving the Coupled Differential Equations**

③ Simplify the right side of (5):
$$-\nabla\times\frac{\partial\vec{B}}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\nabla\times\vec{B}) = -\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\!\left(\mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial\vec{E}}{\partial t}\right) = -\mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial^2\vec{E}}{\partial t^2} \tag{7}$$

(using equation (3) with $\vec{J}=0$)

---

## Page 53 (Slide 53) — Wave Equation for the Electric Field

**Wave Equation Satisfied by the Electric Field**

Substituting (6) into the left side and (7) into the right side of (5):

$$-\nabla^2\vec{E} = -\mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial^2\vec{E}}{\partial t^2}$$

$$\boxed{\nabla^2\vec{E} - \mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial^2\vec{E}}{\partial t^2} = 0}$$

This is the **wave equation for the electric field**.

---

## Page 54 (Slide 54) — Wave Equation for the Magnetic Field (Derivation)

**Wave Equation Satisfied by the Magnetic Field**

① Take the curl of both sides of equation (3):
$$\nabla\times(\nabla\times\vec{B}) = \mu_0\epsilon_0\nabla\times\frac{\partial\vec{E}}{\partial t} \tag{5}$$

② Left side:
$$\nabla\times(\nabla\times\vec{B}) = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\vec{B}) - (\nabla\cdot\nabla)\vec{B} = -\nabla^2\vec{B} \tag{6}$$

(using $\nabla\cdot\vec{B}=0$ from equation (2))

③ Right side:
$$\mu_0\epsilon_0\nabla\times\frac{\partial\vec{E}}{\partial t} = \mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\nabla\times\vec{E}) = \mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\!\left(-\frac{\partial\vec{B}}{\partial t}\right) = -\mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial^2\vec{B}}{\partial t^2} \tag{7}$$

---

## Page 55 (Slide 55) — Wave Equation for the Magnetic Field

**Wave Equation Satisfied by the Magnetic Field**

Substituting (6) and (7) into equation (5):

$$-\nabla^2\vec{B} = -\mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial^2\vec{B}}{\partial t^2}$$

$$\boxed{\nabla^2\vec{B} - \mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial^2\vec{B}}{\partial t^2} = 0}$$

---

## Page 56 (Slide 56) — Wave Equation vs. Equations Satisfied by $\vec{E}$ and $\vec{B}$

**Comparison**

| Wave Equation | Equations for $\vec{E}$ and $\vec{B}$ |
|---|---|
| $\nabla^2\psi - \dfrac{1}{v^2}\dfrac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial t^2} = 0$ | $\nabla^2\vec{E} - \mu_0\epsilon_0\dfrac{\partial^2\vec{E}}{\partial t^2} = 0$ |
| Wave speed $v$ | $\nabla^2\vec{B} - \mu_0\epsilon_0\dfrac{\partial^2\vec{B}}{\partial t^2} = 0$ |

$\Rightarrow$ **Speed of electromagnetic waves:**

$$\boxed{v = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0\epsilon_0}}}$$

From this result, **Maxwell predicted** that electric and magnetic fields would form electromagnetic waves.

---

## Page 57 (Slide 57) — Speed of Electromagnetic Waves

**Speed of Electromagnetic Waves**

$$\nabla^2\vec{E} - \mu_0\epsilon_0\frac{\partial^2\vec{E}}{\partial t^2} = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad v = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0\epsilon_0}}$$

From Coulomb's Law: $\dfrac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} = 8.99\times10^9\ \text{N m}^2/\text{C}^2$

From Biot–Savart Law: $\dfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi} = 10^{-7}\ \text{N/A}^2$

$$v = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0\epsilon_0}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\left(\dfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\right)\!\left(\dfrac{4\pi}{\epsilon_0}\right)}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(10^{-7})\!\left(\dfrac{1}{8.99\times10^9}\right)}}\ \text{m/s} = \mathbf{2.99\times10^8\ m/s}$$

This matches the measured speed of light!

---

## Page 58 (Slide 58) — Summary: Maxwell's Prediction

**Summary**

- From the fundamental laws of electromagnetism, four coupled differential equations — Maxwell's Equations — were derived.
- To solve the coupled equations, differential equations involving only $\vec{E}$ or only $\vec{B}$ were derived.
- These differential equations turned out to have the form of a **wave equation**.
- From this, **Maxwell predicted the existence of electromagnetic waves**.
- The wave speed embedded in Maxwell's wave equation matched the then-measured speed of light, leading Maxwell to conjecture that **light is a form of electromagnetic wave**.

## Page 60 (Slide 60) — History of Measuring the Speed of Light

**History of Measuring the Speed of Light**

**1638 — Galileo Galilei (Italy)**
- First attempted to measure the speed of light but failed.
- Concluded that the speed of light is at least ten times faster than the speed of sound.

**1675 — Ole Rømer (Denmark)**
- Observed eclipses of Jupiter's moon Io to prove that the speed of light is not infinite.
- Using the then-known orbital radius of Earth, calculated the speed of light as $2.27\times10^8\ \text{m/s}$.

**1727 — James Bradley (England)**
- Measured the speed of light from stellar aberration.
- Using the then-known orbital speed of Earth, estimated the speed of light as $3.01\times10^8\ \text{m/s}$.

## Page 61 (Slide 61) — Speed of Light

**Speed of Light**

**1849 — Hippolyte Fizeau (France)**
- Used a rotating toothed wheel to implement very short time intervals.
- Measured the speed of light as $3.13\times10^8\ \text{m/s}$.

**1862 — Léon Foucault (France)**
- Improved Fizeau's toothed wheel by replacing it with a rotating mirror apparatus.
- Over years of experiments, refined the value to $2.99796\times10^8\ \text{m/s}$.

**1865 — James Clerk Maxwell (England)**
- Theoretically predicted the existence of electromagnetic waves from solving Maxwell's equations.
- Calculated the speed of electromagnetic waves from the permittivity and permeability of vacuum as $2.99\times10^8\ \text{m/s}$.

- Came from a family of physicists who studied phosphorescence for three generations (grandfather, father, son).
- After hearing of Röntgen's discovery (X-rays emitted from a fluorescent screen excited by X-rays → visible light), Becquerel investigated the reverse: **whether a phosphorescent material excited by visible light might emit X-like penetrating rays**.
- **1896** — Accidentally discovered radioactive radiation.
- His discovery attracted little attention at first.
- Only after the **Curie couple** identified the source of the radiation did its importance become widely recognized.
- Becquerel and the Curies were jointly awarded the **1903 Nobel Prize in Physics**.

## Page 95 (Slide 95) — Becquerel's First Experiment

**Becquerel's First Experiment**

- Wrapped a photographic plate tightly in thick black paper.
- Placed phosphorescent material on top of the wrapped photographic plate and left it in sunlight for several hours.

## Page 102 (Slide 102) — Mechanical Waves

**Mechanical Waves vs. Electromagnetic Waves**

**Mechanical Waves:**
- A phenomenon in which oscillations of a medium propagate through the medium.
- Each object oscillates in place with the same oscillation but with a time delay; only the pattern propagates.
- Classified as **transverse waves** (e.g., waves on a string, water waves) or **longitudinal waves** (e.g., sound waves) depending on the direction of oscillation relative to the direction of propagation.
- Cannot exist **without a medium**.
- Exhibit interference and diffraction — characteristic wave phenomena.
- Each type of wave has a unique mechanism for transmitting oscillation through the medium.

---

## Page 103 (Slide 103) — Electromagnetic Waves

**Mechanical Waves vs. Electromagnetic Waves**

**Electromagnetic Waves:**
- A phenomenon in which electric and magnetic fields propagate while changing their magnitudes.
- The mechanism by which electromagnetic waves propagate is **electromagnetic induction**.
- Electromagnetic waves are **transverse waves**.
- Electromagnetic waves can exist in a **vacuum** (no medium required).
  - $\Rightarrow$ Matter through which electromagnetic waves pass is called the **medium** of the electromagnetic wave.
- Exhibit **interference and diffraction** — characteristic wave phenomena.

## Page 104 (Slide 104) — Electromagnetic Waves and the Poynting Vector

**Electromagnetic Waves and the Poynting Vector**

*(Diagram of an electromagnetic wave: $\vec{E}$ oscillating in the $y$-direction, $\vec{B}$ oscillating in the $z$-direction, propagating in the $x$-direction)*

*(Portrait of John Poynting, England, 1852–1914)*

**Poynting vector:**
$$\vec{S} = \vec{E} \times \vec{B}$$

Derived by Poynting from Maxwell's equations.

- The **direction** of $\vec{S}$ represents the direction of propagation of the electromagnetic wave.
- The **magnitude** of $\vec{S}$ represents the energy of the electromagnetic wave passing through a unit area per unit time.

## Page 109 (Slide 109) — Speed of Electromagnetic Waves

**Speed of Electromagnetic Waves**

The speed of a wave is determined by its medium.

**Speed of electromagnetic waves in a medium:**
$$v = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu\epsilon}}$$

where $\epsilon$ = permittivity of the medium, $\mu$ = permeability of the medium.

$\Rightarrow$ All electromagnetic waves — regardless of frequency (wavelength) — travel at this same speed in the same medium.

**Speed of electromagnetic waves in vacuum:**
$$c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0\epsilon_0}} = 2.99\times10^8\ \text{m/s}$$

$\Rightarrow$ The speed of light in vacuum $c$ is the **maximum possible speed**.

## Page 111 (Slide 111) — Summary: Electromagnetic Waves

**Summary**

- Electromagnetic waves are waves that propagate through changes in the magnitudes of electric and magnetic fields, driven by electromagnetic induction.
- Electromagnetic waves are classified by frequency into: **radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays, and gamma rays**.
- The colors of visible light change continuously with frequency; Newton divided the visible spectrum into **seven colors**.
- Harmful electromagnetic waves are all **reflected or absorbed by the atmosphere**; only harmless visible light and radio waves reach the ground.
