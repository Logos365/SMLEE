## Page 3 — Force and Work

**Force**

*(Diagram: a person pushing a block to the right with force F)*

A exerts force $F$ on B.

**Work**

*(Diagram: a person pushing a block over a distance d from start to end, with force F pointing right)*

A does work $W = Fd$ on B.

---

## Page 4 — Work (Definition)

When A continuously applies force $\vec{F}$ on B and displaces B by $\vec{d}$, the work done by A on B is $W = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{d}$.

$$W = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{d} = Fd\cos\theta$$

- $W$ is a **scalar**
- $\vec{F} \cdot \vec{d}$ is the **scalar (dot) product of two vectors**

---

## Page 5 — Cases Where No Work Is Done

Condition for $W = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{d} = Fd\cos\theta = 0$:

**(1) $F = 0$** — If no force is applied, no work is done.

**(2) $d = 0$** — If there is no displacement, no work is done.

**(3) $\theta = 90°$** — If there is no displacement in the direction of the force, no work is done.

---

## Page 6 — Energy (Definition)

**Definition of energy:** The ability to do work.

**Work and Energy:**

- Work: We say that A does $W$ amount of work on B.
- Energy: We say that A's energy is $E$.

**When A does work on B:**

A's energy decreases by the amount of work done, and B's energy increases by the amount of work received.

---

## Page 7 — Units of Work and Energy

**SI Units**

- Unit of force: $F = ma \Rightarrow 1\ \text{kg·m/s}^2 = 1\ \text{N}$ (newton)
- Unit of work: $W = Fd \Rightarrow 1\ \text{N·m} = 1\ \text{kg·m}^2/\text{s}^2 = 1\ \text{J}$ (joule)

**CGS Units**

- Unit of force: $F = ma \Rightarrow 1\ \text{g·cm/s}^2 = 1\ \text{dyne}$ (dyne)
- Unit of work: $W = Fd \Rightarrow 1\ \text{dyne·cm} = 1\ \text{g·cm}^2/\text{s}^2 = 1\ \text{erg}$ (erg)

> **The unit of energy is the same as the unit of work.**

---

## Page 8 — Types of Energy

- **Kinetic energy** — the ability to do work possessed by a moving object due to its speed
- **Potential energy** — the ability to do work possessed by objects subject to a conservative force, due to their relative positions
- **Light energy** — the ability to do work carried by light
- **Electrical energy** — the ability to do work carried by electrical phenomena
- $\cdots$

Energy exists in many different forms.

When an object receives work and its energy increases, which form of energy increases depends on the circumstances.

---

## Page 9 — Thermal Energy

**Caloric theory:** Until the early 19th century, people believed that thermal phenomena were caused by a substance similar to a chemical element, called "caloric," unrelated to the motion of matter. Heat was therefore expressed in the separate unit of **calories**.

**Mechanical equivalent of heat:** Later, scientists discovered that thermal phenomena arise from the kinetic energy of the disordered motion of countless constituent particles, showing that heat is a form of energy. In 1840, the British physicist Joule experimentally demonstrated that 1 calorie of heat corresponds to 4.2 joules of energy.

$$W = JQ \Rightarrow J = 4.2\ \text{J/cal}$$

*(J here is the mechanical equivalent of heat)*

---

## Page 10 — Mass Energy

**Mass (19th century view):** Until the 19th century, mass was thought to be the origin of inertia or a property representing inertia. Mass was expressed in the unit of kilograms.

**Special relativity (1905):** Einstein derived from special relativity the equation $E = mc^2$, which revealed that mass is also a form of energy.

$$E = mc^2 \Rightarrow c^2 = (3 \times 10^8\ \text{m/s})^2 = 9 \times 10^{16}\ \text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$$

Conversion factor from mass to energy:

$$m = 1\ \text{kg} \Rightarrow E = mc^2 = (1\ \text{kg})\times(9\times10^{16}\ \text{m}^2/\text{s}^2) = 9\times10^{16}\ \text{J}$$

---

## Page 11 — Conservation of Energy

Energy, whenever conditions are met,

$$\text{one form of energy} \longrightarrow \text{another form of energy} \checkmark$$

However, **the total amount of energy does not decrease or increase** during such a transformation.

*(Conservation of energy)*

## Page 12 — Section 06B: Work by a Variable Force / Line Integrals

**06B — Work by a Variable Force / Line Integrals**

*(Section title slide)*

---

## Page 13 — Work Done by a Constant Force

*(Diagram: a person pushing a block from start to end over distance $d$ with constant force $F$)*

A does work $W = Fd$ on B.

> This holds only when $F$ does not change while A is applying force to B.

---

## Page 14 — Work Done by a Variable Force (Discrete Approximation)

*(Diagram left: F(x) curve with rectangular strips of width $\Delta x$ at positions $x_i$; diagram right: the same with finer strips)*

When an object moves from $x_i$ to $x_i + \Delta x$, if the force remains $F(x_i)$ during that interval, the work done is $\Delta W = F(x_i)\Delta x$.

The total work done as the object moves from $a$ to $b$:

$$W_{a \to b} = \sum_i F(x_i)\,\Delta x$$

---

## Page 15 — Work Done by a Variable Force (Integral Form)

*(Diagram left: infinitesimal strip of width $dx$ at position $x$; diagram right: shaded area under $F(x)$ from $a$ to $b$)*

When an object moves from $x$ to $x + dx$, the force is $F(x)$ and does not change, so the work done is $dW = F(x)\,dx$.

The total work done as the object moves from $a$ to $b$:

$$W = \int_a^b F(x)\,dx$$

---

## Page 16 — Work Done by a Variable Force (1D vs. 3D)

**1 Dimension**

$$W = Fd \Rightarrow W = \int_a^b F(x)\,dx$$

*Integrate with endpoints $a$ and $b$ given.*

**3 Dimensions**

$$W = \vec{F}\cdot\vec{d} \Rightarrow W = \int_C \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r}$$

*Integrate along a given path $C$.* (This is a **line integral**.)

---

## Page 17 — Practice: Line Integrals

Given $\vec{F}(\vec{r}) = \vec{r}$

*(Diagram: xy-plane with three paths $C_1$, $C_2$, $C_3$ all connecting origin (0,0) to point (4,4))*

**Path $C_1$:**
- $y = 0$ when $0 \le x \le 4$
- $x = 4$ when $0 \le y \le 4$

**Path $C_2$:**
- $y = x$ when $0 \le x \le 4$

**Path $C_3$:**
- $x^2 + (y-4)^2 = 4^2$ when $0 \le x \le 4$

---

## Page 18 — Line Integral Along Path $C_1$

$$\int_{C_1} \vec{F}(\vec{r})\cdot d\vec{r}$$

$$\vec{F}(\vec{r}) = \vec{r} = x\hat{x} + y\hat{y} + z\hat{z}$$

$$d\vec{r} = \hat{x}\,dx + \hat{y}\,dy + \hat{z}\,dz$$

$$\Rightarrow \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r} = x\,dx + y\,dy + z\,dz$$

**(1)** When $0 \le x \le 4$: $y = 0$, $z = 0$

$$\vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r} = x\,dx \Rightarrow \int_0^4 x\,dx = \frac{x^2}{2}\bigg|_0^4 = 8$$

**(2)** When $0 \le y \le 4$: $x = 4$, $dx = 0$, $z = 0$

$$\vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r} = y\,dy \Rightarrow \int_0^4 y\,dy = \frac{y^2}{2}\bigg|_0^4 = 8$$

$$\Rightarrow \int_{C_1} \vec{F}(\vec{r})\cdot d\vec{r} = 8 + 8 = 16$$

---

## Page 19 — Line Integral Along Path $C_2$

$$\int_{C_2} \vec{F}(\vec{r})\cdot d\vec{r}$$

$$\vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r} = x\,dx + y\,dy + z\,dz$$

$y = x$, $dy = dx$, $z = 0$

$$\vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r} = x\,dx + x\,dx = 2x\,dx$$

$$\Rightarrow \int_0^4 2x\,dx = x^2\bigg|_0^4 = 16$$

---

## Page 20 — Line Integral Along Path $C_3$

$$\int_{C_3} \vec{F}(\vec{r})\cdot d\vec{r}$$

$$\vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r} = x\,dx + y\,dy + z\,dz$$

$x^2 + (y-4)^2 = 4^2$, $z = 0$

$$\Rightarrow 2x\,dx + 2(y-4)\,dy = 0$$

$$\Rightarrow x\,dx + y\,dy = 4\,dy$$

$$\Rightarrow \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r} = 4\,dy$$

$$\Rightarrow \int_0^4 4\,dy = 4y\bigg|_0^4 = 16$$

> **The line integral values along all three paths are equal.**
>
> If there is a reason why the integral value is always the same regardless of the path (as long as the start and end points are the same), the integration path need not be specified.

---

## Page 21 — Summary: Line Integrals

An integral that sums the integrand function along a given path is called a **line integral**.

A line integral must always specify the integration path.

However, if the integral value is always the same regardless of the path, the specification of the integration path may be omitted.

## Page 22 — Section 06C: Work-Energy Theorem

**06C — Work-Energy Theorem**

*(Section title slide)*

---

## Page 23 — Constant Force vs. Variable Force

**Constant force:** $\vec{F} = m\vec{a}$ → uniformly accelerated motion; only the acceleration needs to be found.

**Variable force:** $\vec{F} = m\dfrac{d\vec{v}}{dt}$ → first-order differential equation.

What condition allows this to be solved by integration?

---

## Page 24 — 1D: Force Depending on $x$

**Equation of motion:** $F(x) = m\dfrac{dv}{dt}$ → not directly integrable

$$F(x) = m\frac{dv}{dx}\frac{dx}{dt} = mv\frac{dv}{dx} \Rightarrow \text{integrable form}$$

$$\Rightarrow F(x)\,dx = mv\,dv$$

$$\Rightarrow \int_{x_0}^{x} F(x)\,dx = \int_{v_0}^{v} mv\,dv$$

---

## Page 25 — 3D: Force Depending on $\vec{r}$

**Equation of motion:** $\vec{F}(\vec{r}) = m\dfrac{d\vec{v}}{dt}$ → not directly integrable

$$\vec{F}(\vec{r})\cdot d\vec{r} = m\frac{d\vec{v}}{dt}\cdot d\vec{r} = m\,d\vec{v}\cdot\frac{d\vec{r}}{dt} = m\vec{v}\cdot d\vec{v} \Rightarrow \text{integrable form}$$

$$\Rightarrow \int_{\vec{r}_0}^{\vec{r}} \vec{F}(\vec{r})\cdot d\vec{r} = \int_{v_0}^{v} mv\,dv$$

---

## Page 26 — Derivative of the Scalar Product of Two Vectors

Differential of $\vec{a}$: $d\vec{a} = \vec{a}' - \vec{a}$, so $\vec{a}' = \vec{a} + d\vec{a}$

Differential of $\vec{b}$: $d\vec{b} = \vec{b}' - \vec{b}$, so $\vec{b}' = \vec{b} + d\vec{b}$

Differential of $y = \vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}$:

$$dy = y' - y = \vec{a}'\cdot\vec{b}' - \vec{a}\cdot\vec{b} = (\vec{a}+d\vec{a})\cdot(\vec{b}+d\vec{b}) - \vec{a}\cdot\vec{b} = d\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b} + \vec{a}\cdot d\vec{b}$$

$$y = \vec{a}\cdot\vec{b} \Rightarrow dy = d\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b} + \vec{a}\cdot d\vec{b}$$

---

Since $\vec{v}\cdot\vec{v} = v^2$:

Left side: $d(\vec{v}\cdot\vec{v}) = d\vec{v}\cdot\vec{v} + \vec{v}\cdot d\vec{v} = 2\vec{v}\cdot d\vec{v}$

Right side: $d(v^2) = 2v\,dv$

$$\Rightarrow \vec{v}\cdot d\vec{v} = v\,dv$$

---

## Page 27 — 3D: Force Depending on $\vec{r}$ (continued)

$$\vec{F}(\vec{r})\cdot d\vec{r} = m\vec{v}\cdot d\vec{v}$$

$$\vec{F}(\vec{r})\cdot d\vec{r} = mv\,dv \Rightarrow \text{integrable form}$$

$$\int_{\vec{r}_0}^{\vec{r}} \vec{F}(\vec{r})\cdot d\vec{r} = \int_{v_0}^{v} mv\,dv$$

---

## Page 28 — Work (Left-Hand Side)

Integrating the equation of motion $F(x) = m\dfrac{dv}{dt}$ once:

$$\int_{\vec{r}_0}^{\vec{r}} \vec{F}(\vec{r})\cdot d\vec{r} = \int_{v_0}^{v} mv\,dv$$

**Left side:**

$$W_{\vec{r}_0 \to \vec{r}} = \int_{\vec{r}_0}^{\vec{r}} \vec{F}(\vec{r})\cdot d\vec{r}$$

= the work done by the net force on the object as it moves from $\vec{r}_0$ to $\vec{r}$

---

## Page 29 — Kinetic Energy (Right-Hand Side)

$$\int_{\vec{r}_0}^{\vec{r}} \vec{F}(\vec{r})\cdot d\vec{r} = \int_{v_0}^{v} mv\,dv$$

**Right side:**

$$\int_{v_0}^{v} mv\,dv = \frac{1}{2}mv^2\bigg|_{v_0}^{v} = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 - \frac{1}{2}mv_0^2$$

**Kinetic energy:** $K \equiv \dfrac{1}{2}mv^2$

$$\Rightarrow \int_{v_0}^{v} mv\,dv = K - K_0 = \Delta K$$

---

## Page 30 — Work-Energy Theorem

$$\vec{F}(\vec{r}) = m\frac{d\vec{v}}{dt}$$

$$\Downarrow$$

$$\int_{\vec{r}_0}^{\vec{r}} \vec{F}(\vec{r})\cdot d\vec{r} = \int_{v_0}^{v} mv\,dv$$

$$\Downarrow$$

$$\boxed{W_{\vec{r}_0 \to \vec{r}} = \Delta K}$$

**Work-Energy Theorem**

- The work-energy theorem is identical in content to Newton's law of motion.
- The work-energy theorem also applies when a variable force acts.
- According to the work-energy theorem, the work done by the net force always becomes kinetic energy.

---

## Page 31 — Work Done by the Net Force

*(Diagram: person pulling a box on a surface; forces N, F, f, mg are labeled)*

When multiple forces act on an object, the net force is:

$$\vec{F} = \vec{F}_1 + \vec{F}_2 + \vec{F}_3$$

Work done by each force:

$$W_1 = \int_C \vec{F}_1\cdot d\vec{r}, \quad W_2 = \int_C \vec{F}_2\cdot d\vec{r}, \quad W_3 = \int_C \vec{F}_3\cdot d\vec{r}$$

Work done by the net force:

$$W = \int_C \vec{F}\cdot d\vec{r} = \int_C (\vec{F}_1 + \vec{F}_2 + \vec{F}_3)\cdot d\vec{r} = \int_C \vec{F}_1\cdot d\vec{r} + \int_C \vec{F}_2\cdot d\vec{r} + \int_C \vec{F}_3\cdot d\vec{r}$$

$$\Rightarrow W = W_1 + W_2 + W_3$$

> **The work done by the net force equals the sum of the work done by each individual force!**

---

## Page 32 — Summary: Work-Energy Theorem

**Newton's equation of motion:** $\vec{F} = m\vec{a}$

**Work-energy theorem:** $W = \Delta K$

Newton's equation of motion and the work-energy theorem are equivalent. The former expresses the law of motion using force and acceleration; the latter expresses it using work and kinetic energy.

The work-energy theorem can be easily applied even when a variable force acts on an object.

## Page 34 — Energy Review

**Definition of energy:** the ability to do work

**Work and energy:**
- Work: We say that A does $W$ amount of work on B.
- Energy: We say that A's energy is $E$.

**When A does work on B:**

A's energy decreases by the amount of work done, and B's energy increases by the amount of work received.

---

However, the form of energy that increases in B differs case by case.

**Work-energy theorem:** The net force increases the object's kinetic energy by the amount of work done.

---

## Page 35 — Potential Energy

Work done on an object that is already subject to another force can also increase **potential energy**.

- Work done on an object subject to **gravity** → increase in **gravitational potential energy**
- Work done on an object subject to **universal gravitation** → increase in **gravitational (universal) potential energy**
- Work done on an object subject to **elastic force** → increase in **elastic potential energy**
- Work done on an object subject to **electric force** → increase in **electric potential energy**
- Work done on an object subject to **friction** → increase in "frictional potential energy" ✗ *(not valid)*

*(Same content as page 35, summarized as a classification)*

- Gravity, universal gravitation, elastic force, electric force → **conservative forces**
- Friction → **non-conservative force**

## Page 37 — Conservative and Non-Conservative Forces (Detail)

**Conservative force:** A force representable by a potential energy.

Most forces defined by force laws are conservative forces.

**Non-conservative force:** A force not representable by a potential energy.

Example: friction

**Constraint force:** Normal force and tension do not need to be classified as conservative or non-conservative.

Constraint forces always act perpendicular to the motion of the object.

**Kinetic energy** — energy possessed by a moving object

**Potential energy** — not energy possessed by a single object

> Potential energy (potential energy) is NOT energy possessed by an object due to its position — it is not energy of a single object!

## Page 39 — Gravitational Potential Energy

*(Diagram: a person using a pulley to slowly lift a block of mass $m$ upward by distance $d$; forces $F$ (upward) and $mg$ (downward) shown)*

I slowly lift an object of mass $m$ by applying force $F$.

$$\Rightarrow F = mg$$

Work done by my force $F$ as I lift the object by displacement $d$:

---

## Page 40 — Gravitational Potential Energy (Calculation)

*(Diagram: pulley system with block raised by $d$, showing force $F$ upward and $mg$ downward)*

I slowly lift an object of mass $m$ by applying force $F$.

Work done by my force $F$ as I lift by displacement $d$:

$$W_F = +Fd = +mgd$$

Work done by gravity $mg$ on the object:

$$W_g = -mgd$$

> Where does the work I did with force $F$ get stored?

---

## Page 41 — Elastic Potential Energy

*(Diagram: spring attached to wall, with block being pulled to the right by force $F$; spring compressed by $x$)*

I slowly pull an object of mass $m$ attached to a spring to the right by displacement $x$ using force $F$.

$$F = kx$$

Work done by my force $F$ as I pull by displacement $x$:

$$W_F = \int_0^x F\,dx = \int_0^x kx\,dx = \frac{1}{2}kx^2$$

Work done by the spring's elastic force on the object as it moves displacement $x$:

$$W_k = \int_0^x (-kx)\,dx = -\frac{1}{2}kx^2$$

> Where does the work I did with force $F$ get stored?

---

## Page 42 — Gravitational and Elastic Potential Energy (Summary)

**Gravitational potential energy:**

The work $mgd$ I did on the object is stored as gravitational potential energy. This equals the negative of the work done by gravity on the object.

**Elastic potential energy:**

The work $\dfrac{1}{2}kx^2$ I did on the object is stored as elastic potential energy. This equals the negative of the work done by the elastic force on the object.

---

## Page 43 — Definition of Potential Energy

Potential energy is defined separately for each force.

- Gravitational potential energy, universal gravitational potential energy, elastic potential energy, electric potential energy, …

The force for which potential energy is defined is called a **conservative force**.

**Definition of potential energy $V(\vec{r})$ for a conservative force $\vec{F}(\vec{r})$:**

$$V(\vec{r}) = -\int_{\vec{r}_s}^{\vec{r}} \vec{F}(\vec{r})\,d\vec{r}$$

*(The lower limit $\vec{r}_s$ is the **reference point** of the potential energy.)*
