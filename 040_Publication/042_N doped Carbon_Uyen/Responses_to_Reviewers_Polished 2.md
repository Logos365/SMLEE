### Comment C1: XPS Overlap between Residual g-C₃N₄ and Pyridinic-N

> **Reviewer's Comment:** The overlap between residual g-C₃N₄ C=N–C (~398.1 eV) and pyridinic-N (~398.3–398.8 eV) in XPS makes it difficult to accurately quantify lattice-incorporated pyridinic nitrogen, especially in NCF-600 and NCF-700. Please explain.

**Response:**

We thank the reviewer for raising this important and technically incisive point. We fully acknowledge that the close proximity of the N 1s binding energies of g-C₃N₄ C=N–C moieties (~398.1 eV) and lattice-incorporated pyridinic-N (~398.3–398.8 eV) presents an inherent and well-known limitation for unambiguous peak deconvolution by XPS alone. We have explicitly discussed this limitation in the revised manuscript and have provided a more rigorous, multi-technique interpretation by cross-correlating the XPS results with thermogravimetric analysis (TGA).

**Scientific rationale:**

The deconvoluted N 1s spectrum of pristine g-C₃N₄ is characterized by three well-resolved components: ~398.1 eV (C=N–C bridging nitrogen), ~399.4 eV ((C)₃–N tertiary nitrogen), and ~400.7 eV (N–H amino groups), which serve as diagnostic fingerprints of the polymeric g-C₃N₄ framework (shown in Figure S3 of the revised manuscript). TGA of the CF/g-C₃N₄ precursor reveals two distinct mass-loss events: cellulose decomposition at approximately 350 °C and g-C₃N₄ thermal degradation in the range of 500–700 °C. This means that at pyrolysis temperatures of 600 and 700 °C, the g-C₃N₄ precursor has not yet fully decomposed, and residual g-C₃N₄-derived nitrogen species can be expected to persist within the sample.

For **NCF-600**, the coexistence of all three characteristic g-C₃N₄ nitrogen environments in the XPS spectrum strongly suggests that a substantial fraction of the nitrogen remains embedded within the incompletely decomposed polymeric g-C₃N₄ framework rather than being incorporated into a conductive carbon lattice. Accordingly, a significant portion of the signal observed in the ~398 eV binding energy region for NCF-600 is most likely attributable to residual g-C₃N₄-derived C=N–C moieties rather than exclusively to electrochemically active, lattice-incorporated pyridinic-N. This interpretation is also consistent with the very low Raman I_D/I_G ratio of NCF-600 (0.44), which reflects a poorly developed sp² carbon network characteristic of incomplete carbonization rather than a true low-defect, graphitized carbon structure.

For **NCF-700**, TGA indicates that this temperature represents a transitional state in which the bulk decomposition of g-C₃N₄ is nearly complete, yet the nitrogen species have not fully reorganized into well-incorporated, electrochemically effective lattice configurations. A significant fraction of the pyridinic-N signal in NCF-700 is therefore likely associated with residual or partially reorganized g-C₃N₄-derived C=N moieties or nitrogen species localized in poorly connected carbon domains, which are less accessible for proton-coupled redox reactions.

For samples pyrolyzed at **≥800 °C**, TGA confirms complete thermal decomposition of g-C₃N₄, and the nitrogen species undergo progressive reconstruction into well-incorporated lattice configurations. Consequently, the N 1s signal in the 398.3–398.8 eV range for NCF-800, NCF-900, and NCF-1000 can be attributed with considerably greater confidence to lattice-incorporated, electrochemically active pyridinic-N. Among these, NCF-900 exhibits the highest pyridinic-N fraction (36.15%) among samples with confirmed complete g-C₃N₄ decomposition, while simultaneously displaying the highest Raman I_D/I_G ratio (0.87), reflecting an optimal balance between nitrogen-induced lattice disorder and graphitic ordering.

We have emphasized in the revised manuscript that these observations do not invalidate the XPS-based nitrogen speciation analysis for the higher-temperature samples, but rather necessitate a more careful, thermally contextualized interpretation for NCF-600 and NCF-700 specifically.

#### Part 2: Influence of Electrolyte pH on Pyridinic-N Activity

We agree with the reviewer that the electrolyte environment can significantly modulate the electrochemical activity of nitrogen-containing functional groups, particularly pyridinic-N. Although a systematic, quantitative pH-dependent study was beyond the primary scope of this work, we have evaluated the electrochemical response of the NCF-T series in both strongly acidic (1 M H₂SO₄, pH ≈ 0) and strongly alkaline (6 M KOH, pH ≈ 14) electrolytes, and we discuss the pH-dependent charge-storage behavior in the context of the proposed mechanism.

**Key experimental observations supporting pH-dependent behavior:**

- In **6 M KOH** (symmetric two-electrode, Figure 4a), the CV curves of NCF-900 maintain quasi-rectangular profiles at 5 mV s⁻¹, characteristic of predominantly electric double-layer capacitance (EDLC) with limited faradaic contribution. The specific capacitance in this system reaches 139.6 F g⁻¹ at 0.5 A g⁻¹.

- In **1 M H₂SO₄** (three-electrode, Figure 4c), the CV curves of NCF-900 exhibit pronounced broad redox humps superimposed on the capacitive background — clear evidence of additional pseudocapacitive contributions. The areal capacitance in the H₂SO₄ system reaches 789.4 mF cm⁻² at 1 mA cm⁻², reflecting the enhanced charge storage arising from proton-coupled surface redox reactions. NCF-900 outperforms all other NCF-T samples across the entire current density range tested (1–10 mA cm⁻², Figure S7b).

**Mechanistic interpretation:**

The more pronounced pseudocapacitive behavior in the acidic electrolyte is fully consistent with the proposed proton-coupled charge-storage mechanism centered on pyridinic-N sites. Under acidic conditions, the abundant proton supply (H⁺ activity ≈ 1 M) directly enables the reversible redox reaction:

**–C=N–C– + H⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ –C=NH–C–**

This reaction requires both a proton donor (favored in acidic media) and an electron acceptor (the pyridinic-N lone pair). Under alkaline conditions (OH⁻-dominated environment, H⁺ activity ≈ 10⁻¹⁴ M), the concentration of free protons is dramatically lower, suppressing the proton-coupled redox contribution and shifting the charge-storage mechanism toward EDLC-dominated behavior via electrostatic K⁺ and OH⁻ adsorption.

This pH-dependent behavior is further supported by our DFT calculations, which reveal a highly favorable proton adsorption energy of *E*_ads = −2.58 eV at pyridinic-N sites — by far the most negative among all four nitrogen configurations examined (pyrrolic-N: +1.62 eV; graphitic-N: +1.42 eV; oxidized-N: −0.70 eV). The strongly exothermic proton adsorption at pyridinic-N sites provides a thermodynamic driving force for the proton-coupled electron transfer reaction under acidic conditions, which is absent at the other nitrogen configurations.

### Comment C1: Surface Sensitivity of XPS and Need for Complementary Characterization

> **Reviewer's Comment:** There has always been argument over the relationship between nitrogen configurations doped in carbon structures and the charge storage performance. This study analyzes the relative contents of various nitrogen configurations using XPS results. Other characterization techniques are required to supplement the experiments, as the limited detection depth of XPS means that the analyzed nitrogen configurations only represent the surface phase.

**Response:**

We sincerely thank the reviewer for raising this important methodological concern. The reviewer is entirely correct that XPS is inherently a surface-sensitive technique, with a typical information depth of approximately 3–10 nm (depending on the photoelectron kinetic energy and the material), and therefore provides no direct information about the nitrogen distribution in the bulk of the carbon fibers.

However, we respectfully argue that the surface sensitivity of XPS is not a limitation in the context of this specific study, but rather a methodological strength that aligns with the fundamental nature of supercapacitor charge storage. We articulate this reasoning as follows:

**1. The synthesis strategy is designed to concentrate nitrogen at the fiber surface.**

In our approach, exfoliated g-C₃N₄ nanosheets were deposited onto the cotton fabric surface via ultrasonication-assisted coating prior to pyrolysis. The g-C₃N₄ loading (~5 mg cm⁻²) was applied as an external surface layer rather than being impregnated into the fiber interior. Consequently, the nitrogen dopants introduced during pyrolysis are by design expected to be concentrated in the near-surface region of the carbon fibers, which is precisely the region probed by XPS.

**2. Cross-sectional EDS analysis directly confirms surface-concentrated nitrogen distribution.**

To address the reviewer's concern with direct spatial evidence, we have added cross-sectional SEM/EDS characterization of NCF-900 in the revised manuscript (Figure S1c). A cross-sectional view of a representative NCF-900 fiber was prepared, and EDS point spectra were acquired at three distinct positions across the fiber cross-section:

- **Spectrum 1 (outer fiber surface):** A distinct and strong nitrogen signal is clearly detected, confirming the presence of nitrogen-containing species at the near-surface region of the fiber.
- **Spectrum 2 (intermediate region):** The nitrogen signal is considerably weaker, suggesting a rapid compositional gradient from surface to interior.
- **Spectrum 3 (fiber core):** The nitrogen signal is negligible (~absent), indicating that the fiber interior consists essentially of undoped carbon, consistent with the surface-coating synthesis strategy.

This spatially resolved EDS evidence directly confirms that the nitrogen functionalities are predominantly enriched in the surface and near-surface regions of the carbon fibers — precisely the domain probed by XPS, and precisely the domain relevant to electrochemical charge storage at the electrode–electrolyte interface.

**3. Surface-accessible nitrogen species are the electrochemically relevant ones.**

Electrochemical charge storage in supercapacitors — whether through electric double-layer formation or pseudocapacitive redox reactions — occurs at the accessible electrode–electrolyte interface. Nitrogen species buried within the bulk interior of carbon fibers are effectively electrochemically inactive because they cannot interact with electrolyte ions or protons. Therefore, the nitrogen configurations characterized by XPS are not merely representative of the "surface phase" in a general sense; they are specifically representative of the **electrochemically active phase**, which is precisely what governs the device performance.

We have revised the manuscript to explicitly clarify this point: the conclusions of this work are framed in terms of electrochemically accessible nitrogen species rather than bulk nitrogen composition, and the cross-sectional EDS data provide independent confirmation that this is the relevant characterization domain.

**Detailed interpretation of the data:**

The Table 3 data, combined with the electrochemical results, provide compelling evidence that neither the total nitrogen content nor the surface area alone is the determinant of electrochemical performance. Instead, the results point to a **synergistic interplay** among nitrogen configuration, nitrogen accessibility, and porous structure:

**(a) Total nitrogen content is not the primary determinant:**

NCF-600 possesses by far the highest total nitrogen content (15.34 at.%) among all samples, yet its specific capacitance in 6 M KOH is the lowest among the NCF-T series. This inverse relationship between total nitrogen content and electrochemical performance directly demonstrates that total nitrogen content is not the primary governing parameter. As discussed in response to C1, the high apparent nitrogen content in NCF-600 reflects predominantly residual g-C₃N₄-derived species rather than electrochemically active, lattice-incorporated nitrogen, which explains the poor device performance.

**(b) Nitrogen configuration and accessibility are decisive:**

Despite having a lower total nitrogen content (8.37 at.%) and even a slightly lower absolute pyridinic-N content (3.03 at.%) compared to NCF-600 (6.54 at.%), NCF-900 delivers markedly superior electrochemical performance. The key distinction is that in NCF-900, the pyridinic-N species are thermally well-incorporated into the carbon lattice, positioned at accessible edge and defect sites, and capable of participating in reversible proton-coupled electron transfer — none of which is true for the incompletely carbonized NCF-600.

Furthermore, NCF-800 and NCF-900 have almost identical total nitrogen contents (8.32 vs. 8.37 at.%), yet NCF-900 outperforms NCF-800 electrochemically (specific capacitance 139.6 vs. ~100 F g⁻¹ at 0.5 A g⁻¹ in 6 M KOH; BET surface area 646.2 vs. 470.2 m² g⁻¹). This performance difference, where nitrogen content is essentially equal, highlights the additional role of porous structure — NCF-900 benefits from a substantially higher BET surface area and more developed mesoporosity.

**(c) The role of BET surface area is real, but not dominant alone:**

The reviewer correctly identifies that the specific surface areas differ significantly among samples — NCF-900 achieves 646.2 m² g⁻¹ (type IV isotherm, mesopores centered at ~3.9 nm), nearly twice that of the control and significantly higher than NCF-600 (229.5 m² g⁻¹), NCF-700 (416.3 m² g⁻¹), NCF-800 (470.2 m² g⁻¹), and NCF-1000 (491.2 m² g⁻¹). The higher surface area of NCF-900 unquestionably facilitates electrolyte accessibility and ion transport, contributing to enhanced capacitance. However, the transition from NCF-900 to NCF-1000 tells a different story: despite NCF-1000 having a higher BET surface area than NCF-700 and NCF-600, its capacitance is among the lowest. This decrease is accompanied by a marked reduction in the absolute content of electrochemically active pyridinic-N (from 3.03 at.% at 900 °C to 0.41 at.% at 1000 °C), demonstrating that the loss of active nitrogen sites — not surface area — is the limiting factor in this regime.

**(d) Synergistic conclusion:**

The superior electrochemical performance of NCF-900 is therefore best understood as emerging from the simultaneous satisfaction of three requirements: (i) a sufficient density of electrochemically active, lattice-incorporated pyridinic-N sites (3.03 at.%); (ii) the highest BET surface area (646.2 m² g⁻¹) with well-developed mesoporosity (~3.9 nm); and (iii) the highest defect density as evidenced by the maximum I_D/I_G ratio (0.87), which introduces the localized electronic states favorable for proton-coupled electron transfer. No single parameter independently controls the performance; rather, their concurrence at 900 °C creates the optimal electrode architecture.
