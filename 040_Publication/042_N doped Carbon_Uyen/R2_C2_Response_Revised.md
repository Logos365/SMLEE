# Response to Reviewer 2, Comment C2

> **Reviewer's Comment:** Furthermore, the research did not provide data on the overall nitrogen content in the carbon. If the total nitrogen content is low, the effect of nitrogen configurations on performance may not be the primary factor, as the BET data indicate that the specific surface areas of the materials differ as well.

**Response:**

We thank the reviewer for this insightful and important comment. The reviewer raises a critical question: to what extent does the total nitrogen content, the specific surface area, and the nitrogen configuration each govern the observed electrochemical performance? We agree fully that this multi-parameter interplay must be addressed rigorously, and we have done so in the revised manuscript through the addition of Table 3 and a comprehensive quantitative analysis. While Table 2 reports the relative fractions of nitrogen configurations from N 1s peak deconvolution, Table 3 now presents the total nitrogen contents determined from XPS survey spectra alongside the calculated absolute atomic contents of each nitrogen configuration, derived by combining the total N content with the relative fractions from N 1s deconvolution.

The data in Table 3, combined with the electrochemical results, provide compelling evidence that neither total nitrogen content nor surface area alone is the determinant of electrochemical performance. The most striking demonstration is the case of NCF-600, which possesses by far the highest total nitrogen content (15.34 at.%) yet delivers the lowest specific capacitance in 6 M KOH among all NCF-T samples. This inverse relationship directly demonstrates that total nitrogen content is not the primary governing parameter. As discussed in response to C1, the high apparent nitrogen content in NCF-600 reflects predominantly residual g-C₃N₄-derived species rather than electrochemically active, lattice-incorporated nitrogen — a distinction that accounts fully for its poor device performance despite nominally high nitrogen loading.

The decisive role of nitrogen configuration and structural accessibility is further illustrated by comparing NCF-900 with NCF-600. Despite having a lower total nitrogen content (8.37 at.%) and even a lower absolute pyridinic-N content (3.03 at.%) than NCF-600 (6.54 at.%), NCF-900 delivers markedly superior electrochemical performance. The key distinction is that in NCF-900, the pyridinic-N species are thermally well-incorporated into the carbon lattice, positioned at accessible edge and defect sites, and capable of participating in reversible proton-coupled electron transfer — none of which applies to the incompletely carbonized NCF-600. The additional role of porous structure is revealed by comparing NCF-800 and NCF-900, which have almost identical total nitrogen contents (8.32 vs. 8.37 at.%), yet NCF-900 significantly outperforms NCF-800 in both specific capacitance (139.6 vs. ~100 F g⁻¹ at 0.5 A g⁻¹ in 6 M KOH) and surface area (646.2 vs. 470.2 m² g⁻¹). This comparison, where nitrogen chemistry is essentially equivalent, isolates pore development as an independent and consequential contributor to performance.

The reviewer is correct that surface area differences among samples are substantial, and their contribution to performance is real. NCF-900 achieves a BET surface area of 646.2 m² g⁻¹ with a type IV isotherm and mesopores centered at ~3.9 nm — substantially higher than NCF-600 (229.5 m² g⁻¹), NCF-700 (416.3 m² g⁻¹), NCF-800 (470.2 m² g⁻¹), and NCF-1000 (491.2 m² g⁻¹). The higher surface area of NCF-900 unquestionably facilitates electrolyte accessibility and ion transport. However, the transition to NCF-1000 demonstrates that surface area alone does not determine performance: despite NCF-1000 having a higher BET surface area than both NCF-600 and NCF-700, its capacitance is among the lowest in the series. This decline is accompanied by a dramatic reduction in the absolute content of electrochemically active pyridinic-N, from 3.03 at.% at 900 °C to just 0.41 at.% at 1000 °C. This decoupling of surface area and capacitance at NCF-1000 unambiguously demonstrates that the density of active nitrogen sites — not surface area alone — is the limiting factor in this high-temperature regime.

The superior electrochemical performance of NCF-900 therefore emerges from the simultaneous satisfaction of three conditions: (i) a sufficient density of lattice-incorporated, electrochemically active pyridinic-N sites (3.03 at.%); (ii) the highest BET surface area (646.2 m² g⁻¹) with well-developed mesoporosity (~3.9 nm); and (iii) the highest defect density as evidenced by the maximum I_D/I_G ratio (0.87), which introduces the localized electronic states favorable for proton-coupled electron transfer. No single parameter independently controls performance; rather, their concurrence at 900 °C creates the optimal electrode architecture. The manuscript has been comprehensively revised throughout the Abstract, Introduction, Sections 3.1, 3.2, 3.3, and the Conclusions to clearly articulate this synergistic interpretation and to discuss pyridinic-N as the major active nitrogen configuration within a multi-parameter performance framework.
