**Proposal for Study**

1\. Please describe in detail your motivations for seeking further learning at this institution. (1598/1600)

My motivation to join the Integrative Program at UST-KIMM traces back to a moment in my undergraduate lab, watching an X-ray diffraction pattern shift as I changed a Cu spacer's thickness by only a few angstroms, and realizing that a few atomic layers could reshape a material's macroscopic behavior. That moment turned my curiosity about solid-state physics into determination to become a researcher who designs materials from the atom up.

I graduated from the High-Quality Program in Physics Education at Hanoi National University of Education, where my thesis on magnetic anisotropy in Co/Pd multilayered spin valves gave me hands-on command of thin-film fabrication, interface engineering, and characterization using XRD, VSM, and four-point probe systems. That work led to a co-authored paper in Thin Solid Films, and revealed how much further atomic-scale interface control could be pushed toward real engineering applications.

UST is uniquely built for this ambition: it embeds students directly inside Korea's national research institutes, letting theory and practice grow together. KIMM's leadership in precision micro/nano-manufacturing makes it the ideal home, and Professor Seung-Mo Lee's laboratory is precisely where I want to grow. His pioneering research on atomic-layer-deposited coatings, functional thin films, and interfacial engineering for advanced energy and composite materials is the natural extension of my thesis work. I am seeking the rigorous training that will let me turn my early promise into lasting scientific contribution under Professor Lee's guidance at KIMM.

2\. Among the courses you have completed (or academic fields you have experienced) at the undergraduate /master's level, which was the one that interested you the most and what are your accomplishments in the field? (1475/1600)

Solid State Physics, Solid State Optics, and Semiconductor-Magnetic Materials captivated me more than any other subject, because they showed me that phenomena invisible to the eye, quantum confinement, spin dynamics, interlayer coupling, are what ultimately decide how a real device performs. That connection between the invisible and the practical is what pulled me toward nanoscale materials research and never let go.

My proudest accomplishment in this field was my undergraduate thesis, investigating how the thickness of a Cu spacer layer governs interlayer exchange coupling and magnetic anisotropy in Co/Pd multilayered spin valves. Systematically varying spacer thickness and correlating the results with structural and magnetic characterization let my team uncover clear trends in the spin-coupling mechanism, work meticulous enough to be co-authored into a paper published in Thin Solid Films. Producing that paper was the moment I first felt like a scientist rather than a student.

Throughout this project I built genuine hands-on fluency with core characterization tools, Bruker D8 Advance XRD, VersaLab VSM, and four-point probe systems, and with MATLAB and Origin for data analysis, skills I also applied while co-authoring a second paper for the TNU Journal of Science and Technology. These experiences trained me to ask precise experimental questions and trust data over assumption. I now carry that training directly into nanoscale device research at KIMM.

3\. Please describe your desired career path upon graduation in detail. (1439/1600)

My desired career path is to become a research scientist who turns atomic-scale control into real technological impact, someone the field trusts to take a functional thin film or an atomic-layer-deposited coating from a laboratory curiosity to a working device. The UST-KIMM Integrative Program is where I intend to build that identity.

In the near term, immediately after completing my degree, I want to deepen my expertise through intensive R&D immersion under Professor Seung-Mo Lee, mastering advanced thin-film growth and interface engineering techniques while learning to translate nanoscale phenomena into solutions for real semiconductor and energy applications. I plan to publish research at each stage of this process, treating every paper not as a milestone for its own sake but as proof that my understanding of solid-state physics is deepening into engineering-ready expertise.

Over the long term, I aspire to lead a senior R&D team at a globally recognized research institute or in the advanced materials industry, building on the foundation and network I will gain at KIMM. I want to lead projects that integrate atomic-scale coatings into energy-efficient nano-devices, and eventually serve as a bridge connecting Korean and international research communities, carrying forward the same rigor Professor Lee's laboratory will instill in me. This is not an aspiration I hold lightly; it is the reason I am applying to KIMM.
