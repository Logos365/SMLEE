## Abstract

An efficient solar vapor generation (SVG) material must absorb the whole solar spectrum and turn that light into heat quickly. These properties are usually obtained only through complex post-synthesis steps. Here we show that they are already built into the solid carbon formed as a by-product of plasma methane pyrolysis. This carbon has a turbostratic graphitic–defect hybrid structure. Electron microscopy, X-ray diffraction, Raman spectroscopy, and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy all show the same picture: small graphitic domains (interlayer spacing ≈ 0.34 nm, I~D~/I~G~ ≈ 0.48) sit inside a continuous defect-rich carbon matrix. The same data, analysed quantitatively, link this structure to its function. The graphitic domains convert absorbed photons into heat efficiently. The disorder creates band-tail (Urbach) states that extend absorption across the ultraviolet–visible–near-infrared range. The many internal interfaces lower the thermal conductivity (κ ≈ 0.44 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹), so heat stays at the surface. With no activation, doping, or nanostructuring, the carbon absorbs more than 95 % of light at every wavelength. A binder-coated melamine-foam evaporator then reaches 2.0 kg m⁻² h⁻¹ under one sun, and desalinates seawater outdoors to below World Health Organization limits. The result is a clear link from the hybrid structure to its properties and its performance, and a way to turn a hydrogen-economy by-product into a low-cost material for solar water purification.

**Keywords:** methane pyrolysis; turbostratic carbon; graphitic–defect hybrid; photothermal conversion; solar vapor generation; desalination

## 1. Introduction

Freshwater is becoming scarce, and solar-driven interfacial evaporation is a low-cost, off-grid way to produce it. The absorbed sunlight is kept in a thin layer at the air–water interface, instead of heating the whole liquid. This raises the solar-to-vapor efficiency well above that of normal bulk heating [1–3]. In these systems the evaporation rate depends almost entirely on the light absorber. Absorber design is therefore the main way to improve performance [2,4,5].

A good photothermal absorber needs three things at the same time. It must absorb across the ultraviolet–visible–near-infrared (UV–Vis–NIR) solar spectrum. It must convert the absorbed photons into heat efficiently. And it must conduct little heat into the bulk, so the heat stays at the evaporation surface [4–6]. Carbon materials are natural candidates. Carbon nanotubes, graphene, graphene oxide, carbon black, carbon foams, carbon cloth, and carbon sponges all absorb light broadly and are stable, cheap, and easy to shape [7–16].

Their photothermal behaviour, however, depends strongly on how ordered the carbon is [6,16]. Highly graphitic carbons have large sp² networks. They turn light into heat efficiently, but they have few electronic states inside the band gap and conduct heat well along the planes [17]. Fully amorphous carbons have a lot of disorder. This broadens their light absorption, but their broken sp² regions convert light to heat less well [18]. Neither extreme is ideal. The best case is a **graphitic–defect hybrid**, where ordered domains and a disordered matrix each do part of the job [19].

So far, such hybrids have almost always been made with extra processing steps: chemical activation, heteroatom doping, template synthesis, partial reduction, or hierarchical nanostructuring [20–23]. These steps add cost and are hard to scale up. A carbon that is **already** a graphitic–defect hybrid, with no extra steps, would be much more practical. Materials like this have hardly been studied.

Methane pyrolysis gives exactly this kind of carbon. It makes hydrogen without direct CO₂ emission ("turquoise" hydrogen). It also produces about three kilograms of solid carbon per kilogram of H₂, and its economics depend on using that carbon [24,26,46]. During pyrolysis, the carbon forms directly from gas-phase species. The gas then cools quickly, which stops graphitization before it can finish. The carbon is frozen into a **turbostratic** structure: small graphitic domains spread through a defect-rich matrix. This is an intrinsic graphitic–defect hybrid, made in one continuous step [27,47]. How this by-product structure controls photothermal behaviour for SVG has not been studied. We study it here, using carbon collected from a rotating gliding-arc (RGA) plasma methane-pyrolysis reactor. We link its structure to its optical absorption, photothermal conversion, and evaporation performance, and we take the key structural and optical numbers directly from spectroscopy. On this basis we relate the structure to its properties and its performance. We then build a melamine-foam evaporator that reaches 2.0 kg m⁻² h⁻¹ under one sun and desalinates seawater outdoors to World Health Organization (WHO) limits, with no post-synthesis treatment.

## 2. Results and Discussion

### 2.1. Intrinsic formation of a graphitic–defect hybrid turbostratic carbon

```

│      carbon photo   (b) graphitic–defect hybrid architecture  │
│  (c) dip-coating of TC onto melamine foam → TC/MF evaporator  │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

We first look at how the by-product carbon gets its hybrid structure. Methane-derived turbostratic carbon (hereafter **TC**) was collected downstream of a custom RGA plasma reactor at atmospheric pressure (discharge power 1.8 kW; CH₄:H₂ = 3:1; total flow 20 SLPM; Table S1) [25]. In the discharge, methane splits into hydrogen and solid carbon (CH₄ → C + 2 H₂). The carbon grows straight from gas-phase species, not from a solid precursor as in normal carbonization [26]. Once the swirling flow leaves the plasma zone, the energy input stops and the carbon cools fast. Graphitization is cut short before long-range order can form, so the disorder is kept [27]. This balance between graphitic ordering and defect retention is what creates the turbostratic structure, with graphitic domains held in a defect-rich matrix (Figure 1a,b). The structure forms in one continuous step, with no chemical activation, heteroatom doping, templating, or other post-synthesis.

To test the carbon's own photothermal behaviour, we made an evaporator in a single dip-coating step. A water-based ink of TC with poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) and chitosan binders was coated onto commercial melamine foam (MF) at 2.5 mg cm⁻² and dried, giving the TC/MF evaporator (Figure 1c). The open-cell MF pulls water up to the lit surface and limits heat loss to the bulk. The measured performance therefore reflects the carbon, not an engineered support [28].

### 2.2. Direct visualization of the graphitic–defect hybrid architecture

```
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  [ FIGURE 2 ]  Direct structural visualization                │
│  (a) SEM (low/high mag)  (b) HRTEM lattice fringes + FFT      │
│  (c) HAADF-STEM + EDS maps (C, O)  (d) XPS survey spectrum    │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

Electron microscopy shows the hybrid directly. SEM (Figure 2a) shows that TC is made of linked spherical nanoparticles. They form a three-dimensional porous network with a rough surface. This shape scatters light many times and leaves open channels for water and vapour [29]. HRTEM (Figure 2b) shows that the carbon is neither fully graphitic nor fully amorphous. Lattice fringes with a ≈ 0.34 nm spacing, matching the graphitic (002) plane, appear in small patches inside a disordered matrix [30]. The matching FFT shows diffuse rings, not sharp spots. This means short-range order with almost no long-range order, which is typical of turbostratic carbon [31].

The composition, by contrast, is uniform. HAADF-STEM with EDS mapping (Figure 2c) shows carbon spread evenly through the particles. Oxygen is present only in small, even amounts at the surface, with no impurity-rich or separated regions. The XPS survey (Figure 2d) gives 97 at % C and 3 at % O. The C 1s peak is mostly sp² C=C/C–C at 284.8 eV, with only weak oxygen groups. The O 1s signal (Figure S1) and the weak C=O/C–O bands in the FTIR spectrum (Figure S2) point to mild surface oxidation in air, not a changed framework. So the differences within TC come from **how ordered the carbon is**, not from its composition. TC is an intrinsic graphitic–defect hybrid.

Having seen the hybrid, we now put numbers on it. XRD (Figure 3a) shows a broad (002) peak at 25.8°, giving an interlayer spacing of d~002~ ≈ 0.34 nm. It also shows a broad (100)/(101) feature near 43–45°, typical of turbostratic carbon [27]. From the width of the (002) peak, a Scherrer analysis gives a stack thickness of L~c~ ≈ `[FLAG]` nm. This is only about N ≈ L~c~/d~002~ ≈ `[FLAG]` stacked layers, so the stacking really is short-range. The Raman spectrum (Figure 3b) shows the defect D band (≈ 1350 cm⁻¹) and the graphitic G band (≈ 1580 cm⁻¹), with I~D~/I~G~ = 0.48. The Tuinstra–Koenig / Ferrari–Robertson relations then give an in-plane domain size of L~a~ ≈ `[FLAG]` nm [32,33]. TC therefore sits between graphite and amorphous carbon: nanometre-sized ordered domains in a defect-rich matrix.

Two more measurements matter for the photothermal response. Nitrogen sorption (Figure 3c) gives a type-IV isotherm with a modest surface area of 40.4 m² g⁻¹ and ≈ 5 nm pores. The performance below is therefore not due to a large surface area. The thermal conductivity (Figure 3d) is only κ = 0.44 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹, far below that of graphitic carbons. The disorder and the many domain–matrix interfaces scatter phonons and slow heat flow [34]. All these numbers—d~002~, L~c~, N, L~a~, I~D~/I~G~, κ, and the optical values in Section 2.5 (Figure 3e)—give a quantitative link between structure and properties. The graphitic domains give fast photon-to-phonon conversion. The disordered matrix adds localized states that widen absorption [48]. The interfaces keep κ low, so heat stays at the surface (Figure 3f).

We now move from the carbon to the device. XRD of the coated foam (Figure 4a) still shows the TC (002)/(100) peaks and the broad MF halo, with no new phases. Dip-coating therefore keeps the structure of both parts. SEM with EDS mapping (Figure 4b) shows an even carbon layer on the foam struts that does not block the open cells. Carbon is spread evenly, and nitrogen comes from the melamine skeleton. Wettability tests (Figure 4c) show that TC powder is hydrophobic and floats. TC/MF, though fully covered by carbon, stays hydrophilic, and droplets soak in within about 1 s. The thin coating does not block the water supply. Raman spectra of water on TC (Figure S4) show only a small rise in free water and a matching drop in intermediate water. The rest of the O–H band is unchanged. So TC only disturbs the local hydrogen bonding at the interface and slightly lowers the effective evaporation enthalpy, without changing bulk water [50].

### 2.5. Broadband solar absorption, band-tail states, and photothermal vapor generation

UV–Vis–NIR spectra (Figure 4d) show that MF is almost transparent. TC absorbs more than 95 % of the light at nearly every wavelength from 250 to 2500 nm. TC/MF keeps this response, so the coating turns a transparent foam into a near-black-body absorber.

The same data show why (Figure 4d, inset). A Tauc plot, (αhν)^1/2^ versus hν, extrapolates to an optical gap of E~g~ ≈ `[FLAG]` eV. The exponential edge, ln α versus hν, gives an Urbach energy of E~u~ ≈ `[FLAG]` meV. A large Urbach energy is a direct sign of band-tail states created by disorder. These states reach into the band gap and allow low-energy transitions, which is why the absorption is nearly flat with wavelength [48,51]. The small graphitic domains (L~a~, Section 2.3) add strong interband absorption and fast carrier–phonon coupling. Together, the two effects explain the full-spectrum absorption of the hybrid, reached with no doping or nanostructuring.

The absorbed light is turned into heat efficiently. Under one sun in the dry state (Figure 4e), the TC/MF surface rises from 25 °C to about 75 °C in one minute and then levels off. MF only reaches about 26 °C. In the wet state, TC/MF settles at about 35 °C. This is lower than in the dry state because some energy drives evaporation, but it is still well above MF (about 25 °C). The infrared images show that the heat stays at the lit surface. The cumulative water loss (Figure 4f; summarized in Figure 5a) reaches 2.00 kg m⁻² for TC/MF in one hour, against 0.87 for MF and 0.11 for bulk water. The curve is nearly straight and stable. After the dark evaporation is subtracted, the net solar rate is about 1.9 kg m⁻² h⁻¹ `[NET RATE — confirm gross vs net]`. This gives a solar-to-vapor efficiency of about `[η = TBD]` % from Equation (2). Table 1 shows that this matches or beats typical carbon-based evaporators (about 1.4–1.9 kg m⁻² h⁻¹), even though TC/MF is made from a pyrolysis by-product with no activation or doping. The performance comes from the three structural features working **together**: ordered domains for conversion, band-tail states for absorption, and interfaces for heat localization. ### 2.6. Outdoor solar desalination and salt rejection

Practical desalination needs the surface to stay free of salt. We seeded NaCl crystals on the evaporator. Under continuous sunlight they dissolved completely within 135 min (Figure 5b). Salt diffuses inward, carried by the capillary water from the hydrophilic MF, faster than evaporation carries it outward. The crystals therefore redissolve and the channels stay open [28]. Outdoors from 10:30 to 16:30 (Figure 5c), with sunlight peaking near 1.0 kW m⁻² at noon, the collected water mass rose steadily despite the changing light. This confirms stable operation in the field. In the collected water (Figure 5d), the salinity dropped below the WHO drinking-water limit. Na⁺, Mg²⁺, K⁺, and Ca²⁺ each fell by two to three orders of magnitude [49]. The by-product carbon therefore supports not just lab evaporation but real drinking-water production.

## 3. Conclusions

The solid carbon left over from plasma methane pyrolysis works, with no further treatment, as an efficient photothermal material for solar vapor generation. Fast cooling after pyrolysis freezes it into a turbostratic graphitic–defect hybrid: small graphitic domains in a continuous defect-rich matrix. Electron microscopy, XRD, Raman, and XPS all confirm this structure. Re-analysing the same data ties the structure to the properties. XRD gives the stack thickness and layer number. Raman gives the in-plane domain size. UV–Vis–NIR gives the optical gap and the Urbach energy. The graphitic domains convert photons to heat efficiently. The disorder-derived band-tail states give more than 95 % absorption at every wavelength. The many interfaces give a low thermal conductivity (≈ 0.44 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹) that keeps heat at the surface. Dip-coated onto hydrophilic melamine foam in one step, the carbon reaches 2.0 kg m⁻² h⁻¹ under one sun, rejects salt well, and produces WHO-compliant water outdoors. The key message is a clear link from the hybrid structure to its properties and its performance: the result comes from graphitic order and disorder working **together**, not from either one alone. The work also gives a simple, scalable, binder-only way to add value to the large carbon by-product of turquoise-hydrogen production, as a material for solar water purification. Future work will test long-term durability and large-area scale-up, refine the mechanism with more spectroscopy (for example electron paramagnetic resonance and temperature-dependent transport), and apply the approach to other solar energy uses.

## 6. Supporting Information — figure and table captions

*Also not counted in the body word limit.*

**Figure S1. High-resolution XPS spectra of the methane-derived carbon.**
**(a)** C 1s spectrum, fitted to a dominant sp² C=C/C–C component at 284.8 eV, minor C–O (~286 eV) and C=O / O–C=O (~288–289 eV) components, and a weak π–π* shake-up satellite; the oxygen-bonded components together account for only a small fraction of the total C 1s area. **(b)** O 1s spectrum, fitted to C=O and C–O environments. The spectra show that oxygen is present only as a small quantity of surface functional groups formed on exposure to air — consistent with the 3 at % oxygen in the survey scan (Figure 2d) — while the sp² carbon framework remains essentially intact.

**Figure S2. FTIR spectrum of the methane-derived carbon.**
The spectrum is dominated by the broad, featureless absorption of conjugated sp² carbon, with only weak bands assignable to C=O stretching (~1700 cm⁻¹), C–O stretching (~1200 cm⁻¹) and C–H (~2900 cm⁻¹). The near-absence of strong oxygen-related bands confirms that surface oxidation is mild and does not disrupt the graphitic framework, in agreement with the XPS data.

**Figure S3. Full-range X-ray diffraction pattern of the methane-derived carbon.**
Wide-angle scan showing the broad (002) reflection at 25.8° (d~002~ ≈ 0.34 nm) and the broad, low-intensity (100)/(101) band near 43–45°, with no sharp graphite reflections. The large peak widths indicate spatially confined graphitic domains surrounded by disordered carbon, i.e. turbostratic stacking; this is the source data for the Scherrer analysis quoted in Figure 3a.

**Figure S4. Raman analysis of interfacial water in contact with the carbon.**
Deconvoluted O–H stretching Raman envelope (2800–3800 cm⁻¹) of bulk water and of water in contact with the methane-derived carbon (Water/TC), separated into free-water (FW), intermediate hydrogen-bonded water (IW) and strongly hydrogen-bonded components. Contact with the carbon slightly increases the FW fraction and decreases the IW fraction, while the overall envelope is almost unchanged. This shows that the carbon perturbs only the local hydrogen-bond network at the solid–liquid interface; this weak "activation" of interfacial water lowers the effective enthalpy of vaporization and helps sustain the high evaporation rate, without restructuring bulk water.

**Figure S5. Textural properties of the methane-derived carbon.**
**(a)** N₂ adsorption–desorption isotherm at 77 K (source data for Figure 3c), of type IV with an H3-type hysteresis loop, giving a BET specific surface area of 40.4 m² g⁻¹. **(b)** BJH pore-size distribution from the desorption branch, showing a broad mesopore population centred near 5 nm with a tail to larger inter-particle voids. Together these confirm that the carbon is a moderately mesoporous aggregate rather than a high-surface-area sorbent, so its photothermal performance cannot be attributed to surface area.

**Table S1. Operating and electrical parameters of the rotating gliding-arc plasma reactor during carbon synthesis.**
RMS voltage 308 V; RMS current 9.9 A; mean discharge power 1.8 kW; run time 60 min; total gas flow 20 SLPM (CH₄:H₂ = 3:1 by volume); reactor pressure 1 atm. These conditions set the high-temperature decomposition and the rapid post-discharge quenching that together produce the turbostratic graphitic–defect hybrid structure characterized in Figures 2 and 3.
