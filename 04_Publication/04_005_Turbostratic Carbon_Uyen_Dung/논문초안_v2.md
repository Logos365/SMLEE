## Abstract

Broadband solar absorption combined with rapid light-to-heat conversion is the central material requirement for efficient solar vapor generation (SVG), yet it is usually met only through elaborate post-synthetic engineering. Here we show that the solid carbon produced as a by-product of plasma-assisted methane pyrolysis intrinsically satisfies this requirement through a turbostratic graphitic–defect hybrid architecture. Electron microscopy, X-ray diffraction, Raman spectroscopy, and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy consistently reveal short-range graphitic microdomains (interlayer spacing ≈ 0.34 nm, I~D~/I~G~ ≈ 0.48) embedded in a continuous defect-rich carbon matrix. Quantitative analysis of the same data links the structure to the function: nanometre-scale graphitic microdomains drive efficient photon-to-phonon conversion through non-radiative relaxation, the disorder-derived band-tail (Urbach) states extend absorption across the ultraviolet–visible–near-infrared range, and the numerous internal interfaces suppress heat conduction (κ ≈ 0.44 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹), favouring interfacial heat localization. Without any activation, doping, or nanostructuring, the carbon shows > 95 % wavelength-independent absorption, and a binder-coated melamine-foam evaporator reaches an interfacial evaporation rate of 2.0 kg m⁻² h⁻¹ under one-sun illumination with stable outdoor desalination to below World Health Organization limits. This work establishes a direct structure–property–function relationship that converts a hydrogen-economy by-product into a scalable solar-driven water-purification material.

**Keywords:** methane pyrolysis; turbostratic carbon; graphitic–defect hybrid; photothermal conversion; solar vapor generation; desalination

## 1. Introduction

Freshwater scarcity is intensifying, and solar-driven interfacial evaporation has emerged as a low-cost, off-grid response to it. By confining the absorbed solar energy to a thin layer at the air–water interface instead of heating the bulk liquid, it pushes the solar-to-vapor conversion efficiency far above that of conventional volumetric heating [1–3]. In such systems the evaporation rate is set almost entirely by the intrinsic photothermal properties of the light absorber, which makes absorber design the primary lever for performance [2,4,5].

An ideal photothermal absorber must meet three requirements at once: broadband absorption across the ultraviolet–visible–near-infrared (UV–Vis–NIR) solar spectrum, efficient non-radiative conversion of the absorbed photons into heat, and low heat conduction into the bulk so that the heat stays localized at the evaporation front [4–6]. Carbon materials—carbon nanotubes, graphene, graphene oxide, carbon black, carbon foams, carbon cloth, and carbon sponges—are natural candidates, combining intrinsically broadband absorption with chemical and thermal stability, low cost, and structural tunability [7–16].

Their photothermal response, however, is dictated by the degree of structural ordering [6,16]. Highly graphitized carbons have extended sp²-conjugated networks that favour fast non-radiative relaxation and efficient heat generation, but they possess few in-gap electronic states and conduct heat readily in-plane [17]. Fully amorphous carbons carry abundant disorder that broadens their optical transitions, yet their fragmented sp² domains convert light to heat less efficiently [18]. Neither limit is ideal: the optimum is a **graphitic–defect hybrid** in which ordered microdomains and a disordered matrix contribute complementary functions [19].

So far, such hybrids have been produced almost exclusively by post-synthetic engineering—chemical activation, heteroatom doping, template-assisted synthesis, partial reduction, or hierarchical nanostructuring—to build the desired balance of order and disorder [20–23]. These routes add cost and processing complexity and are difficult to scale. A carbon that is **intrinsically** a graphitic–defect hybrid, needing none of these steps, would be far more practical, yet such materials remain largely unexplored.

Methane pyrolysis provides exactly this material. In producing hydrogen without direct CO₂ emission ("turquoise" hydrogen), it co-generates roughly three kilograms of solid carbon per kilogram of H₂, and its economic case depends on turning that carbon into a useful product [24,26,46]. During high-temperature decomposition the carbon nucleates and grows directly from gaseous species; the subsequent rapid quenching kinetically arrests graphitization, so the carbon freezes into a **turbostratic** framework of short-range graphitic microdomains dispersed in a defect-rich matrix—an intrinsic graphitic–defect hybrid formed in a single continuous step [27,47]. How this by-product microstructure governs photothermal behaviour for SVG has not been examined. Here we address that question using carbon collected from a rotating gliding-arc (RGA) plasma methane-pyrolysis reactor. By correlating its microstructure with optical absorption, photothermal conversion, and evaporation performance—and by quantifying the key structural and optical descriptors directly from spectroscopy—we define a structure–property–function relationship, and we demonstrate a melamine-foam evaporator that delivers 2.0 kg m⁻² h⁻¹ under one sun and World Health Organization (WHO)-compliant desalination outdoors, with no post-synthetic modification.

## 2. Results and Discussion

### 2.1. Intrinsic formation of a graphitic–defect hybrid turbostratic carbon

```
│  (c) dip-coating of TC onto melamine foam → TC/MF evaporator  │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

We first consider how the by-product carbon acquires its hybrid structure. Methane-derived turbostratic carbon (hereafter **TC**) was collected downstream of a custom RGA plasma reactor operated at atmospheric pressure (discharge power 1.8 kW; CH₄:H₂ = 3:1; total flow 20 SLPM; Table S1) [25]. Inside the discharge, methane dissociates into hydrogen and solid carbon (CH₄ → C + 2 H₂); unlike the carbonization of a solid precursor, the carbon nucleates and grows directly from gaseous species [26]. Once the swirling flow carries these species beyond the confined plasma zone, the energy input stops abruptly and the carbon cools rapidly, so graphitization is kinetically suppressed before long-range order can develop and the structural disorder is retained [27]. This competition between graphitic crystallization and defect retention during quenching is what intrinsically generates the turbostratic framework, with graphitic microdomains embedded in a defect-rich carbon matrix (Figure 1a,b). Crucially, the architecture forms in one continuous step, with no chemical activation, heteroatom doping, templating, or other post-synthetic treatment.

To probe the intrinsic photothermal behaviour of this carbon, we integrated it into an evaporator by a single dip-coating step: a hydrophilic ink of TC with poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) and chitosan binders was coated onto commercial melamine foam (MF) at 2.5 mg cm⁻² and dried, yielding the TC/MF evaporator (Figure 1c). The open-cell MF continuously wicks water to the illuminated surface while limiting heat loss to the bulk, so that the measured performance reflects the carbon itself rather than an engineered support [28].

Electron microscopy resolves the hybrid directly. SEM (Figure 2a) shows that TC is built from interconnected spherical nanoparticles that form a three-dimensional porous network with hierarchical surface roughness—a morphology that promotes multiple light scattering while leaving continuous channels for water transport and vapour escape [29]. HRTEM (Figure 2b) shows that the carbon is neither fully graphitized nor fully amorphous: lattice fringes with an interlayer spacing of ≈ 0.34 nm, matching the graphitic (002) plane, appear locally within a surrounding disordered matrix [30], and the corresponding fast Fourier transform shows diffuse rings rather than discrete spots—short-range graphitic ordering with little long-range crystallinity, the hallmark of turbostratic carbon [31].

The composition, in contrast, is uniform. HAADF-STEM with energy-dispersive X-ray mapping (Figure 2c) shows carbon distributed homogeneously through the particles, with only sparse, evenly dispersed surface oxygen and no impurity-rich or phase-segregated regions. The XPS survey (Figure 2d) gives 97 at % C and 3 at % O, with the C 1s peak dominated by sp² C=C/C–C bonding at 284.8 eV and only weak oxygen-containing groups; the O 1s signal (Figure S1) and the weak C=O/C–O bands in the FTIR spectrum (Figure S2) point to mild surface oxidation from air exposure rather than a modified framework. The structural heterogeneity of TC therefore arises from variations in **carbon ordering**, not in elemental composition: TC is an intrinsic graphitic–defect hybrid.

Having established the hybrid qualitatively, we now quantify it. XRD (Figure 3a) shows a broad (002) reflection at 25.8°, giving an interlayer spacing of d~002~ ≈ 0.34 nm, and a broad (100)/(101) feature near 43–45° typical of a turbostratic framework [27]. A Scherrer analysis of the (002) width gives a stack thickness of L~c~ ≈ `[FLAG]` nm, i.e. only N ≈ L~c~/d~002~ ≈ `[FLAG]` stacked layers—genuinely short-range stacking. The Raman spectrum (Figure 3b) shows the defect-activated D band (≈ 1350 cm⁻¹) and the graphitic G band (≈ 1580 cm⁻¹) with I~D~/I~G~ = 0.48; the Tuinstra–Koenig / Ferrari–Robertson relations then give an in-plane graphitic domain size of L~a~ ≈ `[FLAG]` nm [32,33]. TC thus sits in an intermediate turbostratic regime, with nanometre-scale ordered domains dispersed in a defect-rich matrix.

Two further measurements bear on the photothermal behaviour. Nitrogen sorption (Figure 3c) gives a type-IV isotherm with a modest specific surface area of 40.4 m² g⁻¹ and ≈ 5 nm mesopores, so the performance below cannot be ascribed to a large surface area. The transient thermal conductivity (Figure 3d) is only κ = 0.44 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹, well below that of crystalline graphitic carbons, because the disorder and the many interfaces between graphitic microdomains and the surrounding matrix scatter phonons and impede heat flow [34]. Taken together, these descriptors—d~002~, L~c~, N, L~a~, I~D~/I~G~, κ, and the optical parameters of Section 2.5 (Figure 3e)—form a quantitative structure–property basis: the graphitic microdomains supply fast photon-to-phonon conversion, the disordered matrix introduces localized states that broaden absorption [48], and the internal interfaces keep κ low so that heat stays at the interface (Figure 3f).

We next turn from the carbon to the device. XRD of the coated foam (Figure 4a) retains both the TC (002)/(100) reflections and the broad amorphous halo of MF, with no new crystalline phases, so dip-coating preserves the structure of both components. SEM with EDS mapping (Figure 4b) shows a conformal carbon layer on the MF struts that leaves the open cells unblocked, with carbon uniformly distributed and nitrogen originating from the melamine skeleton. Wettability tests (Figure 4c) show that pristine TC powder is hydrophobic and floats, whereas TC/MF—although fully covered by carbon—keeps the hydrophilicity of MF, with droplets infiltrating within ≈ 1 s; the thin coating does not obstruct capillary water supply. Raman spectra of water in contact with TC (Figure S4) show only a small rise in the free-water component and a matching drop in intermediate-water, with the O–H stretching envelope otherwise unchanged, indicating that TC perturbs only the local hydrogen-bonding network at the solid–liquid interface and slightly lowers the effective vaporization enthalpy without restructuring bulk water [50].

### 2.5. Broadband solar absorption, band-tail states, and photothermal vapor generation

UV–Vis–NIR spectroscopy (Figure 4d) shows MF to be essentially transparent, whereas TC absorbs > 95 % of incident light almost independently of wavelength from 250 to 2500 nm; TC/MF retains this response in full, so the conformal coating turns a transparent foam into a near-black-body absorber.

The origin of this response is visible in the same data (Figure 4d, inset). A Tauc construction, (αhν)^1/2^ versus hν, extrapolates to an optical gap of E~g~ ≈ `[FLAG]` eV, while the exponential absorption edge, ln α versus hν, gives an Urbach energy of E~u~ ≈ `[FLAG]` meV. A large Urbach energy is a direct signature of disorder-derived band-tail states reaching into the gap, which open low-energy optical transitions and produce the near-wavelength-independent absorption seen here [48,51]. Together with the nanometre-scale graphitic domains (L~a~, Section 2.3), which sustain strong interband absorption and fast carrier–phonon coupling, these band-tail states account for the efficient full-spectrum absorption of the intrinsic hybrid—achieved with no doping or nanostructuring.

The absorbed light is converted to heat efficiently. Under one sun in the dry state (Figure 4e), the TC/MF surface rises from 25 °C to ≈ 75 °C within one minute and then plateaus, while MF reaches only ≈ 26 °C. In the wet state, TC/MF stabilizes at ≈ 35 °C—lower than dry, because part of the absorbed energy drives evaporation—but still well above MF (≈ 25 °C), and the infrared images confirm that the heat stays confined to the illuminated surface. The cumulative water loss (Figure 4f; summarized in Figure 5a) reaches 2.00 kg m⁻² for TC/MF in one hour, against 0.87 for MF and 0.11 for bulk water, with a near-linear, stable profile. As Table 1 shows, this matches or exceeds representative carbon-based evaporators (typically 1.4–1.9 kg m⁻² h⁻¹), even though TC/MF is made directly from a pyrolysis by-product with no activation or doping. ### 2.6. Outdoor solar desalination and salt rejection

Practical desalination requires that salt not build up at the surface. Seeding NaCl crystals onto the evaporator, we find that they dissolve completely within 135 min under continuous irradiation (Figure 5b): inward diffusion of salt, driven by the capillary water supplied through the hydrophilic MF, outpaces the outward advective salt flux from evaporation, so the crystals redissolve and the evaporation channels stay open [28]. Outdoors under natural sunlight from 10:30 to 16:30 (Figure 5c), with irradiance peaking near 1.0 kW m⁻² at noon, the cumulative freshwater mass rose continuously despite the fluctuating illumination, confirming stable field operation. In the collected condensate (Figure 5d), the salinity fell below the WHO drinking-water guideline, with Na⁺, Mg²⁺, K⁺, and Ca²⁺ each reduced by two to three orders of magnitude [49]. The by-product carbon therefore supports not only laboratory evaporation but practical potable-water production under real conditions.

## 3. Conclusions

We have shown that the solid carbon left over from plasma methane pyrolysis is, without any further treatment, an intrinsically efficient photothermal material for solar vapor generation. Rapid quenching after high-temperature decomposition freezes the carbon into a turbostratic graphitic–defect hybrid of short-range graphitic microdomains in a continuous defect-rich matrix. Electron microscopy, XRD, Raman, and XPS confirm this architecture, and re-analysing the same data—stack thickness and layer number from XRD, in-plane domain size from Raman, optical gap and Urbach energy from UV–Vis–NIR—ties the structure directly to the measured properties: the graphitic microdomains give efficient photon-to-phonon conversion, the disorder-derived band-tail states give > 95 % wavelength-independent absorption, and the many internal interfaces give a low thermal conductivity (≈ 0.44 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹) that keeps heat at the evaporation front. Dip-coated onto hydrophilic melamine foam in a single step, the carbon delivers an interfacial evaporation rate of 2.0 kg m⁻² h⁻¹ under one sun, strong salt rejection, and WHO-compliant freshwater production outdoors. The central point is a clear structure–property–function relationship: it is the **synergy** of graphitic ordering and structural disorder—not either one alone—that produces the performance. Beyond this mechanistic insight for designing carbon photothermal materials, the work offers a scalable, binder-only way to valorize the large carbon co-product of turquoise-hydrogen production as a high-value material for solar-driven water purification. Future work will address long-term durability and large-area scale-up, sharpen the mechanism with further spectroscopy (e.g. electron paramagnetic resonance, temperature-dependent transport), and extend the approach to other solar energy-conversion applications.
