## Abstract

An efficient solar vapor generation (SVG) material must absorb the whole solar spectrum, convert that light into heat quickly, and lose little heat to the water below. These properties are usually engineered by chemical activation, doping or nanostructuring. Here we show that they are already built into the solid carbon formed as a by-product of plasma methane pyrolysis. Rapid cooling after the plasma discharge freezes the carbon into a turbostratic "graphitic–defect hybrid": nanometre-scale graphitic microdomains dispersed in a continuous defect-rich matrix. SEM, HRTEM with FFT, HAADF-STEM/EDS, XPS, XRD, Raman and FTIR all point to the same structure (interlayer spacing ≈ 0.34 nm; I~D~/I~G~ = 0.48; 97 at % carbon with only mild surface oxidation). The graphitic microdomains convert absorbed photons into heat by fast non-radiative relaxation, while the defect-rich matrix traps photo-excited carriers and broadens the absorption; the many internal interfaces give a low thermal conductivity of 0.44 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹. Without any post-synthetic step, the carbon absorbs close to all incident light from the ultraviolet to the near-infrared. Dip-coated onto melamine foam with a poly(vinyl alcohol)/chitosan binder, it reaches an evaporation rate of 2.0 kg m⁻² h⁻¹ under one sun, rejects salt during continuous operation, and desalinates seawater outdoors to below World Health Organization limits. The work links the intrinsic hybrid structure to its photothermal properties and performance, and turns a hydrogen-economy by-product into a low-cost material for solar water purification.

**Keywords:** methane pyrolysis; turbostratic carbon; graphitic–defect hybrid; photothermal conversion; solar vapor generation; desalination

## 1. Introduction

Freshwater is becoming scarce, and solar-driven interfacial evaporation is a low-cost, off-grid way to produce it. The absorbed sunlight is kept in a thin layer at the air–water interface, instead of heating the whole liquid. This raises the solar-to-vapor efficiency well above that of normal bulk heating [1–3]. In these systems the evaporation rate depends almost entirely on the light absorber. Absorber design is therefore the main way to improve performance [2,4,5].

A good photothermal absorber needs three things at the same time. It must absorb across the ultraviolet–visible–near-infrared (UV–Vis–NIR) solar spectrum. It must convert the absorbed photons into heat efficiently. And it must conduct little heat into the bulk, so the heat stays at the evaporation surface [4–6]. Carbon materials are natural candidates. Carbon nanotubes, graphene, graphene oxide, carbon black, carbon foams, carbon cloth, and carbon sponges all absorb light broadly and are stable, cheap, and easy to shape [7–16].

Their photothermal behaviour, however, depends strongly on how ordered the carbon is [6,16]. Highly graphitic carbons have large sp² networks. They turn light into heat efficiently, but they have few electronic states inside the band gap and conduct heat well along the planes [17]. Fully amorphous carbons have a lot of disorder. This broadens their light absorption, but their broken sp² regions convert light to heat less well [18]. Neither extreme is ideal. The best case is a **graphitic–defect hybrid**, where ordered domains and a disordered matrix each do part of the job [19].

So far, such hybrids have almost always been made with extra processing steps: chemical activation, heteroatom doping, template synthesis, partial reduction, or hierarchical nanostructuring [20–23]. These steps add cost and are hard to scale up. A carbon that is **already** a graphitic–defect hybrid, with no extra steps, would be much more practical. Materials like this have hardly been studied.

Methane pyrolysis gives exactly this kind of carbon. It makes hydrogen without direct CO₂ emission ("turquoise" hydrogen). It also produces about three kilograms of solid carbon per kilogram of H₂, and its economics depend on using that carbon [24,26,46]. During pyrolysis, the carbon forms directly from gas-phase species. The gas then cools quickly, which stops graphitization before it can finish. The carbon is frozen into a **turbostratic** structure: small graphitic domains spread through a defect-rich matrix. This is an intrinsic graphitic–defect hybrid, made in one continuous step [27,47]. How this by-product structure controls photothermal behaviour for SVG has not been studied. We study it here, using carbon collected from a rotating gliding-arc (RGA) plasma methane-pyrolysis reactor. We link its structure to its optical absorption, photothermal conversion and evaporation performance, and demonstrate a melamine-foam evaporator that reaches 2.0 kg m⁻² h⁻¹ under one sun and desalinates seawater outdoors to World Health Organization (WHO) limits, with no post-synthesis treatment.

**Figure 1. Synthesis of the methane-derived carbon and its turbostratic graphitic–defect hybrid nanostructure.**
**(a)** Schematic of the synthesis. A CH₄/H₂ feed is decomposed in a rotating gliding-arc (RGA) plasma at atmospheric pressure; methane splits into hydrogen gas and solid carbon (CH₄(g) → C(s) + H₂(g)), so the carbon forms directly from gas-phase species rather than from a solid precursor. Downstream of the discharge the reactive species cool rapidly ("rapid quenching"), which stops graphitization before long-range order can develop and locks in structural disorder. The product is a low-density black powder (photograph, right). **(b)** SEM images of the powder at two magnifications. The carbon is built from spherical primary particles that are fused into branched, chain-like aggregates, forming an open three-dimensional network with hierarchical roughness. This morphology scatters incident light many times and leaves continuous open channels that later carry water and vapour in the evaporator. **(c)** HRTEM images with the corresponding fast Fourier transform (FFT, inset). Short, curved lattice fringes with an interlayer spacing of ≈ 0.34 nm (graphitic (002)) appear as isolated few-layer stacks — the graphitic microdomains (yellow boxes) — embedded in a fringe-free disordered carbon matrix (red ellipse). The FFT shows broad diffuse rings rather than sharp spots: short-range order without long-range crystallinity, the hallmark of a turbostratic structure. **(d)** HAADF-STEM image with EDS elemental maps of carbon and oxygen. Carbon is distributed uniformly through the aggregates, and oxygen appears only as a faint, even background with no oxygen-rich clusters or separate phases. The carbon is therefore heterogeneous in how ordered it is, but homogeneous in composition — an intrinsic graphitic–defect hybrid formed in a single step, with no activation, doping or templating.

Methane-derived turbostratic carbon (hereafter **TC**) was collected downstream of a custom RGA plasma reactor at atmospheric pressure (discharge power 1.8 kW; CH₄:H₂ = 3:1; total flow 20 SLPM; Table S1) [25]. In the discharge, methane splits into hydrogen and solid carbon (CH₄ → C + 2 H₂), so the carbon grows straight from gas-phase species [26]. Once the flow leaves the plasma zone the energy input stops and the carbon cools fast, which cuts graphitization short before long-range order can form and keeps the disorder [27]. This balance between graphitic ordering and defect retention is what creates the turbostratic structure, with small graphitic domains held in a defect-rich matrix (Figure 1a).

The SEM images (Figure 1b) show that the powder is made of spherical primary particles fused into open, branched aggregates with a rough surface. HRTEM (Figure 1c) shows the carbon is neither fully graphitic nor fully amorphous: ≈ 0.34 nm (002) lattice fringes appear as small few-layer stacks inside a disordered matrix, and the FFT shows diffuse rings rather than sharp spots — short-range order only. HAADF-STEM with EDS mapping (Figure 1d) shows carbon spread evenly and oxygen present only as a faint, uniform trace, with no separate phases. The material is therefore heterogeneous in carbon ordering but homogeneous in composition: an intrinsic graphitic–defect hybrid.

**Figure 2. Spectroscopic and thermal characterization confirming the turbostratic graphitic–defect hybrid structure and its photothermal roles.**
**(a)** XPS survey spectrum. The surface is 97.0 at % carbon and only 3.0 at % oxygen; the high-resolution C 1s and O 1s spectra (Figure S1) show a dominant sp² C=C/C–C component with only a small amount of oxygen from mild surface oxidation in air. **(b)** XRD, (002) region. A broad reflection at 2θ = 25.80° gives an interlayer spacing d~002~ ≈ 0.34 nm, slightly larger than graphite (0.335 nm); its large width indicates only a few stacked layers (Scherrer analysis: stack height L~c~ ≈ `[FLAG]` nm, N ≈ `[FLAG]` layers). **(c)** XRD, (100)/(101) region. A broad, asymmetric band is deconvoluted into components near 43.00° and 44.63°; because these two reflections are not resolved (a single two-dimensional band), neighbouring graphitic layers must be rotationally disordered — turbostratic stacking. The full pattern is given in Figure S2. **(d)** Raman spectrum. The defect-activated D band (~1350 cm⁻¹) and the graphitic G band (~1580 cm⁻¹) give I~D~/I~G~ = 0.48, an intermediate value showing a well-developed sp² network that still carries abundant edge and in-plane defects (in-plane domain size L~a~ ≈ `[FLAG]` nm). **(e)** FTIR spectrum. Only weak C=O and C–O bands and a weak C–H band appear on top of the broad absorption of conjugated sp² carbon, confirming that surface oxidation is slight and does not disrupt the framework. **(f)** Transient temperature rise from a hot-disk measurement, giving a low thermal conductivity κ = 0.44 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹; phonons are scattered by the disorder and by the many graphitic-domain/matrix interfaces, which helps keep solar heat at the evaporation surface. **(g)** Schematic of the proposed microstructure and its photothermal roles: within the graphitic microdomains, absorbed photons are converted to heat by fast photon-to-phonon conversion and non-radiative relaxation; the surrounding defect-rich matrix provides defect-mediated carrier trapping and exciton migration that broaden light absorption. Together the two motifs give broadband light harvesting and efficient heat generation. The modest BET surface area (40.4 m² g⁻¹; Figure S3) shows that the performance is not a surface-area effect.

The XPS survey (Figure 2a) gives 97.0 at % C and 3.0 at % O, and the C 1s/O 1s fits (Figure S1) show that this oxygen is only a thin layer of surface groups. XRD confirms the turbostratic stacking: the (002) peak at 25.80° (Figure 2b) is broad, giving d~002~ ≈ 0.34 nm with only a few layers per stack, and the (100)/(101) band at 43–45° (Figure 2c) is a single unresolved feature rather than two sharp lines, which means the layers are rotationally disordered. The full XRD pattern is in Figure S2. The Raman spectrum (Figure 2d) shows D and G bands with I~D~/I~G~ = 0.48 — a well-formed sp² network that still carries many defects. FTIR (Figure 2e) shows only weak oxygen-related and C–H bands, in line with the XPS result.

Two properties matter for the photothermal function. The thermal conductivity (Figure 2f) is only κ = 0.44 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹, far below that of graphitic carbons, because the disorder and the many domain–matrix interfaces scatter phonons; a low κ keeps the solar heat at the surface. The BET surface area (Figure S3) is only 40.4 m² g⁻¹ with ~5 nm pores, so the performance below is not a surface-area effect. Putting the data together (Figure 2g), the graphitic microdomains give fast photon-to-phonon (non-radiative) heat conversion, the defect-rich matrix traps carriers and broadens absorption, and the interfaces keep κ low. The three act together.

**Figure 5. Practical outdoor solar desalination with the TC/MF evaporator.**
**(a)** Schematic of the salt-rejection test: NaCl crystals are placed on the illuminated TC/MF surface, which sits on a foam float in 3.5 wt % NaCl solution, and the surface is followed under sunlight. **(b)** Photographs of the surface after 0, 50, 100 and 135 min. For practical use the surface must stay free of salt. NaCl crystals seeded on the TC/MF surface (Figure 5a) dissolve completely within 135 min under sunlight (Figure 5b): the capillary water carries salt back into the bulk faster than evaporation concentrates it, so the surface self-cleans. Outdoors from 10:30 to 16:30 (Figure 5c), with irradiance peaking near 1.0 kW m⁻² at noon, the collected-water mass rises steadily, confirming stable field operation. In the collected water (Figure 5d), Na⁺, Mg²⁺, K⁺ and Ca²⁺ each drop by two to three orders of magnitude and fall below the WHO limit, so the product is drinkable. The by-product carbon therefore supports real desalination, not just lab evaporation.

## 3. Conclusions

The solid carbon left over from plasma methane pyrolysis works, with no further treatment, as an efficient photothermal material for solar vapor generation. Fast cooling after the discharge freezes it into a turbostratic graphitic–defect hybrid: small graphitic domains in a continuous defect-rich matrix. SEM, HRTEM/FFT, HAADF-STEM/EDS, XPS, XRD, Raman and FTIR all confirm this structure, and the hot-disk measurement shows a low thermal conductivity of 0.44 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹. The structure explains the function: the graphitic microdomains convert photons to heat by fast non-radiative relaxation, the defect-rich matrix traps carriers and broadens the absorption so the carbon takes up almost all of the solar spectrum, and the many interfaces keep the heat at the surface. Dip-coated onto melamine foam with a PVA/chitosan binder, the carbon reaches an evaporation rate of 2.0 kg m⁻² h⁻¹ under one sun — the highest in a set of representative carbon evaporators — rejects salt during continuous operation, and produces WHO-compliant water outdoors. The result is a clear link from the intrinsic hybrid structure to its properties and its performance, and a simple, scalable, binder-only way to add value to the large carbon by-product of turquoise-hydrogen production. Future work will measure long-term durability and cyclic stability, quantify the solar-to-vapor efficiency, deepen the mechanism (for example a Tauc/Urbach analysis of the UV–Vis–NIR data or electron paramagnetic resonance), and test large-area scale-up.

## 6. Supporting Information — figure and table captions

**Figure S1. High-resolution XPS spectra of the methane-derived carbon.**
(a) C 1s spectrum, deconvoluted into a dominant sp² C=C/C–C component at 284.8 eV and minor oxygen-containing carbon species (C–O, C=O/O–C=O) with a weak π–π* shake-up satellite; the oxygen-bonded components are a minor fraction of the total area. The spectra confirm that the sp² carbon framework is intact and that oxygen (3.0 at % in the survey scan, Figure 2a) is confined to the surface.

**Figure S2. Full-range X-ray diffraction pattern of the methane-derived carbon.**
Wide-angle scan (2θ ≈ 15–50°) showing the broad (002) reflection near 25–26° (d₀₀₂ ≈ 0.34 nm) and the broad, low-intensity (100) band near 43°, with no sharp graphite reflections and no resolved higher-order lines. The large widths indicate spatially confined graphitic domains within disordered carbon (turbostratic stacking); Figure 2b,c are magnified views of the (002) and (100)/(101) regions of this pattern.

**Figure S3. Textural properties of the methane-derived carbon.**
(a) N₂ adsorption–desorption isotherms at 77 K, of type IV with a hysteresis loop near saturation, giving a BET specific surface area of ≈ 40.4 m² g⁻¹. (b) Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) pore-size distribution, showing a broad mesopore population from ~2 to ~20 nm (centred near 5 nm) arising from inter-particle voids in the aggregates. The modest surface area shows that the photothermal performance is governed by the carbon's electronic and phonon structure, not by porosity.

**Figure S4. Raman analysis of interfacial water in contact with the carbon.**
Deconvoluted O–H stretching Raman envelope (2500–4000 cm⁻¹) of pure water and of water in contact with the methane-derived turbostratic carbon (Water/TC), separated into free-water (FW), intermediate-water (IW) and other hydrogen-bonded components. Contact with the carbon slightly raises the FW fraction and lowers the IW fraction while the overall envelope is nearly unchanged, showing that the carbon perturbs only the local hydrogen-bond network at the solid–liquid interface. This weak activation of interfacial water lowers the effective enthalpy of vaporization and helps sustain the high evaporation rate.

**Table S1. Operating and electrical parameters of the rotating gliding-arc plasma reactor during carbon synthesis.**
RMS voltage 308 V; RMS current 9.9 A; mean discharge power 1.8 kW; run time 60 min; total gas flow 20 SLPM (CH₄:H₂ = 3:1 by volume); reactor pressure 1 atm.
