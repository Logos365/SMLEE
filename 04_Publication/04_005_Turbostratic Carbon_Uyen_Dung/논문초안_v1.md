## Abstract

Broadband solar absorption combined with rapid light-to-heat conversion is the central material requirement for efficient solar vapor generation (SVG), yet it is usually met only through elaborate post-synthetic engineering. Here we show that the solid carbon produced as a by-product of plasma-assisted methane pyrolysis intrinsically satisfies this requirement through a turbostratic graphitic–defect hybrid architecture. Electron microscopy, X-ray diffraction, Raman spectroscopy, and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy consistently reveal short-range graphitic microdomains (interlayer spacing ≈ 0.34 nm, I~D~/I~G~ ≈ 0.48) embedded in a continuous defect-rich carbon matrix. The graphitic microdomains drive efficient photon-to-phonon conversion through non-radiative relaxation, whereas the defect-rich matrix introduces localized electronic states that extend absorption across the ultraviolet–visible–near-infrared range; the numerous internal interfaces additionally suppress heat conduction (κ ≈ 0.44 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹), favouring interfacial heat localization. Without any activation, doping, or nanostructuring, the carbon shows > 95 % wavelength-independent absorption, and a binder-coated melamine-foam evaporator reaches an interfacial evaporation rate of 2.0 kg m⁻² h⁻¹ under one-sun illumination with stable outdoor desalination to below World Health Organization limits. This work establishes a direct structure–property–function relationship that converts a hydrogen-economy by-product into a scalable solar-driven water-purification material.

**Keywords:** methane pyrolysis; turbostratic carbon; graphitic–defect hybrid; photothermal conversion; solar vapor generation; desalination

## 1. Introduction

Solar-driven interfacial evaporation has emerged as a low-cost route to freshwater because it confines the absorbed solar energy to a thin layer at the air–water interface rather than heating the entire water body, which markedly raises the solar-to-vapor conversion efficiency [1–3]. In such systems the evaporation rate is ultimately governed by the intrinsic photothermal properties of the light absorber, so absorber design is the primary lever for performance [2,4,5].

An ideal photothermal absorber must simultaneously provide three properties: broadband absorption spanning the ultraviolet–visible–near-infrared (UV–Vis–NIR) solar spectrum, efficient conversion of absorbed photons into heat through non-radiative relaxation, and suppressed heat conduction into the bulk so that the generated heat stays localized at the evaporation front [4–6]. Carbon materials—including carbon nanotubes, graphene, graphene oxide, carbon black, carbon foams, carbon cloth, and carbon sponges—are attractive candidates because they combine intrinsically broadband absorption with chemical and thermal stability, low cost, and structural tunability [7–16].

The photothermal response of carbon, however, depends sensitively on its degree of structural ordering [6,16]. Highly graphitized carbons possess extended sp²-conjugated networks that favour fast non-radiative relaxation and efficient photothermal conversion, but they offer few in-gap electronic states and conduct heat well in-plane [17]. Fully amorphous carbons contain abundant disorder that broadens optical transitions, yet their fragmented sp² domains lower the conversion efficiency [18]. Neither extreme is optimal; a **graphitic–defect hybrid** that unites ordered microdomains with a disordered matrix is expected to combine efficient light harvesting with efficient heat generation [19].

To date, such hybrids have been assembled almost exclusively by post-synthetic engineering—chemical activation, heteroatom doping, template-assisted synthesis, partial reduction, or hierarchical nanostructuring—used to construct the desired balance of order and disorder [20–23]. These steps add cost and complexity and hinder scale-up. A carbon that is **intrinsically** a graphitic–defect hybrid, requiring none of this processing, would be far more practical but has remained largely unexplored.

Methane pyrolysis offers exactly such a material. Producing hydrogen without direct CO₂ emission ("turquoise" hydrogen), it co-generates roughly three kilograms of solid carbon per kilogram of H₂, and its economic viability depends on valorizing that carbon [24,26,46]. During high-temperature decomposition, carbon nucleates and grows directly from gaseous species; the subsequent rapid quenching kinetically arrests graphitization, so the carbon condenses into a **turbostratic** framework of short-range graphitic microdomains dispersed in a defect-rich matrix—an intrinsic graphitic–defect hybrid formed in a single continuous step [27,47]. The relationship between this by-product microstructure and its photothermal behaviour for SVG has not been established. Here, using carbon collected from a rotating gliding-arc (RGA) plasma methane-pyrolysis reactor, we correlate microstructure with optical absorption, photothermal conversion, and evaporation performance, thereby defining a structure–property–function relationship, and we demonstrate a melamine-foam evaporator that delivers 2.0 kg m⁻² h⁻¹ under one sun and World Health Organization (WHO)-compliant desalination outdoors—without any post-synthetic modification.

## 2. Results and Discussion

### 2.1. Intrinsic formation of a graphitic–defect hybrid turbostratic carbon

```

│  (a) plasma pyrolysis + rapid quenching schematic; collected  │
│      carbon photo   (b) graphitic–defect hybrid architecture  │
│  (c) dip-coating of TC onto melamine foam → TC/MF evaporator  │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

Methane-derived turbostratic carbon (hereafter **TC**) was collected downstream of a custom RGA plasma reactor operated at atmospheric pressure (discharge power 1.8 kW; CH₄:H₂ = 3:1; total flow 20 SLPM; Table S1) [25]. Within the discharge, methane dissociates into hydrogen and solid carbon (CH₄ → C + 2 H₂). Unlike carbonization of a solid precursor, carbon here nucleates and grows directly from gaseous carbon species [26]. Once the swirling flow carries these species beyond the confined plasma zone, energy input ceases abruptly and the carbon undergoes rapid natural cooling; graphitization is kinetically suppressed before long-range order can develop, and structural disorder is preserved [27]. The competition between graphitic crystallization and defect retention during quenching intrinsically produces a turbostratic framework in which graphitic microdomains are embedded in a defect-rich carbon matrix (Figure 1a,b). This architecture forms in one step, with no chemical activation, heteroatom doping, template, or other post-synthetic engineering.

To evaluate the intrinsic photothermal behaviour of this carbon, TC was integrated into an evaporator by a single dip-coating step: a hydrophilic ink of TC with poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) and chitosan binders was coated onto commercial melamine foam (MF) at 2.5 mg cm⁻² and dried, giving the TC/MF evaporator (Figure 1c). The open-cell MF continuously wicks water to the illuminated surface while limiting heat dissipation into the bulk, so that the measured performance reflects the carbon itself rather than an engineered support [28].

### 2.2. Direct visualization of the graphitic–defect hybrid architecture

```
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  [ FIGURE 2 ]  Direct structural visualization                │
│  (a) SEM (low/high mag)  (b) HRTEM lattice fringes + FFT      │
│  (c) HAADF-STEM + EDS maps (C, O)  (d) XPS survey spectrum    │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

Scanning electron microscopy (Figure 2a) shows that TC consists of interconnected spherical nanoparticles forming a three-dimensional porous network with hierarchical surface roughness—a morphology that promotes multiple light scattering while providing continuous channels for water transport and vapour escape [29]. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (Figure 2b) reveals that the carbon is neither fully graphitized nor fully amorphous: well-defined lattice fringes with an interlayer spacing of ≈ 0.34 nm, matching the graphitic (002) plane, are locally dispersed within a surrounding disordered matrix [30]. The corresponding fast Fourier transform shows diffuse rings rather than discrete spots, indicating short-range graphitic ordering with limited long-range crystallinity—the signature of turbostratic carbon [31].

High-angle annular dark-field STEM with energy-dispersive X-ray mapping (Figure 2c) shows carbon distributed homogeneously through the particles, with only sparse, uniformly dispersed surface oxygen and no impurity-rich or phase-segregated regions. This is consistent with the XPS survey spectrum (Figure 2d), which gives 97 at % C and 3 at % O; the C 1s peak is dominated by sp² C=C/C–C bonding at 284.8 eV with only weak oxygen-containing groups, and the O 1s signal (Figure S1) together with weak C=O/C–O bands in the FTIR spectrum (Figure S2) indicates mild surface oxidation from ambient exposure rather than a modified carbon framework. The structural heterogeneity of TC therefore originates from variations in **carbon ordering**, not in elemental composition: TC is an intrinsic graphitic–defect hybrid.

### 2.3. Turbostratic ordering and phonon-suppressed thermal transport

```
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  [ FIGURE 3 ]  Ordering & heat transport                      │
│  (a) XRD  (b) Raman (I_D/I_G = 0.48)  (c) N₂ isotherm + PSD   │
│  (d) transient thermal conductivity  (e) structure→property   │
│      summary schematic                                         │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

X-ray diffraction (Figure 3a) shows a broad (002) reflection at 25.8°, confirming graphitic stacking with limited coherence length, and a broad (100)/(101) feature near 43–45° characteristic of a turbostratic framework [27]. Consistently, the Raman spectrum (Figure 3b) exhibits the defect-activated D band (≈ 1350 cm⁻¹) and the graphitic G band (≈ 1580 cm⁻¹) with an intensity ratio I~D~/I~G~ = 0.48, indicating an **intermediate** degree of ordering: sp² domains are sufficiently developed for efficient non-radiative relaxation, yet abundant edge and in-plane defects remain [32,33]. TC thus occupies a turbostratic regime between crystalline graphite and amorphous carbon.

Nitrogen sorption (Figure 3c) gives a type-IV isotherm with a modest specific surface area of 40.4 m² g⁻¹ and ≈ 5 nm mesopores, so the photothermal performance below cannot be attributed to a large surface area. The transient thermal conductivity of TC (Figure 3d) is only κ = 0.44 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹, far below that of crystalline graphitic carbons; the abundant disorder and the many interfaces between graphitic microdomains and the surrounding matrix scatter phonons and impede heat propagation [34]. Taken together (Figure 3e), the graphitic microdomains provide fast photon-to-phonon conversion, the defect-rich matrix introduces localized electronic states that lower the optical absorption threshold and broaden the absorption band [48], and the internal interfaces give an intrinsically low κ that favours interfacial heat localization. This structural picture underlies the optical and photothermal behaviour characterized next.

XRD of the coated foam (Figure 4a) retains both the TC (002)/(100) reflections and the broad amorphous feature of MF, with no new crystalline phases, confirming that dip-coating preserves the intrinsic structure of both components. SEM with EDS mapping (Figure 4b) shows a conformal carbon layer covering the MF struts without blocking the open cells; carbon is uniformly distributed, while nitrogen originates from the melamine skeleton. Wettability tests (Figure 4c) show that pristine TC powder is hydrophobic and floats, whereas TC/MF—despite being fully covered by carbon—retains the hydrophilicity of MF, with water droplets infiltrating within ≈ 1 s. The thin carbon coating therefore does not impede capillary water supply. Raman spectra of water in contact with TC (Figure S4) show only a slight increase in the free-water component and a corresponding decrease in intermediate-water, with the overall O–H stretching envelope essentially unchanged, indicating that TC perturbs only the local hydrogen-bonding network at the solid–liquid interface and mildly lowers the effective vaporization enthalpy without restructuring bulk water [50].

### 2.5. Broadband solar absorption, photothermal conversion, and interfacial vapor generation

UV–Vis–NIR spectroscopy (Figure 4d) shows that MF is essentially transparent, whereas TC absorbs > 95 % of incident light almost independently of wavelength from 250 to 2500 nm; TC/MF fully retains this response, so the conformal coating turns a transparent foam into a near-black-body absorber. This near-unity broadband absorption is consistent with the graphitic–defect hybrid architecture of Section 2.3, in which defect-derived localized states extend the absorption edge while the sp² network sustains strong interband absorption.

Under one-sun illumination in the dry state (Figure 4e), the TC/MF surface heats from 25 °C to ≈ 75 °C within one minute and then plateaus, while pristine MF reaches only ≈ 26 °C. In the wet state, TC/MF stabilizes at ≈ 35 °C—lower than dry because absorbed energy is consumed by evaporation—yet still well above MF (≈ 25 °C); the infrared images confirm that heat remains localized at the illuminated surface. The cumulative water mass loss (Figure 4f, summarized in Figure 5a) reaches 2.00 kg m⁻² for TC/MF over one hour, versus 0.87 for MF and 0.11 for bulk water, with a near-linear, stable profile. ### 2.6. Outdoor solar desalination and salt rejection

Salt rejection was assessed by seeding NaCl crystals on the evaporator surface: under continuous solar irradiation the crystals dissolved completely within 135 min (Figure 5b). Spontaneous redissolution indicates that inward diffusion of salt, driven by continuous capillary replenishment from the hydrophilic MF, outpaces the outward advective salt flux caused by evaporation, so salt does not accumulate and the evaporation channels stay open [28]. Outdoor testing under natural sunlight from 10:30 to 16:30 (Figure 5c), with irradiance peaking near 1.0 kW m⁻² at noon, gave a continuously rising cumulative freshwater mass despite fluctuating illumination, confirming stable field operation. Analysis of the collected condensate (Figure 5d) shows the salinity dropping below the WHO drinking-water guideline, with Na⁺, Mg²⁺, K⁺, and Ca²⁺ concentrations each reduced by two to three orders of magnitude [49]. ## 3. Conclusions

We have shown that the solid carbon obtained as a by-product of plasma methane pyrolysis is, without any further treatment, an intrinsically efficient photothermal material for solar vapor generation. Rapid quenching after high-temperature decomposition freezes the carbon into a turbostratic graphitic–defect hybrid, in which short-range graphitic microdomains are embedded in a continuous defect-rich matrix. Electron microscopy, XRD, Raman, and XPS consistently confirm this architecture, and it directly explains the measured properties: the graphitic microdomains provide efficient photon-to-phonon conversion, the defect-rich matrix introduces localized electronic states that give > 95 % wavelength-independent absorption, and the many internal interfaces yield a low thermal conductivity (≈ 0.44 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹) that keeps heat at the evaporation front. Coated onto hydrophilic melamine foam by a single dip-coating step, the carbon delivers an interfacial evaporation rate of 2.0 kg m⁻² h⁻¹ under one sun, strong salt rejection, and WHO-compliant freshwater production outdoors. The central result is a clear structure–property–function relationship: it is the **synergy** of graphitic ordering and structural disorder—not either feature alone—that produces the performance. Beyond mechanistic insight for designing carbon photothermal materials, this provides a scalable, binder-only route to valorize the large carbon co-product of turquoise-hydrogen production into a high-value material for solar-driven water purification. Future work will resolve the atomic-scale origin of the graphitic–defect synergy by first-principles calculation, establish long-term durability and scale-up, and extend the approach to other solar energy-conversion applications.
